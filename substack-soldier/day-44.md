*A work of fiction inspired by the accounts of Ukrainian soldiers.*

# Day 44 — Dispatch: Sleep Debt

I put salt in my tea this morning and didn't notice until Vira stared at me.

"What?" I said.

"You're drinking it."

"It's tea."

"It's salt. You put salt in it. I watched you." She said this not unkindly. She took the mug from my hand, sniffed it, and set it down. "Lys. When did you last sleep?"

I tried to count. Yesterday? The day before? There was a watch, then a long walk, then another watch, then Yaroslav had a nightmare and I sat up with him, then it was morning. Something like four hours in two days. Maybe.

"I'm fine," I said, and then forgot what I'd been about to do.

There's a thing nobody tells you about exhaustion. It doesn't feel like tiredness. Tiredness is honest, it pulls your eyelids down and begs you to lie flat. This is different. This is a flat gray fog behind the eyes, a slow thickening of the world, where simple words slide away and decisions grow fur. You laugh at things that aren't funny. You nearly cry at the sound of a spoon. You walk into a room and forget why, and then you stand there, in a daze, for a minute, without minding.

I've seen it in everyone. Bohdan, yesterday, tried to charge a phone with a pencil. Zenko lost his glasses and found them on his head and said, with immense dignity, "That's where I keep them." Motor, in all the weeks I've known him, has never made a mistake with a tool. Yesterday he put a wrench in the bread bin. He stared at it for a long time. We all stared at it. Nobody said anything.

It's dangerous, too. That's the truth under the comedy. A tired man misjudges a distance. A tired man misses a sound. A tired man takes the wrong path in the dark. Halyna, who sees this clearly, has started enforcing sleep like a military operation. "You," she says, pointing. "Bed. You, thirty minutes. You, after tea. Do not argue with the nurse."

She sent me to my bunk at ten. I protested. She stood over me with her arms folded. "Lys, in your current state, you're a threat to yourself and others. Go to sleep."

I lay down. The Colonel climbed up beside me, hot and heavy, and put his chin on my chest. Someone laid a blanket over me, and I never found out who.

I slept for eleven hours. When I woke, someone had put a mug of tea beside my pillow. I took a sip. It was perfect. Sweet, hot, correct.

I nearly cried again. Slept-in people are very emotional.

# Day 44 — Notes: What Exhaustion Does to Judgment

Plain facts, from experience and from Halyna.

**Signs you're running on empty:**
1. You reread the same line three times.
2. You forget a name you've known for weeks.
3. You get irritable over small things.
4. You laugh too loudly.
5. You make a silly mistake and don't catch it.
6. You stop being afraid, which is the dangerous one.

**What to do:**
- **Sleep, when you can, in pieces.** Twenty minutes counts.
- **Eat.** A sugar spike is a bad bandage, but a bandage.
- **Water.** More than you think.
- **Tell someone.** "I'm bad right now." It's a gift to the group.
- **Don't make big decisions.** Not at 3 a.m. Not ever, at 3 a.m.

**What I now believe:** Sleep isn't a luxury. It's equipment, as essential as a rifle.

If you're reading this at 2 a.m., please go to bed.
