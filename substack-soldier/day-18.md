# Day 18 — Dispatch: Rain Again

It rained for three days, and by the second, we'd all stopped talking about it, the way you stop talking about a relative who's overstayed.

This kind of rain isn't dramatic. It doesn't storm. It just falls, thin and steady and gray, as if the sky has all the time in the world. It gets into the earth and the earth gives it to the dugout, a slow seep along the wall, a dripping from the ceiling that Motor battles with a tarp and a series of increasingly sad buckets. There's a rhythm to it: plink, plink, plonk. Three different pots, three different notes. Bohdan says if we tuned them we could start a band.

"The Wet Orchestra," said Zenko. "Opus one in a flat."

By the second night the floor of the dugout was a damp paste and everything that touched it was cold. Socks wouldn't dry. Letters curled. The tea tasted of wet wool, though this might have been me. There's a kind of tiredness that has nothing to do with sleep. It's what comes from being slightly damp, slightly cold, and slightly worried, for seventy-two hours. It makes you irritable. It makes you short.

I snapped at Bohdan today. Over nothing. He'd put his boots in the wrong place, which was where my boots go, and I said something sharp, and he went quiet, and the dugout went quiet, and I felt like a man who had kicked a dog.

Fifteen minutes later I found him by the stove and said, "I'm sorry. I'm not mad at you. I'm mad at the roof."

"I know," said Bohdan. "I'm mad at the roof too. Can we be mad at it together?"

So we sat there, two men glaring at the ceiling, and Vira, passing with a bucket, said, "If you two are going to bond over hating the roof, at least say it out loud." And so we did. We said terrible things about the roof. Zenko joined. Motor joined, in a deadpan voice, with a slow, devastating review of the roof's engineering. By the end we were laughing, and the plinks and plonks seemed almost friendly.

At dusk the rain thinned. A strip of pale orange cut under the clouds, low in the west, and for ten minutes every puddle on the field burned like a coin. I stood on the steps and looked at it. The Colonel shook himself, spraying everyone.

Weather is the only thing here that treats everyone fairly. It rains on all of us. That's a terrible comfort, but a comfort.

# Day 18 — Notes: Waterproofing Your Mind

You can waterproof your boots. Here's how I'm waterproofing the rest.

1. **Name the misery.** "I am cold and wet and tired." Say it. It shrinks it by a third.
2. **Pick one dry thing.** One dry sock, one dry page, one warm mug. Hold it.
3. **Don't make big decisions in the rain.** Everything looks worse. Wait for the sun.
4. **Blame the weather, not the people.** The roof is the enemy. Bohdan is not.
5. **Move.** Even ten steps. Stagnant water is grim. Moving water is just a river.
6. **Rhythm helps.** Plink, plonk. Hum along. Something predictable.
7. **Remember it ends.** It always does. I've now been told that three hundred times and it's even been true twice.

What's your best trick for a gray, soaking stretch of life?
