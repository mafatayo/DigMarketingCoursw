# Day 26 — The Goal: Banter and Cohesion

*Notes on why trivial arguments help a unit.*

**Bottom line:** A fight over an old football goal gave us three hours of peacetime.

**OPSEC & Context:** *Fiction.* The narrator is a mobilized sound engineer serving in a small drone-support section. Anything that could identify a place, a time or a person's unit has been changed.

## The Reality on the Ground

The argument started at lunch and ended, I think, around midnight, and in between I learned that men who've been shot at together will fight to the death over a disallowed goal from years ago.

It began innocently. Bohdan said something about the national team. Motor, who'd been eating, put down his spoon, which is how you know he's about to say something that matters.

"That game against England," he said. "The goal that wasn't given."

"It crossed the line," said Vira. "Everyone saw it cross the line."

"It did not cross the line."

"Motor, the ball was *in.*"

"The ball was *on* it."

I won't pretend I understood. I'm not much of a football person. But inside five minutes the dugout had divided into factions. Bohdan and Vira, the *it was in* party. Motor, Zenko, Halyna, the *it never was* party. I, the sound engineer, was asked to settle the matter "by analyzing the audio of the stadium."

"I don't have the audio."

"Then use your instincts."

"My instincts say I'm hungry."

Nobody cared. Zenko gave a lecture on the history of the offside rule. Halyna accused Vira of emotional bias. Vira accused Halyna of being a Lviv person. The Colonel left in disgust.

Then I noticed something. Everyone was shouting. Everyone was red in the face and laughing and cutting each other off. It was loud and human and completely beside the point, and for three hours nobody in the dugout was a soldier. They were a bunch of people who cared about a ball.

Because the front has no room for pointless arguments. Every conversation is about something: supplies, weather, plans, who's on watch. Fighting about nothing felt almost luxurious. It felt like peacetime.

At one point, in the middle of it, I saw Motor glance up at the beam, at the sound of something far off, and then back at the table, and carry on arguing. He'd measured it, filed it, and returned to the dispute. It took half a second. I understood that this was why he was loud: the goal didn't matter, he was choosing which noise to listen to.

Around eleven he said, "Fine. It was in."

A gasp. "You're conceding?"

"All these years. I'm tired. I wanted to see if I could still be this angry about something stupid."

"And?"

"I can." He smiled, slow and rare. "Best I've felt all week."

I lay on the bunk later grinning at the ceiling, and thinking: that's the thing. That's all of it. The right to argue about a football match at midnight in your own kitchen. And I also thought, because I couldn't help it, about how many kitchens in this country have a chair nobody argues from anymore.

## Tactical Observations

- Cohesion is built in ordinary arguments. Sections with trivial banter tend to handle serious stress better.
- Sections with ordinary banter tend to handle serious stress better.
- A shared dispute about an old match is a small act of peacetime.

## The Human Element

# Day 26 — Notes: Watching the Match

**Bottom line:** Even football is watched in shelters, out of stubbornness.

There's a basement in a town on this line where forty people gather on match nights, around a phone propped on a stack of tires, to watch a game they've watched for thirty years. Whoever's on duty holds the aerial.

The shell lands, they pause, they go back. It's the most stubborn thing I've seen. It's not about football.
