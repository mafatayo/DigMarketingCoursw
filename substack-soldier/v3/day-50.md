## The Reality on the Ground

Vira found the green notebook. She didn't open it for two days. Then, on the third evening, with the lamp low and the dugout quiet, she took it from the shelf and sat down at the crate and said, to nobody, "Someone should read it."

Nobody moved.

"He'd want it done properly. He wrote it for the donors. So the donors should be told." She looked at the cover for a while. "I'll do it. I can read a list."

She opened to the first page and read, in her flat radio voice, and I'll give you the shape. A date. A number. A word. *Lost. Jammed. Shot. Lost. Crashed in field, recovered. Lost.* Fifty-three, then the new ones. Fifty-four. Fifty-five. Fifty-six. She read each as he'd have said it, without comment.

Under most of them, a small note. *Wind.* *Cold, battery.* *Their jammer.* *My fault — too long up.* A few had a second word. *Sorry.* It was written the same way every time, small and neat, like a signature on a card.

She got to the end and stopped. There was one more line, on the last page, in the same neat hand. Not a loss. A date, and next to it, a drawing: a very small fox, whiskers and all. She turned the page. It was blank.

"Fifty-seven," said Zenko. "That's the number. Fifty-six machines, and then..." He didn't finish.

It was Motor who spoke. "Lieutenant."

Maksym, who'd been sitting at the edge of the table in a silence I'd never seen on him, lifted his head.

"We need a way to tell the donors."

"I'll write to them," said Maksym. "I'll write a letter. With the numbers. I'll tell them what each one did."

"No," said Vira. "Not a letter. Read them the notebook. All of it. Every line, every note. They gave a month of their wages for each frame. They should hear it the way he said it."

"Vira—"

"They should hear *sorry,*" she said. Her voice went, finally, and she put the book down very carefully. "Fifty-six times. They should hear that he apologized to every single one."

I didn't breathe.

She stood, went to the stove, and, standing with her back to us, cried for the first time since I'd known her. Quietly, with a thin shaking breath and her hands flat on the hot metal. Halyna was behind her in two steps. Nobody else moved.

I went and sat with the Colonel in the corner, and he leaned into me, and I looked at the shelf where the mug with the cartoon cat stood beside a razor and a candle stub and a small wooden swallow.
