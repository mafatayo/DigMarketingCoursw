# Day 33 — Cold: Layers, Feet and Fuel

*Notes on how winter changes the daily demands of a section.*

**Bottom line:** The first real cold arrived; Halyna counts our toes, and the rules are layers and dry feet.

**OPSEC & Context:** *Fiction.* The narrator is a mobilized sound engineer serving in a small drone-support section. Equipment counts, stock levels and supply routes are withheld.

## The Reality on the Ground

It arrived overnight and didn't ask.

Yesterday was cool. Today I woke at four with my breath standing in front of my face and the blanket stiff along the edge where my exhale had frozen into little white feathers. The water in the bucket had a skin. The Colonel was curled in a tight ring on my chest, which I'd call affection if it wasn't also survival.

People say "it gets cold" as if it were a feeling, like sadness. It's a force. It enters your boots, your wrists, the gap under your collar. It makes your fingers stupid. By noon I couldn't undo a button. By afternoon I'd stopped being annoyed and started being grateful for each small warmth like a monk.

On the first real morning of it, Halyna stood at the end of the table like a general and gave a speech.

"Listen. Cold kills slowly. It's polite about it. You'll feel sleepy, comfortable, a bit silly. That's the danger. If your hands stop hurting, tell me. If you stop shivering, tell me. If someone starts talking nonsense, tell me. Understood?"

We nodded.

"And feet. Feet, feet, feet."

"We know about feet," said Bohdan.

"You know nothing about feet."

So we layered. I wore everything I had. Three shirts, a wool sweater, the jacket, two scarves, a hat Katya had knitted from a pattern she'd found online and a small amount of faith. I looked like a tall onion. Vira laughed until she coughed.

"Come on, onion," she said. "We're digging."

The ditch was brutal. The ground rang under the spade like iron. After ten minutes my palms were on fire and my breath was coming in clouds. But there was a strange joy in it, the warmth of work, the steam rising off us in a row like kettles. Zenko, red-cheeked, was reciting something in Latin. Motor shoveled beside me, humming into the cold.

That evening, when I got in, Halyna had my feet in her hands before I'd sat down. She held them until they stopped hurting. She rubbed them as she'd rub a child's. Then she said, "Good," and looked at the next man. She was counting toes. It's the first time she's ever done it that openly.

At night the stove ran hot and the dugout smelled of soup and wet wool. We peeled off the outer layers and held our hands to it, fingers returning in a sharp bright pain that felt like being forgiven. Winter had barely started.

I thought, not for the first time, about the other side. About men in holes like this one, a few hundred meters off, counting their own toes. I couldn't make myself hate them, in that minute. I don't know what that says about me. Probably only that I was warm.

# Day 33 — Notes: Cold as a Weapon

**Bottom line:** Freezing a city is a decision.

They hit the power stations in winter. That isn't a rumor in the dugout, it's arithmetic. Radiators go cold. Pipes burst. Old people wrap themselves in everything and sit in the kitchen with the oven door open, which is dangerous, and they do it anyway.

I would like someone to say clearly, in the rooms where these things are said, that to freeze a city is a decision.
