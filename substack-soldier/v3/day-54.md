# Day 54 — Taras Leaves: Personnel Turbulence

*Notes on transfers and cohesion.*

**Bottom line:** Taras was transferred out, nobody says goodbye here, and I've started counting the people who are gone.

**OPSEC & Context:** *Fiction.* The narrator is a mobilized sound engineer serving in a small drone-support section. Names of living people are altered; locations and unit details are removed.

## The Reality on the Ground

Taras, the guitar man, is being transferred.

He came to tell us at dusk, in his big coat, long hands in his pockets. His unit's being moved. A different sector, a different job. Orders came down that morning. He has until tomorrow.

He stood at the head of the steps for a while as if reluctant to come down, then did, ducking under the blanket. "I wanted to say goodbye properly. I don't know how."

Nobody did. There's no etiquette for the simple leaving of a friend. No ceremony. A truck, a bag, a nod. Next week he'll be somewhere else and so will we, and we'll write, maybe. Or we won't. I've now seen this three times in two months and I've understood that people don't say goodbye here because it might be wrong. A man who says goodbye and comes back has to explain. A man who says "see you" has nothing to retract.

"Sit," said Halyna. "Tea."

He sat. Zenko gave a short speech. "You have been a gentleman and a musician. The first is rare, the second rarer. May your strings always be more than three."

Taras smiled. "I'm leaving the guitar."

"You can't."

"It likes it here."

"It's your guitar."

"It's *ours,*" he said. "Somebody has to play it."

He looked at us one by one, as if memorizing. I've watched men do it. A slow deliberate sweep, the eyes touching each face. I think it's a kind of insurance.

When it came to me, he held my eyes a moment. "Look after the dog."

"I will."

"He's your dog."

"He's the Colonel's."

"He's yours," said Taras.

He didn't hug anyone, he isn't the type. He shook hands, long and firm, in the old way. With Motor he did something I'd never seen. They pressed foreheads together briefly, two big tired men, like bulls. Then he left.

I went out to see him off. Nearly dark. He walked toward the track with his bag over one shoulder, a long thin shape against the gray, and didn't turn. I watched until I couldn't tell him from the fence posts.

Back in the dugout, someone had put the guitar on his chair. Nobody touched it. We sat in a long quiet. After a while Yaroslav picked it up and began, haltingly, the lullaby. Badly, with wrong notes. Nobody corrected him.

I've kept a count since Bohdan. I mean of the people who aren't here. Pasha. Bohdan. Taras, who is, in the proper sense, still alive. I notice that I count him anyway. It's a habit I've picked up and I don't like it.
