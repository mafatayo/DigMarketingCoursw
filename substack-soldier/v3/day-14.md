# Day 14 — Pasha: Distance and Survival

*Notes on how evacuation time shapes who lives.*

**Bottom line:** Pasha, whose bunk I sleep above, died of wounds, and Motor decided his razor stays on the shelf.

**OPSEC & Context:** *Fiction.* The narrator is a mobilized sound engineer serving in a small drone-support section. Medical details and casualty identities are limited to what a family would already know.

## The Reality on the Ground

The call came for Halyna at four in the afternoon, in the middle of a mug of tea.

She took it by the post where the signal is, and the rest of us tried to look occupied. I watched her through the door. She stood very straight, one hand over her free ear, saying "yes" and "I understand" and "when" in the same even voice. It took less than two minutes. When she hung up she kept standing there with the phone against her chest, looking at the field, for long enough that Motor put down the wrench he was holding and went up the steps without a word.

I couldn't hear them. I saw him take his cap off.

They came down together. Nobody asked. Halyna sat on the crate, the one nobody uses because it wobbles, and folded her hands in her lap, and said, to the table, not to any of us:

"Pasha died. At the stabilization point. Yesterday, about noon. He'd been doing better, they said, and then he wasn't."

That was all. Nobody said anything for a while. Zenko took his glasses off. Vira turned the radio down to nothing. The stove ticked.

It was strange, because I didn't know him. I'd slept above his empty bunk for twelve nights. I'd learned the exact angle of the razor on the shelf. I'd been told not to sit on his blanket and I hadn't. I never heard his voice. And I found I couldn't breathe properly, as after a fall, and I couldn't work out whose grief I was having.

"He was twenty-six," Halyna said. "He had a sister. He kept a cat in a village that isn't there anymore." She stopped. "I told him to keep his head down on the way out. He said, 'Yes, Doc.' He always said it like it was a joke."

Bohdan was crying quietly, wiping his face with his sleeve, and he stopped halfway through to say, "Sorry." Vira, not looking at him, said, "Don't." That was the entire exchange.

Motor stood up. He went to the shelf, picked up the razor, held it for a second, and put it back exactly where it was. Same angle.

"It stays," he said.

"Sergeant," said Zenko, carefully.

"It stays."

Nobody argued.

That night I lay on the top bunk and listened to the dugout breathing. The bunk beneath me was a different kind of empty. Before, it was *out for now.* Now it was a fact. Around two I heard someone crying on the other side of the room. I didn't look. In that place, you give it the dark.

Motor hummed his four bars until about three, and then stopped, and I heard him breathe out like a man setting something down.

## Tactical Observations

- Evacuation time shapes survival odds. A death at a stabilization point reflects distance as much as injury, which is why everyone is trained in first aid.

# Day 14 — Notes: His Sister

**Bottom line:** When you deliver bad news, slow down and say the name first.

Pasha had a sister. She's twenty-two. She was told on the phone, by a stranger, in about forty seconds.

If you ever have to be the one who delivers that kind of news: slow down. Say the name first. Let there be a pause. It's the last thing she'll remember about the call.
