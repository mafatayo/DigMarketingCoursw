# Day 48 — Bohdan: A Drone Operator's Last Morning

*Notes on what the loss of an operator costs a small section and how the section holds together.*

**Bottom line:** Bohdan was killed by an enemy drone, and I couldn't make myself walk the forty feet to him.

**OPSEC & Context:** *Fiction.* The narrator is a mobilized sound engineer serving in a small drone-support section. Medical details and casualty identities are limited to what a family would already know.

## The Reality on the Ground

I've written this three times and burned two of them in the stove. This is the one I can send.

It was a little after ten in the morning. Gray, cold, ordinary. Bohdan had been up since six. He'd flown two short patrols and brought the machine in and was about to go out again. At ten he came up the steps with the drone under his arm like a small animal and stopped at the top, as he always did, to let his eyes adjust.

I was by the sandbags with Zenko. I heard it before anyone.

I'll say it as flatly as I can. I heard the buzz first. Thin and close and wrong, lower than ours, coming from behind the tree line. I said "Bird." I said it quietly, almost conversationally, the way you'd say "rain." And then, because something in me had understood faster than the rest, I said it again, loud, and I said his name.

He turned his head.

I won't describe what happened next. I'm not being delicate. I mean I physically can't, I've tried three times. It was fast. That it was not the machine he'd been carrying. That he'd taken one step back toward the door.

Halyna was past me before I'd moved. I know she was running because I remember seeing her bag hit the ground behind her. I know she got to him in four seconds. I know she did everything. I stood forty feet away and heard her voice, the new voice, steady, steady, saying his name over and over, *Bohdan, look at me, Bohdan.* And then, at some point, she stopped saying it, and I'm not sure when.

It wasn't long. Under ten minutes. I'll tell you he wasn't alone, that she held his hand, that Motor knelt on the other side with his cap off. That the Colonel, who isn't supposed to be near, lay down against his boots and wouldn't move.

I didn't go to him. I keep going over that. I stood by the sandbags with the bag of water in my hand, thinking *I should go,* and my legs wouldn't. I'd carried a stretcher. I'd been in a van. I could not make myself walk forty feet. Zenko stood beside me with his hand on my shoulder, shaking, and neither of us moved.

Afterward we sat in the dugout, the six of us, and nobody spoke until the light changed in the doorway. The kettle was cold. Nobody lit the stove. Somebody had put a mug in front of me and I hadn't touched it, and the tea had grown a skin.

Zenko was the first to move. He got up, very slowly, took off his glasses, wiped them on his scarf, put them back. "Gentlemen," he said, and his voice broke. "Ladies. I would like to say something. And I find I can't. So let us sit."

We sat.

It was the most honest hour of my life. Nobody offered comfort. Nobody said it would be all right. We sat in a hole in the earth and let the day be what it was.

At some point Halyna took my hand. Then Vira took hers. Then Yaroslav took Vira's. A ring of cold clumsy hands round a crate, nobody speaking. I felt the small bones in each. The warmth, such as it was.

On the shelf the notebook with the green cover lay where he'd left it. Fifty-three, and a few more. I didn't touch it.

## Tactical Observations

- Drone operators and their positions are priority targets. This loss fits a pattern, not a surprise, which is why teams stay small and dispersed.
- A section that loses its operator loses its eyes first, and rebuilding that capability takes longer than replacing equipment.
- Evacuation and first aid in the first minutes matter more than anything after, and they were not enough here.

## The Human Element

Nobody offered comfort. The section sat together, then held hands round a crate.

# Day 48 — Notes: Twenty-Three

**Bottom line:** He was twenty-three; ask about his life, not his death.

He was twenty-three. His birthday was a month ago. We made a cake out of biscuits.

I don't want to be told what his death was for. I want to be told what his life was. Ask me, and I'll tell you for an hour.
