*A work of fiction inspired by the accounts of Ukrainian soldiers.*

# Day 8 — Dispatch: Mud

I need to talk about mud. Nobody warned me. Everyone warned me about the shooting, the cold, the fear. Not one person said, "Danylo, you will think about mud more than you will think about your own mother."

It's not just wet earth. Mud here has a personality. It's thick, orange-brown, and it holds on. It takes your boot and doesn't give it back unless you ask nicely. I lost one on day four and had to stand on one leg in a field while Bohdan pulled the other out like a cork. The sucking sound made Vira laugh so hard she had to sit down. In the mud. Which she then complained about for an hour.

Mud gets in everything. Your socks, obviously, your sleeves, your teeth somehow. It dries on your hands in gray cracks and when you wash, the water turns the color of tea. It gets into the dugout on every boot, and Motor has a rule: boots off at the third step. The rule is enforced by a strip of old carpet and Motor's disappointed look. I'd rather be shot at than receive the look.

But the thing about mud is that it changes how you move. You learn a new walk. Shorter steps, weight low, never fully trust your front foot. Soldiers walk like old men in the mud. I've seen a twenty-year-old walk like my grandfather on ice.

Today it rained from before dawn. Everything slowed. There's a trench line I walk each morning, about two hundred meters, and today it took twenty-five minutes. Halfway, I stopped. Both boots deep, rain coming down in a flat gray sheet, and I thought: this is the least heroic thing I've ever done. Standing still in the rain, up to my ankles.

Then Zenko came up behind me, slow, in his two scarves, and said, "Napoleon lost an army to mud, you know."

"Did he?"

"Partly. Mud and cold and bad logistics. It's less romantic than people prefer."

"Are you saying we're Napoleon?"

"I'm saying the mud is older than both of us, and it's winning." He patted my shoulder. "Keep walking, gentlemen."

We kept walking.

By the time I got back, my socks were wet through. Halyna took one look at my feet, said nothing, and handed me a dry pair from the shelf she keeps for exactly this purpose. She does it like a quiet miracle, every time. I'd like to say thank you here, in print, because I haven't done it out loud.

Thank you, Halyna.

# Day 8 — Notes: Boots, Socks, and the Gospel of Dry Feet

If I could pass on one thing from this week it would be this: feet are the army.

**The rules, as taught by a nurse:**

1. **Two pairs minimum.** One on, one drying. Never the same pair twice in a day if you can avoid it.
2. **Dry feet at night.** Always. Even if you have to sit awake in the dark with your boots off. Your skin is more important than your pride.
3. **Powder.** Between the toes. It looks silly. It works.
4. **Check each other.** You can't see your own heel. Someone else can. Halyna does rounds.
5. **Wool, never cotton.** I've said it. I'll say it again. Cotton is a lie.
6. **Cut your nails straight.** Ingrown toenails have ended careers.
7. **Rotate your insoles.** Take them out to dry by the stove, but not too close, or they curl.

**What happens if you ignore this:** Trench foot. It's real, it's old, and it's why people in 1915 lost toes. I'm told they didn't have powder.

I used to think the army marched on its stomach. I'm revising that. The army marches on its socks.

What's the most underrated thing in your daily life? Mine used to be a sink.
