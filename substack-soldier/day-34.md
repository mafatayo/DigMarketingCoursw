*A work of fiction inspired by the accounts of Ukrainian soldiers.*

# Day 34 — Dispatch: The Medic's Hands

I want to describe Halyna's hands, because I think they're the most important thing in the dugout and no one ever says so.

They're square, strong, a little rough from washing. A nurse's hands, short nails, a faint pale mark on the left thumb where a scalpel once slipped. They're always warm. She keeps them in her armpits when she's not using them, a habit from the ward, so that when she touches a patient the hands aren't cold. "It's courtesy," she says.

Yesterday she taught a lesson on a thing everyone dreads, and we all sat in a half-circle on crates while the Colonel slept in the corner. Today's course was how to stop bleeding.

She held up a tourniquet, a flat black strap with a plastic bar. "This will save a life. Yours. Mine. His," nodding at Bohdan. "I want you to be able to put it on in the dark, with your eyes closed, with your teeth. Understand?"

We practiced. It's harder than it sounds. The strap is stiff. Your fingers shake. She made us do it again and again, on our own arms, our own legs, each other's. "Higher. Tighter. More. It will hurt. It should hurt. If it doesn't hurt, it isn't working."

When it was my turn I fumbled, and she didn't say a thing. She put her warm hands over mine, steadied the strap, and guided it up the way you'd guide a child's hand to a light switch. "There. Pull. Turn the bar. Good. Now the time. Write the time. Always the time."

She wrote the time on my arm in marker. 14:07. It's still there, faded, a small gray number. I haven't washed it off.

At the end she looked at the six of us, all frowning and a little scared, and said something I've kept. "You won't use this. I hope you won't. But one day you'll be in a field with someone you love, and you'll be the only one with a pair of hands. I'm giving you the hands."

It was a strange speech. Nobody laughed.

Later, over tea, I asked her how she managed it. Twenty years of wards, and now this. She thought about it for a while, stirring her tea.

"You do the next thing," she said. "Always. You stop the bleeding. You check the breath. You talk to them. You never stop talking. They need your voice more than your hands, half the time." She smiled, crooked. "And you cry later. In the shower. When there's a shower."

I laughed, and then I didn't.

# Day 34 — Notes: What a Tourniquet Teaches

I'm not a medic. This isn't medical advice. Get proper training from people who know. Here's only what I've learned about learning.

1. **Practice beats knowledge.** I'd read about it. My hands didn't know it.
2. **Calm is a skill.** Halyna's voice slows when things speed up. You can learn that.
3. **Mark the time.** It's the first thing the next doctor will ask.
4. **Talk to the person.** Say their name. Say "I'm here." It matters.
5. **Do the next thing.** Not the whole thing. The next thing.
6. **You can be afraid and still do it.** Both at once. That's what the training is for.

If you'd like to do something useful anywhere in the world, take a first aid course. A real one, with a real instructor, on a real mannequin. I mean it. It's a quiet kind of courage that costs a weekend.

Who taught you something that you hope you never have to use?
