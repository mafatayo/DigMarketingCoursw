# Day 13 — Dispatch: The Dog Who Adopted Us

He showed up on a Wednesday, which is how I know it was fate, because nothing good ever happens on a Wednesday.

A scrawny, rust-colored dog with one ear up and one ear down, sitting at the top of the dugout steps like he'd been hired. No collar. A tail that wagged in short, cautious sweeps, as if he wasn't sure he'd earned the right to wag it. He looked at us, and we looked at him, and for a full minute nobody moved.

"Don't feed him," Motor said.

"He's starving," said Halyna.

"If we feed him, he stays."

"He's staying," said Halyna.

She went down and came back with a tin of meat and put it on the step. The dog ate it in four seconds, then looked up with an expression that said, plainly, *I'm still here and I still exist.* Motor sighed the sigh of a man who has lost a very small war.

By evening the dog had a name. We argued for an hour. Bohdan wanted "Rocket." Vira wanted "Chuck," for no reason she'd explain. Zenko proposed "Cincinnatus," after a Roman general who put down his sword and returned to his farm, which got a long silence. In the end it was Motor, quiet, looking at the dog's rumpled coat and dignified sad eyes, who said, "Colonel."

So he's the Colonel. He outranks us all.

The Colonel's duties, as he understands them:

1. Inspecting each person who enters the dugout. A thorough sniff, boots to knees.
2. Sleeping on whichever bunk is warmest, usually mine.
3. Patrolling the perimeter, which is about nine meters.
4. Alerting us to danger. Here's the strange part. He seems to know. Twice already he's gone rigid and low a few seconds before anything happened, ears flat, and Vira has said, "Down," without anyone telling her why. I don't know how. I won't pretend to.

Last night I was on watch, cold and stiff on a crate, thinking about too much. Something warm pushed against my leg. The Colonel had come up out of the dugout, quietly, and simply sat against my shin, leaning his whole weight on me, the way a tired child leans on a parent. I didn't pet him. I just let him sit. We watched the dark together for an hour. He sighed, once, like a man at the end of a long shift.

I've been trying to work out why that undid me. I think it's that nobody asked him to be there. Everyone else here came because of a call, a law, a duty. The Colonel came because he chose to. He looked at a dugout full of tired, dirty, frightened strangers and decided we were worth leaning on.

I'll try to deserve that.

# Day 13 — Notes: Animals at the Front

A short and unscientific survey.

**Dogs.** Common. They attach to a unit like barnacles. Most are strays, a few were left behind when people fled. Their senses are better than ours. Several soldiers I've met say their dogs have saved them. I believe it. Feed them. Check their paws. Get them vaccinated if you can.

**Cats.** Usually appear in cellars and command posts, where they sit with the quiet self-regard of senior officers. They tolerate you. They do not need you. You need them.

**Birds.** Barometers. When they go quiet, we pay attention.

**Chickens.** In villages, still. I've seen one walk through a checkpoint like a tax inspector.

**Horses, cows, goats.** Many abandoned. Many rescued by volunteers. There are people whose entire war is hauling hay.

**Why it matters:** An animal doesn't care what happened yesterday. A dog's only question is: *are you here, and are you kind?* That's a gift when everything else is a question about the future.

If you want to help, look up the groups that rescue animals from the war zones. They're small and tired and need food and trucks.
