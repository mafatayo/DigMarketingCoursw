# Day 28 — Dispatch: Good News from Home

The next day it was Zenko's turn, and I'd like to tell you how a man receives good news after a month of none.

He was sitting at the table reading, as always, with his two scarves, his small round glasses on his nose, a pencil behind his ear. The mail truck had come at noon. Vira handed him an envelope, thin, grayish, with a child's handwriting on the front. He looked at it for a moment, frowned, and set it aside.

"Aren't you going to open it?" said Bohdan.

"I'm saving it."

"For what?"

"For when I'm ready."

We left him alone. He went back to his book, but I noticed he turned the same page for twenty minutes. At some point his hand drifted to the envelope. Then away. Then back.

It was the evening before he opened it. He took it up the steps to the cold, where the light was almost gone. Through the open door I could see him on the sandbags, in profile, tearing the paper carefully along the top, the way a man handles a thing he intends to keep.

He read it standing. Then he sat. Then he put his hand over his mouth and his glasses fogged, and for a moment I thought something terrible had happened, and I started to rise.

Vira caught my sleeve. "Wait," she said.

He came down the steps with the letter held against his chest. His face was wet and he was grinning, a huge, lopsided, embarrassed grin I'd never seen on him. "My daughter," he said. "She's expecting."

Nobody moved. Then the dugout exploded. Bohdan shouted. Vira threw a sock in the air. Halyna hugged him so hard his glasses slid sideways. Motor, from the corner, said, "Zenko, you're going to be a grandfather."

"I'm *forty-one*," said Zenko.

"Grandfather," said Motor, with immense satisfaction.

"It's," said Zenko. "It's." He couldn't finish. He sat down. "She wrote it in a letter because she wanted me to read it with my own eyes. She said she didn't want to tell me on the phone, because the phone is for bad news now. She wanted me to have a good one in an envelope."

I thought about that for a long time afterward. The phone is for bad news now. It's true. Everyone here has learned to flinch at a buzz. And a woman who knew that sat down, with her pregnancy, and wrote a letter, with a pen, so her father could have one clean moment of joy.

We celebrated with tea and the last of Babusya's plum jam, a spoon each. Zenko gave a toast, longer than the one for Bohdan. "To the next generation," he said. "May they be bored. May they be thoroughly, magnificently bored."

# Day 28 — Notes: Joy Is Allowed

I've caught myself, more than once, feeling guilty for being happy. Here's what I think about that.

**The guilt says:** "How can you laugh when others are suffering?"

**The answer, as I've learned it:**
1. Joy isn't betrayal. It's proof there's something worth the fight.
2. Your joy might be the only light someone else has today.
3. You don't pour out grief by drinking it all. You pour it out by letting some warmth in.
4. The dead would not want you to stop laughing.

**What I do:**
- Let myself enjoy a good bowl of soup. Fully.
- Celebrate news, even small news, with whoever's near.
- Take a photo of a good moment. Not for anyone. For me.
- Say out loud: "That was good."

**A phrase Vira taught me:** "Take the sweet and swallow it whole."

When did you last let yourself feel pure joy, without a footnote?
