# Day 52 — Dispatch: Journalists Visit

They came on Thursday: two of them, a man and a woman, in clean vests marked PRESS, with a camera as big as a small dog and a young local driver who refused to leave the car.

The commander had authorized it. We'd been told the day before, with the sort of weary politeness reserved for bad weather. "They'll want to see the dugout. Be natural."

"Natural?" said Vira. "I'm on a radio and I smell of onions."

"That's natural."

I'll be honest: I was nervous. I'd watched a great many reports from my sofa, never from the other side. I'd formed a picture of journalists as predators, or saints, depending on the day. These two were neither. The woman, small and quick, with a notebook and steady dark eyes, said in careful Ukrainian, "Please don't change anything for us. We're guests." The man set up the camera in the corner and then, I noticed, mostly stood back.

They talked to everyone. They asked Vira about the radio. They asked Halyna about the tourniquet. They asked Zenko about history and listened to his whole answer, which took eleven minutes. They asked Bohdan if he was afraid, and he said, "Yes, every day, but I get up," and the camera, very gently, moved closer.

And then, at the end, they turned to me.

"What would you like people to know?"

I'd prepared an answer. Something grand. I opened my mouth, looked at the lens, and felt the whole thing evaporate. "That we're ordinary," I said. "That's all. Bohdan's twenty-three and apologizes to machines. Zenko teaches history to people who won't pass the exam. Vira's the funniest person I know. We're people. We're tired. We're doing something very hard and we'd like to be home."

The woman nodded. She wrote nothing. She said, quietly, "That's what I came for."

After they left, the dugout was strangely subdued, as if a bright light had been shone on a delicate room and then turned off. Motor swept the floor, which he never does. Halyna made tea. Nobody mentioned the camera.

I've thought about it since. We were being witnessed. A small crew from a far country would carry us home in a box of film, and strangers would see our faces, our shabby little home, our dog. And somewhere, someone would watch for ten minutes and think: *those are people.* And maybe that would matter.

Or maybe it would just be a segment before the weather. I'll never know. But I stood straighter for a day.

# Day 52 — Notes: How to Read War Reporting

From the other side of the lens.

1. **Look for the ordinary detail.** The mug, the dog, the sock. That's where truth sits.
2. **Be wary of the perfect quote.** Real people mumble.
3. **Note who's missing.** Who isn't being heard?
4. **Check the date.** Things change fast.
5. **Check the source.** A name, a place, a photo credit.
6. **Be gentle with yourself.** You don't have to watch everything.
7. **Don't confuse a single story with the whole.** Ours is one dugout of thousands.
8. **Remember there's a person on both sides of the camera.**
