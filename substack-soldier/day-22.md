*A work of fiction inspired by the accounts of Ukrainian soldiers.*

# Day 22 — Dispatch: The Boy Who Draws Tanks

At the edge of the village there's a boy of about nine who sits on a wall every afternoon with a sketchbook and watches us pass.

He doesn't wave. He's too cool for waving. But he looks, with the whole serious focus of a child who's decided something matters. Bohdan noticed him first. "He's drawing us," he whispered, as if we might scare him off.

Today we stopped, because the truck broke down. Motor was under it with a wrench and a stream of quiet profanity. The rest of us stood about, bored. I walked over to the wall and, trying to look casual, said, "What're you drawing?"

He turned the page toward me without a word.

It was a tank. A big, careful, detailed tank, with a long barrel and a hundred small rivets and a flag on the turret that was slightly too large. Under it, in neat letters: *OURS*. Beside it, a second drawing, rougher, scribbled over in black: *THEIRS*.

I looked at the boy. He looked at me, eyes steady, a bit of pencil dust on his cheek.

"Are you a soldier?"

"Yes."

"Do you have a tank?"

"No. I work with... sound. And drones. Little ones."

He considered this with great scorn. "Drones don't have a gun."

"No."

"Tanks are better."

"For drawing, absolutely," I said.

That seemed to satisfy him. He held out his pencil. "Draw me something."

I'm no artist. But I took the sketchbook and, on a blank page, drew, badly, a fox. A scruffy fox with a big tail and a tiny scarf. The boy watched. "Why a fox?"

"It's my name. At work."

"Foxes are sneaky."

"Yes."

"That's good," he decided. "You can sit here."

So I sat on the wall for twenty minutes and watched a nine-year-old draw tank after tank, each one a bit more elaborate. He knew a lot more than I'd like him to know. He could name types by their silhouettes. It made me quietly sick. At nine, I could name the planets.

When Motor called, the boy handed me back my pencil. "Come again. I'll do your portrait."

"With a mustache?"

"With a fox," he said.

I walked back to the truck and said nothing for a while. Bohdan, loading tools, asked, "What did he draw?"

"Tanks," I said. "Lots of tanks."

"Of course he did." He took a long breath. "Do you think he'll draw something else, someday?"

I said I thought so. I'm trying to believe it.

# Day 22 — Notes: Children and War

I don't have the right to speak for children who live this. I can only report what I've seen from the edge.

**What I've noticed:**
1. They know more than we think. They know the sounds, the words, the codes.
2. They play differently. Games of sirens, shelters, sentries.
3. They draw what surrounds them. Tanks, flags, houses in two halves.
4. They're also still children. They still laugh at dogs. They still want sweets. They still fight over a ball.
5. They're remarkably kind. Many have given us their own little treasures.

**What helps (according to teachers and nurses I've met):**
- Routine. School, even in a basement. Meals at the same time.
- Being allowed to be afraid, and being held.
- Art. Music. Anything where the feeling goes out of the body into a page.
- Adults who don't lie, but don't pour the whole truth on a small head.

**What I worry about:** That the drawings will never change. That a generation will draw tanks forever.

**What gives me hope:** The boy asked me for a fox.

If you want to help, there are groups doing art therapy for children in frontline towns. They're small, and they're doing something huge.
