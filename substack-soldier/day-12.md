*A work of fiction inspired by the accounts of Ukrainian soldiers.*

# Day 12 — Dispatch: Borshch in a Tin Can

The borshch came in a tin can the size of a bucket, strapped to the back seat of a car driven by a woman who looked about sixty and who refused to give her name.

I'd like to tell you what she looked like. Short, round, in a purple coat that did not belong anywhere near a front line, a headscarf with roses on it. She climbed out of her little hatchback, which was caked in mud to the windows, and started unloading as if she were arriving at a family picnic. Thermoses. Foil packets. Two loaves of bread wrapped in a towel. A jar of something pickled. And the can.

"Where is the sergeant?" she said.

Motor came up the steps wiping his hands. He took one look at her and his whole face changed. I've never seen it do that. He took his cap off.

"Mother," he said.

"Don't *mother* me," she said. "You look like a scarecrow. Eat."

She'd driven six hours, I found out later, from a town I'd never heard of, in a car with a cracked windshield, because her neighbor's son was in this brigade and the neighbor had said, "They never get proper food." So she cooked. A whole day of cooking. Then she drove.

We lined up with our mugs. I'm not exaggerating when I say that nobody spoke. There was just the clink of the ladle and the soft sound of people breathing in steam. I got my turn, and the first spoonful was beets and cabbage and a little sour cream melting in, and dill, so much dill. It tasted exactly like my grandmother's kitchen on a Sunday.

Beside me Bohdan had his eyes closed. His spoon was halfway to his mouth and he'd just stopped, the way a child stops when something is too good to hurry. Vira was looking at the wall, but her shoulders were shaking. Zenko ate slowly, formally, nodding to himself, and said very quietly, "Madam, this is the finest borshch in Ukraine."

"I know," the woman said.

When it was gone she packed her pots and wiped her hands and went around the group giving each of us a quick hard look, like checking we were all there. At me she stopped. "You. You're thin. Eat more." And she pinched my cheek. At thirty-four. In front of my section.

Then she got back in her little car and drove away, the whole way back, six hours, to do whatever else she had to do. I never learned her name. Motor stood at the top of the steps watching the dust until it was gone.

"That," he said to nobody, "is why we're going to win."

# Day 12 — Notes: What Food Means at 3 a.m.

I've been awake at 3 a.m. a lot lately. Watch duty, nerves, a bad back on a wooden bunk. Here's what I know about food in the dead hours.

**It's not about calories.** Nobody's hungry at 3 a.m. They're lonely. Food is just company that can be chewed.

**Warm beats good.** A hot, plain cup of anything beats a gourmet cold sandwich every time.

**Smell is memory.** The borshch hit me like a door opening. I was eight years old again, in a kitchen with a window full of steam. No amount of "resilience training" can touch that.

**Sharing is the whole point.** Nobody eats alone at 3 a.m. You hear the stove, you hold out a mug, someone's there. It's the closest thing to a conversation we have at that hour, and it needs no words.

**What we talk about:** Always food. Always what we'll cook when we're home. Bohdan wants his mother's dumplings. Vira, a particular cake from a particular bakery in Poltava that I've now heard about forty times. Zenko wants an enormous plain omelet and a silent morning.

I want to stand at my own stove, in my own socks, and make Katya eggs. That's it. That's the list.

What's the dish that takes you home when you smell it?
