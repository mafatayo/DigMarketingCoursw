*A work of fiction inspired by the accounts of Ukrainian soldiers.*

# Day 39 — Dispatch: Mobilized at 41

Zenko told me his story tonight, and I'm going to tell it back, slowly, because he deserves it.

He doesn't talk about himself. He'll talk about the Cossacks, the Treaty of Pereiaslav, the history of the potato (he has a lecture), but not himself. Tonight, though, we were both on late watch, sitting on a crate under a sky of stars, and the silence got long, and he began.

"I was a teacher for nineteen years," he said. "History, in a school with a leaky roof. I had a class of thirty-one fourteen-year-olds, who I adored and who, I'm sure, found me ridiculous."

"I'm sure you were wonderful."

"I was adequate. They liked my stories." He adjusted his scarf. "I always thought history was something that happened to other people. In books. In the past. I taught it with a certain affectionate distance. As if it were weather in another country."

He paused.

"The letter came on a Wednesday. In the middle of a lesson on the Hetmanate. A man in a uniform stood at the door of my classroom, very polite, and asked if he could speak to me in the corridor. I remember I told the class to continue reading. I remember a boy in the back row, a boy called Maksym who never did the homework, looking at me with a face that said he already knew."

"What happened to the class?"

"Another teacher took them. I didn't get to say goodbye properly. I wrote them a letter. It was an awful letter. I put in a quote from Plutarch, which I'm now fairly sure was invented." He laughed softly. "I told them that history isn't a thing you study. It's a thing you stand inside. I think I was trying to explain myself to myself."

I didn't speak. The stars turned slowly overhead.

"I was afraid," he said. "That's the truth. I'm not young. I wear glasses. I've never held anything heavier than a stack of essays. I thought, they've made a mistake. They'll see it in an hour, and send me home."

"They didn't."

"No." He smiled, sidelong. "And you know what I found? That I'm good at one thing here. I'm good at *explaining.* The young ones come in terrified, and I sit with them and say, 'Here is how it works. Here is why. Here is what is normal.' It turns out a history teacher is useful to the frightened. We are very good at saying: this has happened before, and people survived."

He turned to me. His glasses caught the starlight.

"I used to teach children about the past," he said. "Now I'm teaching soldiers about the next hour. It's the same job. Only the classroom has changed."

I found I couldn't answer. So I did what we do here. I poured him some tea from my thermos and put it in his hands.

# Day 39 — Notes: Older Soldiers, Younger Soldiers

The generations in our dugout, and what each brings.

**The young (Bohdan, Yaroslav):**
- Speed. Comfort with machines.
- Energy at three in the morning.
- Honesty. They say what they feel.
- A lightness that the rest of us need.

**The middle (me, Vira, Halyna):**
- A patchwork of skills from other lives.
- Enough experience to know what to be afraid of.
- Enough youth left to still hope.

**The older (Motor, Zenko):**
- Calm.
- Perspective. "I've seen worse," said with a shrug.
- Stories, jokes, patience.
- Knees that predict the weather.

**What it teaches:** The war doesn't ask for one kind of person. It takes all of us, and finds a job for each.

**A small observation:** The old protect the young. The young carry the old's bags. It's a very old arrangement, and it works.

Who is the oldest person you've ever learned something from?
