# Day 42 — Dispatch: Power Out

It happened at 6:14 p.m., which I know because Vira was looking at the clock when the lamp went out.

Not just ours. Everything. The generator coughed, shuddered, and died, and from somewhere beyond the walls came a long, falling hum as the whole region exhaled and stopped. Then silence, and a darkness so total that for a moment I couldn't tell whether my eyes were open.

"Don't move," said Motor calmly. "Everyone stay where you are. Hands in front."

"I'm not moving," said Bohdan, "I'm just standing very still and screaming."

"You're not screaming."

"Quietly. Inside."

A match flared. Halyna, lighting a candle stub with her usual steady hand, and the room swam back out of the black, yellow and trembling. Seven faces, huge shadows on the wall. The Colonel, wide-eyed, pressed against my knee.

It was our first real blackout. The generator was dead beyond Motor's toothbrush. The power line to the village was cut. There was no more light, no more charging, no more radio battery beyond what we'd saved. For the first time, we were cut off in the plain old sense of the phrase.

I expected panic. I got something stranger. A kind of hush, a settling, as if the whole dugout had been waiting for permission to slow down. Without lamps, without screens, without the glow of Bohdan's workbench, we sat in a small circle around the stove and the one candle and found ourselves, I don't know how else to say it, talking.

Zenko told the story of how his grandfather once walked to Kyiv to find his brother. Vira sang, quietly, a children's song about a sleepy bear. Yaroslav, who'd barely spoken in three days, told us about the farm where he grew up, and a cow named Malvina, who "was cleverer than most people I know." We laughed so hard that Motor had to hush us, in case the neighbors thought we'd gone mad.

It was the best night in weeks.

At some point I stepped outside. The dark was colossal. No lights from any village, no glow on the horizon. Just a vast field of stars, thousands, close enough to touch. I stood there with my head tilted back. The Milky Way hung overhead like spilled milk. The air smelled of frost and distance. Behind me, through the door, I could hear the warm low murmur of voices.

I thought: this is what the world looked like for most of human history. Dark, and cold, and full of people talking by a fire.

And I thought: we can do this.

# Day 42 — Notes: Living Without Electricity

What the dark taught us in a single night.

**Immediate problems:**
- No light. Candles are slow, smoky, and precious.
- No charging. Phones, radios, torches, all starved.
- No heating, if it's electric. Ours is a stove, thank God.
- No news. Strange how quickly that gets uncomfortable.

**Things that helped:**
- A stash of candles. A good torch with a hand crank.
- Warm clothes. Everyone sat close.
- A plan: who goes where, who watches, who sleeps.
- Rationing battery power. One phone on, the rest off.
- Cards, a chessboard, songs, stories.

**What surprised me:** How easy it was to enjoy it.

**What I'd tell anyone at home during a blackout:** Light a candle. Sit with whoever's near. Tell them something true.

What's the best conversation you've ever had in the dark?
