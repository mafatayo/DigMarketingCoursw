*A work of fiction inspired by the accounts of Ukrainian soldiers.*

# Day 24 — Dispatch: Library in a Basement

There's a library in a basement, in the village, and it has forty-one books. I know because I counted. Twice. It's an addiction.

A retired schoolteacher called Mrs. Olena runs it. She's tiny, sharp-eyed, with silver hair pinned up in a style that I suspect is older than my mother. The basement of a cultural center, partly collapsed upstairs, partly stripped of its furniture, is now her kingdom. Shelves made from planks and bricks. A little electric heater when there's power. Two armchairs. A kettle. A sign on the wall, hand-lettered: "SILENCE IS FOR THE LIBRARY. THE REST OF THE WORLD MAY SHOUT."

She lends books to anyone: soldiers, kids, old men. She keeps a ledger in careful cursive. "Borrowed by: Lys, on Thursday. Due: when you can."

I picked up a worn paperback, a collection of short stories, spine broken, someone's name written inside the cover in a child's hand. I read the whole first story on one of the armchairs, while the heater clicked, and for twenty minutes I forgot where I was.

I need to tell you how strange that felt. I had been so used to the sharp small world of the position that I'd forgotten books do that. They take you out. You stand in a field outside yourself.

Mrs. Olena watched me from her desk, over half-moon glasses. "The first story is the best," she said. "Everyone says so."

"It's very good."

"It's about a man who walks home. That's all. He walks home across a whole country, and it takes a hundred pages." She poured tea. "I find soldiers like it. They don't say why."

I knew why.

The soldiers who come to her library, she told me, ask for three kinds of books. Funny ones. Very old ones, anything from before the twentieth century. And poetry, which surprised me. "Short," she said. "You can read a poem in a bad hour and put it in your pocket."

She has a list of the poems she's given out. Forty-seven so far. One young man took a poem and returned it a week later with the page folded down and said, "I read it every night." She never asked him which line.

I borrowed the stories. She lent me a pencil too, so I could underline, which she said is a sin, but a forgivable one. On the way out she stopped me at the door. "Come back and tell me how it ends."

"I will."

"Don't die before the last page."

I promised her I'd try.

# Day 24 — Notes: Books We Actually Read

Our section's informal library, in the order we've passed it round.

1. **A battered collection of short stories.** Mine now. It's a long walk home, on paper.
2. **A thick history of the Cossacks.** Zenko. He reads parts aloud. We tolerate it.
3. **A cookbook, with missing pages.** Bohdan reads it like a thriller.
4. **Poetry, in tiny volumes.** Passed around quietly. Nobody comments.
5. **Detective novels, two-ruble paperbacks.** Fast and cheap, perfect for stand-by.
6. **A children's book with a dragon.** Halyna. She reads it to us on bad nights, in her nurse voice. It works. Don't laugh.
7. **A manual about growing tomatoes.** Motor. He says it's relaxing.

**What we don't read:** Anything heavy about the war. It's all around us. We don't need it in our pockets.

**What books do:** They're a door in the wall. Not an escape, exactly. A change of room.

What's the book you'd bring to a place where you could carry only one?
