# Day 10 — Dispatch: A Quiet Sector Isn't Quiet

"It's quiet today," Bohdan said at breakfast, and the whole table turned and stared at him like he'd cursed the house.

You don't say it. I learned that in the first week. Say "quiet" and the sky takes it as a challenge. Vira threw a spoon at him. It missed, because she's a radio operator, not a pitcher, and it clanged off the stove. Bohdan apologized to the spoon.

But it was quiet. By the standards of the last ten days, it was practically silent. No thudding in the ground. No engines. Just wind, and a faint dripping, and Motor's four bars drifting across the room.

And here's what I've learned about quiet: it isn't peace. It's attention. Everyone in the dugout was hyper-alert, the way you are in a house where you've heard a noise and nobody's sure what. Halyna kept looking up at the ceiling. Zenko read the same page of his book three times. I stood at the top of the steps and listened so hard my ears began to ring.

That's the trick of it. When it's loud, you have no choice. You're busy, you react, the body takes over. When it's quiet, the mind has room, and the mind is a much worse place to be.

Around noon I went out to check the line, and I heard something I couldn't place. A scratching. A click, faint, regular, like a clock in another room. I stood there for a full minute with the hairs on my arms up before I found it: a loose wire against a post, tapping in the wind. Tick. Tick. Tick.

I laughed. Out loud, alone, in a field. It was the funniest thing I'd ever heard. I laughed until my eyes watered, then sat on a sandbag and stopped, suddenly, because I realized how thin I was stretched.

When I got back, Vira looked at my face and just said, "Wire?"

"Wire," I said.

"Everybody gets the wire," she said. "Mine was a plastic bag."

That's the thing about quiet. It teaches you what your nerves have turned into. By the time the first week is over, a plastic bag can ruin your afternoon.

The evening was loud again. Distant, but loud, a rolling sound like furniture moving upstairs. Strangely, the whole dugout relaxed. Shoulders came down. Bohdan opened a book. The normal had come back, and the normal, God help us, was a relief.

# Day 10 — Notes: Reading Sounds

A beginner's glossary, written by a sound engineer, who has now been humbled by a wire.

**Low rumble, long, rolling.** Far. Usually someone else's problem today.

**Sharp crack, close.** Pay attention. Don't freeze. Move to the nearest cover and count.

**A whistle that rises.** That's the one you don't wait to understand. Down first, think after.

**A buzzing, like a very large insect.** A small drone. You learn to hear which direction without looking. It gets in your sleep.

**Silence, sudden, after loud.** The worst. Everyone feels it. Everyone looks up.

**Ticking, scratching, flapping.** Wires, plastic, a loose tarp. Nothing. Still gets you.

**Birds going quiet.** An old trick of nature. If the birds stop, we stop too.

**Your own heartbeat.** Louder than all of them, once you notice it.

My job, back home, was to remove sounds nobody wanted to hear. I thought I was good at listening. I wasn't. I was good at editing. This is a different thing, and it's teaching me.

What's a sound in your life you only notice when it stops?
