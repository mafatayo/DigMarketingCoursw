# Day 80 — Dispatch: Talking About Trauma

A psychologist visited, a gentle, slightly rumpled woman named Olha, and I almost didn't go.

The invitation had come as a notice on the wall: *Voluntary conversations with a specialist. Confidential. Tea provided.* The last word was what did it. I'm not proud. Bohdan, Yaroslav, and I trooped over together one gray afternoon to a small borrowed room behind the command post, three men sitting on plastic chairs with our hands between our knees, like boys waiting outside a headmaster's office.

Olha didn't have a clipboard. She had a kettle and a plate of biscuits. She sat on a crate, slightly lower than us, and said, "I'm not going to ask what happened. I'm going to ask what you've noticed."

Nobody spoke for a long while. A biscuit was eaten, with great concentration.

Then Yaroslav, in a very small voice, said, "I can't stop checking the door."

"How long?"

"Since the first week."

"That makes sense," Olha said. "Your body's learned a job. It hasn't been told the job's changed."

She said it so simply that I felt something loosen in my chest. Not *you're weak,* or *you're broken,* but: *your body's learned a job.* Bohdan said he'd been flinching at kitchen sounds. I admitted, haltingly, that I'd stopped being able to listen to music with a bass line. I'd walk out of any room that had one. A whole career built on low frequencies, and now one vibration in the chest could end my evening.

"What do you do when it happens?" said Olha.

"Walk away."

"Does it help?"

"A bit."

"Good. Then you've already got one tool." She smiled. "I'll give you two more."

Her tools were small. Breathing out longer than in. Naming five things you can see. Putting a cold hand on the back of the neck. Telling someone, even just: *I'm having a bad minute.* Nothing grand. But there was something calming about the way she laid them out, one by one, like a seamstress arranging needles.

"And this," she said at the end, "is the most important one. It's *normal.* It's what a healthy body does in an unhealthy place. You are not the problem. The place is."

Bohdan was crying quietly. Yaroslav had both hands over his face. I sat very still.

Walking back, the three of us said nothing. At the dugout door, Bohdan stopped and said, "I thought it'd be worse."

"What?"

"Asking."

Motor, who'd been listening from the steps, grunted. "Next time," he said, "I'll come."

# Day 80 — Notes: Mental Health in Uniform

Not professional advice. Just what I'd like others to know.

**Normal things, after bad things:**
- Jumpiness.
- Poor sleep.
- Numbness, or sudden tears.
- Anger, out of nowhere.
- Guilt, even without cause.
- Not enjoying things you used to.

**When to ask for help:**
- It's getting worse, not better.
- You can't function.
- You're thinking of hurting yourself.
- You're drinking to cope.
- You just *want* to.

**How to ask:** "I'm not okay. Can we talk?" Five words.

**If you're reading this and hurting:** Please reach out to someone today. A friend, a doctor, a helpline in your country. You're not a burden. You're a person.
