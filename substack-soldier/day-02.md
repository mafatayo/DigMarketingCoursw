*A work of fiction inspired by the accounts of Ukrainian soldiers.*

# Day 2 — Dispatch: First Night in the Dugout

Six weeks of training had taught me how to strip a rifle, how to carry a stretcher without dropping the person on it, and how to stand in line. I was good at standing in line. Nobody had taught me what a dugout smells like, so let me do that now.

Wet earth, first. Then smoke from the little stove, then feet, then something sweet underneath it all that I eventually worked out was old tea leaves in a bucket. If you've ever been in your grandfather's cellar in autumn, add forty years of tobacco and a few men who haven't had a proper wash since Tuesday. That's it. That's home now.

We came in after dark, which is the only way anyone comes in. A guy I'd never met led the way with a red light, saying "step here, not there, here, no, there" in a whisper like we were burgling a church. At the bottom of the steps a blanket hung over the door. He lifted it and warm air and yellow light fell out at me, and for about three seconds I was ridiculously happy. A room! With a lamp!

Then someone said, "Close the blanket, you idiot," and the three seconds were over.

There were six of us in a space where my grandmother would have kept her preserves. Bunks stacked like shelves, a table made from an ammunition crate, a shelf with a kettle and nine mismatched mugs. One of the mugs had a cartoon cat on it. I want you to know that this was the first thing I noticed. Not the maps or the radios. The cat mug.

"That's Vira's," said the sergeant, who hadn't looked up from whatever he was doing to a tiny engine part with a toothbrush. "Touch it and you'll be sleeping outside."

He was in his fifties, thick through the shoulders, with a gray mustache that looked like it had opinions. They called him Motor. I found out later he'd fixed city buses for twenty-six years. He hummed while he worked, the same four bars over and over, never the full song.

They gave me the top bunk, which I think was a kindness, because the top bunk is warmest. Heat rises. This is physics, and also a small mercy, and I'll take both.

I lay there with my boots on, because they told me to, and listened. Somewhere above us, far enough that it was only a feeling in my chest, something thudded. Not close. The kind of far where the ground tells you rather than your ears. The stove ticked. Someone snored. Motor hummed his four bars, quieter now.

I thought: I should be more afraid than I am. Then I thought: maybe it's coming and I'm just early.

I didn't sleep much. But sometime near morning the kettle started going, and somebody pushed a mug into my hand without a word, and it was the cat one. I looked at Vira across the table. She raised one eyebrow and said, "Don't get used to it."

I held that mug with both hands like it was the Holy Grail.

# Day 2 — Notes: How to Sleep When the Ground Talks

Nobody gave me this in training, so here's what the six of us worked out.

**1. Boots on, laces loose.** You want to be able to run. You also want your feet to survive the night. Loose laces do both.

**2. Learn the sounds you can ignore.** The stove ticking. Water dripping. Motor humming. These are the sounds of normal. Your brain can't sleep until it files them under "safe." Give it a few nights.

**3. Earplugs, but only one.** Put one in, leave one ear open. Yes, it feels ridiculous. You'll sleep better than you think and still hear your name.

**4. Face the wall.** This isn't a tactic. It's just that the light bothers you less and you can pretend the wall is yours.

**5. Don't count the thuds.** I tried. Counting makes you a spectator, and spectators don't sleep. Count something else. I count the people I love in order of how annoyed they'd be if I got hurt. Katya wins every time. My mother-in-law is second, which tells you something.

**6. Sleep when you can, anywhere.** On the stairs, on a crate, sitting up in the truck. Nobody here is picky.

I slept four hours last night. The man below me slept ten minutes and swears he's fine. I don't believe him, but I'm learning not to say so.

What helps you sleep when you're afraid? I mean it. I'm taking suggestions.
