*A work of fiction inspired by the accounts of Ukrainian soldiers.*

# Day 33 — Dispatch: Cold Arrives

It arrived overnight and didn't ask.

Yesterday it was cool. Today I woke at four with my breath standing in front of my face and the blanket stiff along the edge where my exhale had frozen into tiny white feathers. The water in the bucket had a skin on it. The Colonel was curled into a tight ring on my chest, which I would call affection, though in fairness it was also survival.

Cold changes everything. People don't tell you that. They say "it gets cold," as if it were a feeling, like sadness. It's not. It's a force. It enters your boots, your wrists, the gap under your collar. It makes your fingers stupid. By noon I couldn't undo a button. By afternoon, I'd stopped being annoyed and started being grateful for each small warmth like a monk.

The first morning of real cold, we got a lesson from Halyna, who stood at the end of the table like a general and gave a speech.

"Listen. Cold kills slowly. It's polite about it. You'll feel sleepy, comfortable, a bit silly. That's the danger. If your hands stop hurting, tell me. If you stop shivering, tell me. If someone starts talking nonsense, tell me. Understood?"

We nodded.

"And feet. Feet, feet, feet."

"We know about feet," said Bohdan.

"You know nothing about feet," said Halyna, with menace. "Layers."

So we layered. I wore everything I had: three shirts, a wool sweater, the jacket, two scarves, a hat Katya knitted me from a pattern she'd found online and a small amount of faith. I looked like a very tall onion. Vira laughed so hard she coughed.

"Come on, then, onion," she said. "Time to dig."

There was a long ditch to deepen, in frozen ground, in the wind. The work was brutal. The earth rang under the spade like iron. After ten minutes my palms were burning and my breath was coming in quick white clouds. And yet, strangely, there was a kind of joy in it. The warmth of the work. The steam rising off us all like a row of kettles. Zenko, red-cheeked, was reciting something in Latin. Motor, beside me, shoveled steadily, humming his four bars into the cold.

At dusk we returned and the stove, our small black faithful stove, was roaring. Halyna had made soup. We peeled off our outer layers, gloves steaming, fingers returning to life in a sharp bright pain that felt like being forgiven.

I held my hands to the stove and thought: winter has barely started.

# Day 33 — Notes: Layers, Explained

A short, practical guide, in the order of your body.

**Head.** A hat. Wool, with ears. Half your heat leaves here. An old rule, but true enough.

**Neck.** A scarf or a buff. Never skip it. The neck is the door the cold walks through.

**Core.** Three layers. Thin close one. Warm middle one. Windproof outer one. Not cotton. Never cotton.

**Hands.** Thin gloves inside thick ones, so you can work and still keep your fingers. Mittens are warmer. Gloves are more useful. We argue.

**Legs.** Thermal under, trousers over. Don't tuck them too tight, or the circulation dies.

**Feet.** Two thin pairs, not one thick. Air between them is the warm part. Dry insoles. Wriggle toes often.

**Everything else:**
- Don't sweat. Sweat freezes. Strip a layer when you dig and put it back when you stop.
- Eat. Calories are fuel for the little furnace.
- Sip hot water. Drink before you're thirsty.
- Hand warmers in the pockets, not near the skin.
- Keep dry. All of it.

If you'd like to help a soldier this winter, wool and warm things always find a home.
