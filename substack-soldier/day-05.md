# Day 5 — Dispatch: The Rules Nobody Writes Down

They gave me a thick booklet in training. Regulations, orders, procedures. I read it twice and it told me nothing about how to live in a hole in the ground with five other people.

The real rules, I've learned, are never written. Here are the ones I've picked up so far, some by being told and some by doing it wrong.

**You don't take the last of anything without saying so.** The last sugar, the last dry sock, the last good cigarette. You say it out loud. "I'm taking the last one." Someone might need it more. They usually say "go ahead." But the asking is the point.

**You don't tell a man he looks tired.** Everyone looks tired. It's like telling someone the sky is up.

**You don't sit in someone's spot.** Not on the bunk, not at the crate. I did it on day three and the room went quiet in a way I felt in my teeth. Zenko leaned over and said gently, "That's Halyna's." I moved. Halyna wasn't even there. It didn't matter.

**You wash up even when you're dead on your feet.** This one came from Halyna, and it comes with a small lecture each time, but there's wisdom under the nagging. A man who gives up on soap is a man giving up.

**You don't talk about what scares you in the dugout at night.** Do it outside, in daylight, to a person you trust. Nighttime fear is contagious. Daytime fear is manageable.

**You don't read someone else's letters.** Even if they leave them open. Even if they're crying. Especially then.

**You don't say "if I get home." You say "when."** I slipped once and Vira looked at me like I'd spat on the floor. She didn't say a word. She didn't need to. I haven't said "if" since.

**You always tell someone where you're going.** Even to the latrine, which is a bucket behind a tarp. "Going out." That's all. It's so no one has to wonder.

I asked Motor yesterday why none of this is written down. He thought about it for a long time, long enough that I figured he'd forgotten the question.

"Because if you write it down," he said finally, "someone will follow the letter and forget the point."

Then he handed me a bucket.

# Day 5 — Notes: Ten Words of Soldier's Ukrainian

I'm not a linguist. These are just words I use now that I didn't six weeks ago.

1. **Нуль (nul)** — the front line, "zero." Where it starts.
2. **Двохсотий / трьохсотий (dvokhsotyi / trokhsotyi)** — "two hundred" and "three hundred." The first means killed, the second wounded. I'll leave it there. It's a code, and the heaviest one we have.
3. **Бліндаж (blindazh)** — the dugout. Home.
4. **Ротація (rotatsiia)** — rotation. The most beautiful word, because it means someone's going home to sleep.
5. **Прильот (prylit)** — an impact. Something arrived. Said flatly, like a delivery.
6. **Птахи (ptakhy)** — "birds." Drones.
7. **Сірі / сірко (siro)** — okay, gray, so-so. If someone asks how you are and you say "grey," they understand.
8. **Бахнуло (bakhnulo)** — "it went bang." The kind of word a child would invent. It's perfect.
9. **Тривога (tryvoha)** — alarm. Also anxiety. The same word for both, which tells you about the language.
10. **Дякую (diakuiu)** — thank you. Used constantly. Never wasted.

Strangers sometimes ask whether soldiers swear a lot. Yes. We have an entire second language for it. I won't print it here, partly because Substack has standards and partly because Zenko, a history teacher, would be hurt.
