*A work of fiction inspired by the accounts of Ukrainian soldiers.*

# Day 38 — Dispatch: The New Guy

He arrived at dusk with a bag too big for him and boots too new, and the first thing he did was apologize to the Colonel.

"Sorry," he said, as the dog sniffed his knees. "Sorry. I'm new."

The Colonel, who accepts all apologies, wagged.

His name was Yaroslav, nineteen, from a small town I'd never heard of, with ears that stuck out and a thin neck he kept tucking into his collar. He was a replacement, the first we'd had. For a month, we'd been the same six, a closed circle. Now there was a seventh, and nobody knew how to behave.

Motor looked him over from boots to cap. "Eaten?"

"Not since the morning."

"Sit."

Halyna was already ladling soup. Vira, at the radio, glanced over her shoulder and said, "Who's your mother?" which is her standard question, and he said, "She's a teacher," and Vira said, "Good. Teachers raise polite ones."

"He's very polite," said Zenko, delighted.

I recognized the look on Yaroslav's face, because I'd worn it a month earlier. It's a blend of terror and hope and the strong desire to be invisible. His hands wouldn't stay still. He held the soup bowl with both hands and ate fast, shoulders hunched, as if someone might take it away.

"Slow down," said Halyna. "It's not going anywhere."

He slowed.

It fell to me to show him the dugout, which gave me a strange feeling, being the senior one, the one who knew where the bucket went. I walked him through the rules. The boots at the third step. The watch rota. The mug with the cartoon cat, "touch it and you're dead." I stopped, because I realized I was quoting Vira, and that she'd quoted somebody, and so on, a chain of tired people handing down the same small jokes.

"The latrine's outside, behind the tarp," I said. "Tell someone when you go."

"Why?"

"So we don't wonder."

He thought about that. Then he nodded, very slowly. "That's... nice," he said.

"It's the rule."

"No, I mean the reason."

That night, for the first time, I heard a different breath in the dark. A seventh sleeper on the lowest bunk, curled tight, the way I'd once curled. A few hours in, he whispered, "Lys?"

"Yes?"

"Is it always this loud?"

I listened. There was a distant thud, far, and the stove ticking, and Motor's four bars.

"This isn't loud," I said. "This is Tuesday."

I heard him laugh, a surprised little breath. Then, at last, he slept.

# Day 38 — Notes: Training Replacements

What I'm trying to do for the new guy. In order of importance.

1. **Feed him first.** Everything else can wait.
2. **Tell him it's all right to be scared.** Say it early.
3. **Teach him the unwritten rules.** Gently, one at a time.
4. **Give him a job right away.** A small useful one. Idle hands are the worst.
5. **Make sure someone sits next to him.** A person, not a rule.
6. **Don't lie about how hard it is.** He'll know.
7. **Remember who showed *you*.** Pass it on.

**What I've realized:** Nobody becomes a soldier in a single day. They become one in a hundred small kindnesses. Mine came from a woman with a mug.

I'm trying to pay it forward.
