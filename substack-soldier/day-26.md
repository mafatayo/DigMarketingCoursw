*A work of fiction inspired by the accounts of Ukrainian soldiers.*

# Day 26 — Dispatch: The Football Argument

The argument started at lunch and ended, I think, around midnight, and in between, I learned that men who've faced real danger together will fight to the last breath about a disallowed goal from years ago.

It began innocently. Bohdan said something about the national team. Motor, who'd been quietly eating, put down his spoon, which is how you know he's about to say something that matters.

"That game against England," he said. "The goal that wasn't given."

"Which wasn't given?" said Vira. "It crossed the line. Everyone saw it cross the line."

"It did not cross the line."

"Motor, the ball was in."

"The ball was *on* it."

I won't pretend I understood what happened next. I'm not a big fan. But within five minutes the whole dugout was divided into factions. Bohdan and Vira, the "it was in" party. Motor, Zenko, and Halyna, the "it was never in" party. I, the sound engineer, was asked to determine the truth by "analyzing the audio of the stadium."

"I don't have the audio."

"Then use your instincts."

"My instincts say I'm hungry."

Nobody cared. They were off. Zenko gave a lecture on the history of the offside rule. Halyna accused Vira of "emotional bias." Vira accused Halyna of "being a Lviv person." The Colonel left in disgust.

And then I realized something. Everyone was shouting. Everyone was red in the face, waving hands, laughing, cutting each other off. It was loud, it was human, it was completely beside the point. For three hours nobody in the dugout was a soldier. They were a bunch of people who care about a ball.

Because the thing is, the front doesn't leave room for pointless arguments. Every conversation is about something, supplies, weather, plans, who's on watch. It's rare to have a fight about nothing. It felt almost luxurious. It felt like peacetime.

At some point Motor said, "Fine. It was in."

A gasp. "You're conceding?"

"All these years," he said. "I'm tired. I wanted to see if I could still be this angry about something stupid."

"And?"

"I can." He smiled, a rare slow one. "It's the best I've felt all week."

I lay in my bunk later, grinning at the ceiling, thinking: that's what we're protecting, in the end. The right to argue about a football match, at midnight, in our own kitchens, for no reason.

# Day 26 — Notes: Normality as Resistance

Some of the things we do on purpose, as acts of stubbornness.

- **Argue about sport.** Fiercely.
- **Shave.** Zenko, Sundays.
- **Celebrate birthdays.** With cake, with crumbs.
- **Make tea properly.** Not just hot water.
- **Tell jokes.** Old ones.
- **Make plans.** "When I'm home I'll..." Always "when."
- **Keep a clean notebook.** Vira's is immaculate.
- **Look at the sky.** On purpose. Name a constellation.
- **Say thank you.** Even for the small stuff.

**Why:** Because the war tries to make us into only one thing. A soldier. A target. A number. Everything normal we do is a small refusal.

I used to think resistance was loud. Now I think it's often a man at a kitchen table, defending a disallowed goal from a match nobody can change.

What's a small normal thing you refuse to give up?
