*A work of fiction inspired by the accounts of Ukrainian soldiers.*

# Day 40 — Dispatch: Women in the Platoon

I want to say something about Vira and Halyna, and I want to say it plainly, because they'd hate it if I got sentimental.

There are, in our little group, two women, and the first thing they'd tell you is that they're not "the women." They're the radio operator and the medic. Their gender is, in the dugout, about as relevant as the color of their socks. And yet I've noticed how often, in the wider world, it still is.

A man came through last week from a staff office, a clean, pink, well-fed sort of man in a very neat jacket. He walked in, looked around, and addressed Motor. "Sergeant, where's your radio operator?"

"There," said Motor, pointing.

The man looked at Vira. Then back at Motor. "No, I mean the *radio operator.*"

"That's what I said."

There was a pause. Vira, without turning, took her headset off her left ear and said pleasantly, "Good morning. I'm Magpie. What's your question?"

The man flushed. He asked his question. She answered it in four sentences, correctly, and then added a fifth that fixed a mistake in his own paperwork. He left looking like a man who had stepped on a rake.

"Did you enjoy that?" I asked her later.

"Enormously," she said. "It's the highlight of my month."

But the thing I want you to know is the rest. The long, dull, unspectacular part. Vira's work is ten, twelve hours a day, listening to a stream of static and voices, catching what matters, passing it on, never missing a word. It takes concentration that would break most men in an hour. Halyna's is worse: she carries more than any of us, medically and otherwise, and she does it without complaint. I've seen her sew a torn boot at midnight, then treat a sprain at two, then cook breakfast.

They don't do it for recognition. They do it because they're good, and because somebody has to.

I asked Vira once what she'd say to people who thought women didn't belong here. She considered it, chewing on the end of a pen.

"I wouldn't argue. I'd hand them the headset. Twenty minutes. Then we'd talk."

"You wouldn't argue?"

"No," she said. "Arguing is for people who have time. I have a radio."

I think I understand now that respect in a place like this has nothing to do with speeches. It's built from small things. You do the job. You do it well. You're reliable. You help. Eventually even the pink man in the neat jacket notices.

# Day 40 — Notes: Respect Is Earned the Same Way

What I see, over and over.

- Respect comes from **reliability**. You show up.
- Respect comes from **competence**. You know your work.
- Respect comes from **kindness**. You help, even when you're tired.
- Respect comes from **honesty**. You say what you don't know.
- Respect never comes from **volume.** The loudest people here are often the least trusted.

And yes, it's harder for some. Women, younger people, anyone who doesn't fit the old picture. They have to do it twice. I've watched it. It's unfair, and everyone in the dugout knows it.

**What we do about it:**
1. Back each other up, out loud.
2. Never laugh at a joke that undermines a colleague.
3. Pay attention to who's being talked over.
4. Say "that's her job, ask her," and mean it.

There are more women in uniform now than ever before, and they're good. I'll say it plainly: they're better than some of the men who doubted them.

If you've ever been underestimated, how did you answer it?
