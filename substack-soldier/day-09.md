# Day 9 — Dispatch: The Sergeant Who Hums

I finally found out what Motor hums.

It took nine days. I'd been working on it the way you work on a crossword, in the background, a little every day. Four bars, same four bars. Up, up, down, a long note that doesn't resolve. It sounded like something I knew from somewhere. A radio in a kitchen. A lullaby. A song a mother sings that you can't quite place.

Bohdan guessed it was a pop hit. Vira said it was a funeral march. Zenko offered "possibly a Cossack lament, possibly nothing," which is the kind of answer he gives to most questions.

Finally this morning, I was helping Motor carry water from the canisters, two buckets each, and out of nowhere I said, "What's the song?"

He stopped. Water sloshed on his boots. He looked at me for a long moment like I'd asked what was in his wallet.

"What song?"

"The one you hum. The four bars."

He was quiet. Then he put down the buckets and, very quietly, gruffly, he sang it. Properly, the whole thing. His voice was low and rough and a little cracked, and he sang a few lines in an old-fashioned way, like a village. I won't print the words because I don't think they're mine to share, but it was about a bus. A woman waiting for a bus at the end of a road, and the road long and the evening coming.

"My wife used to sing it," he said. "Twenty-six years in the depot, and she'd be sitting at the stop when I came off shift. Every night. She'd have a thermos."

"Used to?"

"She's alive," he said, a little offended. "She's in Chernihiv. She still sings it. I just get four bars of it out loud, because after four I start to think about her too much." He picked the buckets back up. "That's all it is. Four bars is what I can afford."

I didn't know what to say. So I said nothing, and we carried the water.

That's how I've come to think of leadership. Not the shouting. Not the speeches. A fifty-three-year-old bus mechanic who parcels out his own longing in four-bar portions so he can keep the rest of us steady. He's never said a word about fear. He hums it into the walls where it can't hurt anyone.

When we got back, Bohdan asked what the song was. Motor said, "A song about buses," and returned to his tiny engine parts.

That was true, in its way.

# Day 9 — Notes: Leadership by Small Habits

I've been watching Motor all week, and here's the pattern. None of it is dramatic.

- **He eats last.** Not as a gesture. He just waits until everyone has a bowl.
- **He learns your name on day one and uses it right.** Not the callsign. The actual name, quietly, when it matters.
- **He asks one question at the end of the day.** "Anything hurt?" Not "How are you." Anything hurt. You can answer that honestly.
- **He fixes things himself, in front of us.** A boot, a stove, a hinge. It's a small thing. It says: nothing here is beneath me.
- **He apologizes when he's wrong.** Short and flat. "I made a mistake. Here's the fix." Nobody has ever respected him less for it.
- **He doesn't promise.** He says, "I'll try," or "I'll see." When he does say "yes," the room believes it.

I used to think good leaders were charismatic. Loud. Visionary. Here, the best leader I've met is a mechanic with a mustache who checks my feet and hums.

Who's led you well in your life, and what did they actually do? I'd like the specifics.
