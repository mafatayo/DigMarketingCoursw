*A work of fiction inspired by the accounts of Ukrainian soldiers.*

# Day 85 — Dispatch: A Fallen Friend's Family

I met the family of one of the names in my ledger, and I'm still not sure I did it right.

It happened on a rare short pass, on the road back from the rear. The man was Oleksandr, "the one who sang while peeling potatoes," the first line I'd written in the notebook, the one who called everyone *friend.* His widow had written to our command, asking if anyone who'd known him could stop by. A message was passed down. I volunteered, with a stomach full of knives.

She lived in a small house on the edge of a small town, with a green gate and a pear tree in the yard. I'd brought a jar of Katya's jam and Sonya's drawing of a fox, the only things I had. She met me at the door. Fifties, narrow, tired, with gray hair pulled back in a clip and a faded apron over a good blouse. She'd obviously dressed for me. I felt a wave of shame.

"You're the sound man," she said. "He talked about you."

"He did?"

"He said you were a quiet one. He said, 'That's the best kind. They hear things.'"

I couldn't speak.

She led me through to the kitchen, where a girl of about sixteen sat at the table, doing homework, and a very old man in the corner by the stove. "My father," she said. "And my daughter, Olena. Sit."

I sat. I'd planned to say something. A careful sentence. I'd rehearsed it on the bus. Instead, to my own astonishment, I said, "He sang. Every morning. While he peeled the potatoes. A song about a girl at a gate. He never finished it."

The girl at the table looked up.

"'The Girl at the Gate,'" said the old man, and his voice shook. "He learned it from me."

"He called everyone *friend,*" I said. "Everyone. Even the new guy. Even the dog."

The widow put a hand to her mouth. Then, to my relief, she laughed. A short, wet, astonished laugh. "He did that here too. The postman. The priest. The man who sold him fish."

For two hours we sat there, and I told her every small thing I knew. A spoon he'd carved. A joke he told badly. The way he warmed his hands on the mug before he drank. And she told me her side, the boy who'd been terrible at school, the man who'd learned to cook for her, the potatoes, always, always the potatoes.

When I left, the girl walked me to the gate, under the pear tree. "Thank you for telling us the little stuff," she said. "Everyone else just says he was brave."

"He was," I said. "But the little stuff is the whole man."

She nodded. At the green gate she hugged me, hard, quickly, and then stood very straight, as he must have taught her.

# Day 85 — Notes: Carrying Their Stories

What I've learned about visiting the families of the fallen.

1. **Go, if you can.** Even if you're afraid.
2. **Bring a small thing.** A jar. A drawing.
3. **Tell the little stories.** They're the ones nobody else has.
4. **Don't say "he's in a better place."** Say what you remember.
5. **Let them cry. Let them laugh. Both.**
6. **Write down what they tell you.** It's another page in the ledger.
7. **Say the name.**

**What I carry now:** A song with no ending, and a girl at a gate.
