# Day 41 — Dispatch: The Translator

The foreigner came with a delivery of medical supplies, and for the first ten minutes nobody could tell if he was a doctor, a journalist, or lost.

He was tall, thin, bearded, in a jacket several sizes too clean, with a backpack that looked like it had been bought the day before and a face of sincere, wide-eyed cheerfulness. He spoke English, quickly, and a very small amount of Ukrainian, which he'd obviously learned from an app. "Hello," he said to Motor, with a deep bow. "I am happy to see you. I am a volunteer. I have... *bandages.*"

Motor looked at him with great gravity. "Welcome," he said in Ukrainian. "You are very tall."

The foreigner smiled enormously and nodded.

"I don't think he understood that," said Vira.

"He understood the tone," said Motor.

So began a day of comedy. The foreigner's name was Daniel, a nurse from a small city in Canada, who'd taken three months off work and paid his own way. He had a phrasebook. He consulted it with great seriousness, and used it with great confidence, and said things like "I am warm in the stove" and "Please, I desire the bread."

"This man is a treasure," whispered Bohdan.

Zenko, who speaks decent English, volunteered to translate. He stepped into the role with enormous dignity, like a diplomat at a peace conference, and the conversation ran for an hour. Daniel told us about his mother, his dog, his cold winters. We told him about borshch. Zenko translated, and edited, and sometimes just invented, because a man of his training cannot resist a good transition.

At one point Daniel asked what the word was for "grandmother," and Vira said "babusya," and he said it with a lovely flat Canadian accent, "bah-boo-sya," and we all fell about laughing.

But there was a moment, late, when the laughter stopped. Daniel had gone quiet. He was looking at the wall of cards, the children's drawings, Maria's blue paper heart, the flowers from far away. He put a finger gently on a drawing of a sunflower.

"I came," he said slowly, in careful English, and Zenko leaned in to translate, "because I didn't know what else to do. I watched on a screen. It wasn't enough."

Zenko translated. No one said anything.

"It's a small thing," Daniel said. "A few bandages."

Halyna reached over and took his hand. In Ukrainian, which he didn't understand, she said, "It's not a small thing." And somehow he understood, because he squeezed back.

# Day 41 — Notes: Foreign Volunteers

A few observations on the people who come from far away.

**Who they are:** Nurses, mechanics, retirees, students, pilots, cooks, interpreters, a surprising number of Scots.

**What they do:** Drive vans. Carry stretchers. Deliver supplies. Teach first aid. Fix generators. Sometimes simply sit and listen.

**What they bring:** Equipment, yes. But mostly proof that someone looked up from their life and said: *this matters.*

**What I want to say to them:**
- Thank you.
- We know it cost you.
- We know you're afraid too.
- You don't have to be heroic. You can be useful. That's more than enough.

**A small tip for anyone thinking of coming:** Learn five words. *Hello. Thank you. Please. Tea. Sorry.* It's astonishing how far they go.

*Dyakuyu*, Daniel.
