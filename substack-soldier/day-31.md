*A work of fiction inspired by the accounts of Ukrainian soldiers.*

# Day 31 — Dispatch: The Drone Above Us

You hear it before you decide to hear it.

I was outside the dugout, mid-yawn, a mug in my hand, when the sound slid into the morning. High and thin, like a mosquito in a dark bedroom, except the mosquito had a purpose. Everyone outside stopped. Not dramatically. Nobody dove for cover. We just stopped, the way a room stops when someone says your name.

I didn't look up. You learn not to. Looking up is the one thing it wants. Motor taught us that in the first week, in the quiet voice he uses for things that matter: "Don't point. Don't stare. Don't run in the open. Walk like you've got nothing interesting to hide, and get under something."

So I walked. Slowly, with a mug of cooling tea, toward the door, and my skin felt as if someone were holding a cold hand an inch above it. The buzz hung there, drifting, patient. Somewhere above the gray sky a small machine with a camera was looking down at the roof of my life.

Inside, Vira was already on the radio, voice flat and quick, relaying what we'd heard. Bohdan came in behind me looking sick. "That's not ours," he said.

"I know."

"It's *theirs.*"

"I know, Bohdan."

He sat down heavily. He flies little machines every day. He knows how they handle, how they sing, how they get tired in the cold. He told me once that after a long flight he feels the drone is a kind of extension of his own eyes. And now someone else's was above us, and he knew exactly how it was looking.

"They can see the stove smoke," he said quietly. "They can see the path."

"They see a lot of paths."

"Yes." He rubbed his face. "It's strange. I wasn't scared of the guns. The guns are loud and then they stop. This is just... looking."

It passed. After eleven minutes, the buzz thinned and drifted away, as if bored. We waited another half hour anyway. Nobody spoke. Halyna put the kettle on, very softly, as if the sound of the lid might call it back.

I've been trying to work out why the drone is worse than anything else. I think it's that it doesn't hate you. It doesn't feel anything. It's a patient eye, a small cold intelligence, and what you fear isn't it, but the person at the other end of the signal, at a screen, who will decide.

Later, Bohdan flew a patrol of his own. I stood behind him and watched the little screen. A road. A line of trees. A bright flat sky. And I thought: it's the same machine, the same thing, and we're no different. This is what the century has done to us.

# Day 31 — Notes: The Sound of a Quadcopter

From a man who used to record sounds for a living.

**What it sounds like:**
- A pitch around the range of a very high hum, with a ragged edge where the little motors fight.
- A faint rhythmic pulse inside the pitch, as four propellers never quite agree.
- When it turns, the pitch bends. When it rises, it thins.
- When it hangs in the air, it sounds like thinking.

**What it does to a person:**
1. Your neck goes tight.
2. You stop talking mid-sentence.
3. You scan for cover without deciding to.
4. For days afterward, any mosquito makes you flinch.

**What I've learned about fear and sound:** It isn't the volume. It's the *meaning.* A bomb is loud, and you know what it means. A buzz is quiet and means everything.

A strange fact: I used to love that sound. I made a short film once with a drone shot over a field of sunflowers, the camera drifting up, the hum like a bee. I'll never hear it the same way again.

What sound in your life used to be innocent?
