# Day 3 — Dispatch: Meet the Section

There are six of us in the dugout, and I'm going to introduce them the way I would if you came down the steps tonight, except you'd get less light and more coughing.

**Motor.** The sergeant. Fifty-three. You've met his mustache. He doesn't give speeches. He gives chores. If Motor looks at you for more than two seconds, a bucket will appear in your hand. He told me on day one, "I don't need you brave, I need you on time," and I've built my whole personality around it since.

**Vira.** Radio operator, thirty-one, from Poltava. She has a laugh like a door slamming and a talent for finding the exact word that will make you feel small and then giving you chocolate. Where she gets chocolate I will never know. She runs the radio like a conductor. Everybody else in the dugout is at best an instrument.

**Bohdan.** Twenty-two. The kid. He was a third-year student of something to do with computers until the winter, and now he flies the small drones. He's quick, nervous, and he apologizes to the drones when he lands them badly. I have seen this with my own eyes. "Sorry, sorry, good girl."

**Halyna.** The medic. Forty-four, a nurse from Lviv for twenty years before this. She has the calmest hands I've ever seen. Her hands don't shake when anything is happening. Her voice, though, she saves for yelling at us about washing. "Wash your hands! I'm not sewing up something you grew in your pocket!"

**Zenko.** Forty-one. A schoolteacher. History, which he teaches the way people talk about the weather, as if it's mostly bad and you'd better dress for it. He's been mobilized three months and still calls us "gentlemen," including Vira and Halyna, who've both told him to stop.

And me. The sound engineer who reads the quiet like a book.

I want to tell you the thing that surprised me, which is that nobody here is who you'd pick. If you'd asked me to assemble a team, I'd have hired a different team. A tidier, tougher one, from a film. This team has a teacher who wears two scarves and a nurse who cries at dog videos and a kid who says sorry to quadcopters.

But watch them for a day. Motor sees that Bohdan hasn't eaten and just sets a bowl next to his elbow without a word. Vira reads Zenko's letters out loud when his eyes are too tired. Halyna checks everyone's feet every evening like a kindergarten teacher checking mittens. Nobody calls this anything. It's just Tuesday.

On my second night Zenko leaned across the table and said, quietly, "You'll learn their names for things. Not the callsigns. The other names. The ones that mean they like you."

"What's mine?" I said.

"You don't have one yet." He smiled. "That means you're new."

I'm still waiting.

# Day 3 — Notes: Callsigns, and How You Get Yours

You don't choose it. That's rule one. If you choose it, you're stuck with something like "Wolf" or "Viper," and by the third week somebody will be calling you "Wolfie" in front of the whole unit.

**How callsigns happen, as far as I can tell:**

- **The first mistake.** A man in the next section is called "Pot" because he dropped one on his foot the first morning. He's a very good soldier now. He is still Pot.
- **The job.** Motor is Motor. Obvious.
- **A physical thing.** Halyna calls the tall guy in the supply truck "Lamppost." He pretends to hate it.
- **Where you came from.** Anyone from Odesa is "Sea." Everyone. Doesn't matter what their name is.
- **A mistake in the radio.** Mine. I'll tell you in a couple of weeks, and I'll regret it.

**What a callsign does:** It lets you be somebody else for a few hours. You aren't the man who left a kettle on the stove and a kid asleep in the next room. You're "Lys" (Fox), and Lys has a job and a bag and doesn't think about the kettle.

I think that's why they're a gift, even the stupid ones.

**A rule I learned fast:** Never use someone's real name on the radio. Never use it when you're angry. Never use it when someone's hurt. A real name is the door to home, and nobody wants to open it where there's smoke.

If you could pick a callsign for yourself, it would be the wrong one. Tell me anyway.
