*A work of fiction inspired by the accounts of Ukrainian soldiers.*

# Day 25 — Dispatch: A Guitar with Three Strings

Somebody found a guitar in the ruins of a house. Three strings, a crack down the soundboard, a sticker of a cartoon sun on the back that had faded to the color of weak tea. They brought it back to us like it was a rescued puppy.

Nobody in the section can play. This became immediately clear. Bohdan tried a chord and produced a sound like a door falling downstairs. Vira plucked a string and said, "It's a very sad guitar."

"It has a soul," said Zenko.

"It has three strings."

"A soul and three strings. Most of us have less."

For two days the guitar sat in a corner, looked at but not touched, like a relative nobody wanted to be the first to greet. Then, last night, around ten, a quiet man named Taras walked in from the next dugout. He's a tall, gentle, sleepy-eyed fellow who works with the supply group and hadn't said ten words to me in three weeks. He saw the guitar. He stopped.

"May I?"

Nobody breathed. He sat on a crate, put the guitar across his knee, and tuned the three strings, by ear, to something that I'm sure no music teacher would call correct. He turned a peg, listened, turned it again. Then he began to play.

It wasn't virtuosic. It was a handful of notes, simple, slow, repeating. He used the three strings like a man walking across a river on three stones. But the song, I recognized, after a moment. An old Ukrainian lullaby, one my grandmother used to sing. It has no real chords, it's mostly a melody, and three strings turned out to be exactly enough.

And then he sang. Low, a little hoarse, without any show. Just the words, almost spoken. The dugout was totally still. Even the Colonel lifted his head from my boot.

I looked around. Halyna had both hands over her mouth. Bohdan's eyes were closed. Motor's four bars hummed along under Taras's voice, very faint, in a different key, like two people walking the same road at different speeds. Vira, who is never quiet, wasn't quiet. She was crying openly, without any sound, tears running down to her jaw.

When he finished, the room stayed silent for a long moment. Then someone said, in a whisper, "Again?"

He played it again. Then another. A song about a river. A song about a girl at a gate. By midnight we were all humming. Zenko, in a surprising baritone, knew every verse.

I don't know what it did for anyone else. For me it was as if someone had opened a door in a cold house and let in a room full of warm air. For three-quarters of an hour, nobody was a soldier. We were people in a kitchen, singing, with a guitar that had a crack down its middle.

Taras left at one. He left the guitar. "It likes it here," he said.

# Day 25 — Notes: Music at the Front

A short and faintly nostalgic playlist, in the order of requests.

- **Lullabies.** More than you'd expect. Men ask for them without shame.
- **Old folk songs.** About rivers, girls, orchards, soldiers going away and coming back.
- **Rock from the nineties.** A strange comfort. Everyone knows the words.
- **New songs written in the war.** Short and angry and tender. Some are heartbreaking.
- **Anything with a good beat.** For the long walks. For the digging.
- **Silence.** Underrated. I've learned to ask for that, too.

**Why music works:**
1. It's rhythm. The body knows rhythm.
2. It's shared. You can't hum alone for long.
3. It's old. A song carries a hundred years inside it, and that's a lot of company.
4. It carries things we can't say.

I used to record sound for a living, and I thought I knew what music did. I didn't. I've now watched a three-string guitar in a basement make nine tired men cry, and laugh, and then sleep.

What's the song you'd request first?
