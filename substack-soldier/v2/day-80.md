# Day 80 — Dispatch: Olha

**Bottom line:** A psychologist told us our bodies learned a job, and Halyna finally talked.

A psychologist visited, a gentle slightly rumpled woman called Olha, and I almost didn't go.

The invitation was a notice on the wall: *Voluntary conversations with a specialist. Confidential. Tea provided.* The last word did it. I'm not proud. Yaroslav, Halyna and I trooped over one gray afternoon to a small borrowed room behind the command post, three people on plastic chairs with our hands between our knees like children outside a headmaster's door.

Olha didn't have a clipboard. She had a kettle and a plate of biscuits. She sat on a crate slightly lower than us. "I'm not going to ask what happened. I'm going to ask what you've noticed."

Nobody spoke for a long while. A biscuit was eaten with great concentration.

Then Yaroslav, in a very small voice: "I can't stop checking the door."

"How long?"

"Since the first week."

"That makes sense. Your body's learned a job. It hasn't been told the job's changed."

She said it so simply that something in my chest loosened. Not *you're weak,* not *you're broken.* *Your body's learned a job.* Halyna said, very flatly, that she'd stopped being able to sleep without counting her kit. I said I'd stopped being able to hear a bass line without wanting to leave the room. A career built on low frequencies, and now a vibration in the chest could end my evening.

"What do you do when it happens?"

"Walk away."

"Does it help?"

"A bit."

"Good. You've already got one tool. I'll give you two more."

Her tools were small. Breathing out longer than in. Naming five things you can see. A cold hand on the back of the neck. Telling someone, even just *I'm having a bad minute.* Nothing grand. But there was something calming in the way she laid them out one by one like a seamstress arranging needles.

I told her about the counting, in the corridor at home. How Katya had said I frightened her. Olha listened. Then she said, "Does your wife know why?"

"No."

"Would you like her to?"

I hadn't known I wanted to. "Yes," I said.

"Then that's your homework."

Halyna talked last. I won't say what she said. She talked for forty minutes and Olha didn't write anything, and that at the end Halyna was crying and Olha put a hand on her shoulder and said, "You've been carrying everyone's feet for six months. Nobody's been counting yours."

Walking back we said nothing. At the dugout door Yaroslav stopped. "I thought it'd be worse."

"What?"

"Asking."

Motor, who'd been listening from the steps, grunted. "Next time I'll come."

# Day 80 — Notes: A Place to Say It

**Bottom line:** If you can, pay for an hour with a counselor.

Olha says most of the people she sees aren't soldiers. They're mothers, teachers, bus drivers. The line queues up outside her room, and nobody asks for a medal; they ask for sleep.

If you can pay for one hour with a counselor in a town like this, do it.
