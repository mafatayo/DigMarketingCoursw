*A work of fiction inspired by the accounts of Ukrainian soldiers.*

# Day 20 — Dispatch: The Old Man

A few kilometers back, there's a village, and we got half a day to help.

A single road, a church with a dented dome, a shop with its shutters half down. About a third of the houses had people in them. The rest had plywood windows, or none, and wild gardens. A dead gray sunflower leaned against one door like a man at a wall.

The job was ordinary: carry wood, fix a fence, help an old man move his things from one room to another because the ceiling had come down in the first. I carried an armchair with Bohdan. It was heavier than it looked, stuffed with horsehair, and smelled of lavender and cats.

His name was Pavlo. Eighty-one, tall, bent, hands like bundles of roots. He directed us in a voice used to commanding oxen. "Not there, boy. There. Careful, that was my mother's."

"Sorry, grandfather."

"Don't be sorry, be accurate."

I liked him at once.

When we'd finished, he sat us down in his kitchen, which had no window glass, just a plastic sheet that breathed in and out with the wind. He put out walnuts, a bottle of something clear, dried apples. "Now. Tell me you've eaten."

I asked him, awkwardly, why he'd stayed. The whole street had left or half-left. He took a long time. Then: "Where would I go? My wife is in that cemetery. My father is in that field. I know every tree on this road. If I leave, who tells the dog where I've gone?" There was a dog, old, gray-muzzled, under the table, who thumped his tail at the word.

"You think it's courage," he said, as if reading my face. "It's laziness. Packing is hard."

Bohdan laughed so hard he had to put down his walnut.

I want to be careful here, because Pavlo wasn't funny the whole time. He told us, in passing, that there'd been twenty-six families on the road last spring. There were eleven now. He said the number the way Motor says things, flat, no weight on it. The ones who'd gone had gone in different ways, and he named a couple. A woman who'd been hit by a piece of something in her own yard, hanging washing. A boy who'd been in the wrong car. He took a walnut, cracked it in one hand, and gave me half.

When we got up to go, his daughter's picture was on the wall by the door, in a plastic frame. He didn't mention her. I noticed that the glass was clean and the rest of the wall was not.

On the walk back none of us said much. Bohdan, ahead of me, was carrying a jar of pickled tomatoes Pavlo had pressed on him, in both arms, like a baby. At the edge of the village, I looked back, and the old man was standing in his gate with the dog, one hand up, not waving. Just up, like a man checking the weather.

# Day 20 — Notes: Who Stays

Age. Roots, graves, orchards. Animals; cows don't evacuate, somebody has to milk. Stubbornness. No money, no place, no one. And a kind of quiet defiance: *you'll have to carry me.*

They give us walnuts and apples and directions and gossip. They don't want pity.

The drivers who bring them food and medicine are mostly retired teachers. I've seen one hand a woman a sack of flour and wait while she checked every inch of it. That's the war too.
