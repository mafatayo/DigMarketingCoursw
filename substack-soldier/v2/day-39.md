# Day 39 — Dispatch: Zenko

**Bottom line:** Zenko was pulled from his classroom, and he turns out to be good at calming the frightened.

Zenko told me his story tonight, and I'll tell it back slowly, because he deserves it.

He doesn't talk about himself. He'll talk about the Cossacks, the Treaty of Pereiaslav, the history of the potato (he has a lecture), but not himself. Tonight we were both on late watch on a pair of crates under a sky of stars, and the silence ran long, and he began.

"I taught for nineteen years. History, in a school with a leaky roof. Thirty-one fourteen-year-olds, whom I adored and who, I'm sure, found me ridiculous."

"I'm sure you were wonderful."

"I was adequate. They liked my stories." He adjusted a scarf. "I always thought history was something that happened to other people. In books. I taught it with an affectionate distance, like weather in another country."

He paused.

"The man came on a Wednesday in the middle of a lesson on the Hetmanate. Very polite. Stood at my door and asked if he could speak to me in the corridor. I remember telling the class to keep reading. I remember a boy in the back row, Oleksiy, who never did the homework, looking at me as if he already knew."

"What happened to the class?"

"Another teacher took them. I didn't get to say goodbye properly. I wrote them a letter. It was an awful letter. I put in a quote from Plutarch I'm now fairly sure I invented." He laughed softly. "I told them that history isn't a thing you study, it's a thing you stand inside. I think I was trying to explain it to myself."

The stars turned.

"I was afraid," he said. "That's the truth. I'm not young. I wear glasses. I'd never lifted anything heavier than a stack of essays. I thought, they've made a mistake. They'll notice in an hour and send me home."

"They didn't."

"No." Sidelong smile. "And do you know what I found? I'm good at one thing here. Explaining. The young ones come in terrified and I sit with them and say: here is how it works, here is why, here is what's normal. It turns out a history teacher is useful to the frightened. We're very good at saying: this has happened before, and people survived."

He turned. His glasses caught the starlight.

"I used to teach children about the past. Now I teach soldiers about the next hour. It's the same job. Only the classroom has changed."

I couldn't answer. So I did what we do. I poured him tea from my thermos and put it in his hands.

We sat a while longer. Then he said, "Some of the ones I taught are out here. I recognized a name on a list last month. A boy from the third row. I don't know where he is. I look at the lists." He took off his glasses and cleaned them. "I never know what I'm hoping to see. Isn't that strange?"

# Day 39 — Notes: Empty Classrooms

**Bottom line:** Empty classrooms, with chairs still on the tables.

Every school in the district has desks with nobody at them. Some have chairs set neatly on the tables the way they're left at the end of a day, and the day never ended.

Zenko says he dreams of the roll call. He reads the names in his head. He's afraid he'll hear one of them answer.

*Dispatches from the Line is a work of fiction.*
