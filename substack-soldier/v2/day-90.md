*A work of fiction inspired by the accounts of Ukrainian soldiers.*

# Day 90 — Dispatch: Four Bars

It's early. The kettle's on. There's a thin silver light in the east and a lark has already begun, going up and up into a sky the color of the inside of a shell.

I'm on the sandbags with the notebook on my knee. The Colonel's beside me, gray at the muzzle now, or maybe I'm only noticing. His head is on my boot. Behind me, through the open door, I can hear the dugout waking: Motor's cough, Vira muttering about the radio, a spoon on a mug. A lamp clicks on. Somebody laughs.

I'd imagined a big ending. A summing-up, a line you could underline. I've been composing it for weeks in the dark on watch. I find I don't have one.

I have a kettle and a dog and a list of names. A rabbit in my breast pocket and a small wooden fox from a sergeant. A tin of letters to the future taped with pink. A mug with a cartoon cat. A bridge pinned to a wall.

Zenko's out of the ward. He called last night, on the good line, and the first thing he said was "Did anyone feed the Colonel?" and the second was "I have a granddaughter," and the third was a very long silence in which Vira, next to me, could be heard breathing. He's walking with a stick. He says it's very distinguished.

Maksym's being sent east to a different post. He'll have a desk and an arm that works about half. He wrote us a letter. Halyna read it out. The last line was: *Tell Sergeant Motor that I carried one bucket at a time.*

Yaroslav came up the steps this morning with a towel round his neck. He'd shaved. Badly, with a nick on his chin. He looked about nineteen and about forty.

"Whose razor?" said Vira, from the door.

He didn't answer. He lifted his chin. Motor, behind her, who'd taken it down from the shelf the night before and put it on Yaroslav's bunk without a word, just looked at him.

"It's heavier than I expected," said Yaroslav.

"They are," said Motor.

I opened the green notebook, the one with the sorries, last week, and turned to the last blank page. Here's what I wrote: the date and, under it, in my own hand, not neatly: *Not lost.* Then I drew a small fox. I'm not sure what it means. I think it means somebody should keep counting.

I've been more afraid than I've ever been. I've also been more loved. I didn't expect the second.

It isn't over. I won't tell you it is. There's more cold coming and more mud and letters I'll be afraid to open. People I love will be tired, and people I haven't met yet will need tea. The war doesn't end because a count reaches ninety.

But I'm here, and so are you. Thank you. You've been the stranger with the letter, the grandmother with the pickles, the girl with the blue paper heart. You've been the woman who drove seven hours. You've been, I'd like to think, the bakery smell on the morning I left.

Motor has come up the steps. He stands beside me with his hands in his pockets, looking east. He doesn't say anything. After a while he starts to hum.

Four bars. Up, up, down. A long note that doesn't resolve.

And then, to my astonishment, he doesn't stop. A fifth. A sixth. The whole tune, low and rough, a woman at the end of a long road, an evening coming, a thermos. I don't look at him. I don't dare. The sun comes up while he sings. The lark goes up.

I just listen.

# Day 90 — Notes: Not an Ending

Put the kettle on. Fill it to the line. Whoever empties it fills it. Look after the person next to you. Say the names. Listen under the first sound.

Be gentle. It's very easy. It's very rare.

*Dyakuyu.*

— Lys
