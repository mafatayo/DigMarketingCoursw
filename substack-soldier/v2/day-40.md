*A work of fiction inspired by the accounts of Ukrainian soldiers.*

# Day 40 — Dispatch: Magpie

A man came through last week from a staff office, a clean pink well-fed sort of man in a very neat jacket. He walked in, looked round, and addressed Motor. "Sergeant, where's your radio operator?"

"There," said Motor, pointing.

The man looked at Vira. Then back at Motor. "No, I mean the *radio operator.*"

"That's what I said."

A pause. Vira, without turning round, took her headset off one ear and said pleasantly, "Good morning. I'm Magpie. What's your question?"

He flushed. He asked. She answered in four sentences, correctly, and added a fifth that fixed an error in his own paperwork. He left like a man who'd stepped on a rake.

"Did you enjoy that?" I asked her later.

"Enormously. Highlight of my month."

But I want you to know the rest, the long dull part. Vira's work is ten, twelve hours a day listening to static and voices, catching what matters, passing it on, never missing a word. Last night the net went to hell. Their jamming got heavier, and the channels dissolved into a rushing roar, and she sat for four hours with her eyes shut, headset pressed to one ear, shifting frequencies by hand, picking out our own voices from the noise like threads from a rug. When someone in the next position called, thin and broken, with a casualty, she was the one who got it through. I watched her do it. Her lips were white. She didn't blink for what felt like minutes.

When it was over she put her head down on the set. Just for a moment. Then she lifted it, said, "Water," and Motor was there with a mug.

Halyna's work is worse in a different way. She carries more than any of us, medically and otherwise, and complains of nothing. I've watched her sew a torn boot at midnight, treat a sprain at two, cook breakfast at six.

Neither of them is doing it for recognition. They're doing it because they're good, and somebody has to.

I asked Vira once what she'd say to anyone who thought women didn't belong here. She chewed on the end of a pen.

"I'd hand them the headset. Twenty minutes. Then we'd talk."

"You wouldn't argue?"

"No. Arguing is for people with time. I have a radio."

Respect here isn't built from speeches. It's built from doing the job and doing it well and being the one who's still at the set when everyone else has gone to sleep. Eventually even the pink man in the neat jacket notices.

I notice something else, too. She's the only person in the dugout who hasn't cried since I arrived. Not once. I think she's saving it, and I think she knows exactly how much.

# Day 40 — Notes: Earned

It's the same for everyone: show up, be competent, be kind, be honest. The loudest people here are often the least trusted.

It's harder for some. They have to do it twice. We all know it. We say "that's her job, ask her," and mean it.
