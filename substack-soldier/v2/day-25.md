*A work of fiction inspired by the accounts of Ukrainian soldiers.*

# Day 25 — Dispatch: Three Strings

Somebody found a guitar in the ruins of a house. Three strings, a crack down the soundboard, a faded cartoon sun sticker on the back. They carried it in like a rescued puppy.

Nobody could play. Bohdan tried a chord and produced a sound like a door falling downstairs. "It's a very sad guitar," said Vira. "It has a soul," said Zenko. "It has three strings." "A soul and three strings. Most of us have less."

For two days it sat in the corner, looked at but not touched, like a relative nobody wanted to be first to greet. Then, last night around ten, a quiet man called Taras walked in from the next dugout. Tall, gentle, sleepy-eyed, in supply, hadn't said ten words to me in three weeks. He saw the guitar and stopped.

"May I?"

Nobody breathed. He sat on a crate with the guitar across his knee and tuned the three strings by ear to something no music teacher would call correct. He listened, turned a peg, listened again. Then he played.

It wasn't skilled. It was a handful of notes, slow, repeating, like a man crossing a river on three stones. But I knew the song after a moment. An old lullaby. My grandmother sang it. It has hardly any chords, mostly melody, and three strings turned out to be exactly enough.

Then he sang, low and a little hoarse, almost spoken. The dugout went entirely still. The Colonel lifted his head off my boot. Halyna had both hands over her mouth. Bohdan's eyes were closed. Motor's four bars hummed along under Taras's voice, in a different key, two men walking the same road at different speeds. And Vira, who's never quiet, was crying with no sound at all.

When he finished, nobody said anything until the last note stopped ringing. Then someone whispered "Again?" and he played it again. And another. A song about a river. A song about a girl at a gate. By midnight we were all humming. Zenko, in a surprising baritone, knew every verse.

I don't know what it did for everyone else. For me it was a door opening in a cold house. For three-quarters of an hour nobody was a soldier. We were people in a kitchen, with a guitar that had a crack down its middle.

Taras left at one. He left the guitar. "It likes it here."

Walking him to the steps, I asked if he played often. He took a while. "Not since the spring," he said. "I had a friend in the platoon. He played. I haven't touched one since." He looked at the sky. "It felt like it might be time."

That was all he said. I didn't ask what happened to the friend. You don't.

# Day 25 — Notes: What We Sing

Lullabies, more than you'd think. Men ask for them without shame. Old folk songs about rivers and girls and orchards and someone going away. Nineties rock, everyone knows the words. Newer songs written in the war, short, angry, tender. Some break your heart. Anything with a beat for the long walks.

Silence is underrated. I've started asking for that too.
