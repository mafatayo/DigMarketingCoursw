*A work of fiction inspired by the accounts of Ukrainian soldiers.*

# Day 18 — Dispatch: Yaroslav

It rained for three days, the thin gray kind that doesn't storm, that just continues, and by the second day we'd all stopped talking about it, the way you stop talking about a relative who's overstayed.

The roof leaks into three pots. Plink, plink, plonk, three different notes. Bohdan says we could start a band. "The Wet Orchestra," said Zenko. "Opus one in a flat."

On the third evening the blanket over the door lifted and a boy came in with water running off his helmet. Nineteen, I'd have said, if he'd given me the chance to guess. Ears sticking out. A thin neck he kept tucking into his collar. A bag too big for him, and boots too new.

He saluted. Nobody here salutes. He realized this about half a second after he'd done it and let his hand fall, very slowly, like a man lowering something heavy.

"Yaroslav," he said. "Replacement. I was told to report to Sergeant Motor."

"Sit," said Motor. Then, after a look: "Eaten?"

"Not since the morning."

Halyna had a bowl in front of him before he'd finished the sentence. He ate fast, shoulders hunched, both hands around it as if somebody might take it away. "Slow down," said Halyna. "It isn't going anywhere." He slowed.

I knew the look. I'd worn it three weeks ago. It's terror and hope and a strong wish to be invisible.

The question nobody had asked was where he'd sleep. There was one bunk. I watched the thought cross every face at once. Vira looked at Motor. Motor looked at the shelf, where the razor lay at its precise angle.

It took him a long time. I could see him working it out, the way you'd see a man balancing a very full bucket.

"Lowest bunk," Motor said finally. "That one."

"Whose is it?" said Yaroslav, because he's not stupid.

"It's yours."

"But it's—there are things on the—"

"The razor stays," said Motor. "The bunk's yours. You're on the same side of the wall, now. Get some sleep."

I don't think he understood fully. He nodded and lay down on the blanket, in his wet boots, because someone had told him on the way in that you sleep with your boots on, and he looked at the underside of my bunk, a foot from his face, with the expression of a man waking up inside a story he hadn't read.

An hour later, I heard him whisper. "Lys?"

"Yes?"

"Is it always this loud?"

I listened. A distant thud. The stove ticking. Motor's four bars, soft.

"This isn't loud," I said. "This is Tuesday."

I heard a small surprised laugh in the dark, and then, after a long while, the breathing changed.

I lay there awake. I want to say something about replacements, about how a unit closes round a gap like water. But what I thought was: *someone made a phone call to send a nineteen-year-old to a hole in the ground, and this is what it looks like when it arrives.* It looks like a boy with a salute and a very clean pair of boots.

# Day 18 — Notes: New

Feed him first. Tell him it's okay to be scared, early. Teach him the rules slowly, one at a time. Give him a small job right away. Sit next to him. Don't lie about how bad it is, he'll know.

I was told once that nobody becomes a soldier in a day. It takes a hundred small kindnesses. Mine came from a woman with a mug.
