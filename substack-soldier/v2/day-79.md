# Day 79 — Dispatch: Listening Under

**Bottom line:** I taught Yaroslav to listen, he heard a threat first, and I'm afraid of what that costs him.

Yaroslav asked me to teach him to listen.

A Sunday evening, rainy, long gray light, the two of us on the steps with a bag of sunflower seeds. He'd been quiet all day. I'd learned to read it, the particular dip of the shoulders when he was working up to something. I waited, cracking seeds, spitting husks into the mud.

"Lys. You can tell what's coming before the rest of us. How?"

"It's the job."

"No. It's not only that. Vira can, a bit. Bohdan—" He stopped on the name. Started again. "Bohdan could. In a different way. You do it like a trick."

"It isn't a trick." I thought about it. "It's patience. And a lot of mistakes."

"Can you teach it?"

I'd never been asked. I'd learned it the long way, in a studio with headphones, a thousand hours of nothing until I could hear the hum in a wall. But I looked at his face, young and serious and a little scared, and said, "Close your eyes."

"Now?"

"Yes. Tell me everything you hear."

He did. He sat on the step with his eyes shut and hands loose on his knees. After a minute he said slowly: "Rain. Dripping. Someone coughing far off. The stove. A truck, a long way away. A bird."

"What kind?"

"I don't know."

"Listen again."

"A small one. High. It goes up at the end."

"That's a lark. It's the third. The other two are nearer. Do you hear?"

He frowned. Then his eyes opened. "I do. There are three."

"That's all it is. You listen for what's under the first layer. Then under the next. Eventually you stop being surprised."

We did it every evening after that. Ten minutes, eyes closed, naming what we heard. He got better quickly, faster than I had. After a week he caught something I'd missed, a faint regular tapping on a far roof. "How did you hear that?" I said, genuinely amazed.

"I listened under," he said, and grinned.

Then, last night, he caught something else. We were on the steps with our eyes shut and he went very still. "Lys," he whispered. "There's a bird."

"Yes. The lark."

"No. A different kind. Low. A long way off." His eyes opened. "Not a bird."

I listened. He was right. I'd have missed it. Thin, steady, a good distance away, coming slowly nearer on a line I couldn't place. "Inside," I said. "Now. Tell Motor."

We were down the steps in four seconds. Vira had the net up before we'd sat. It was only a patrol. It went past. Nothing happened. But Yaroslav stood in the dugout with his hands pressed flat on the crate, very white, and said, "I heard it first."

"You did."

"Before you."

"By two seconds."

He nodded. He was shaking and his eyes were bright, and I knew with a cold dry certainty that I'd just taught a boy to hear the thing that would be coming for him.

I looked at Motor. Motor looked at me. Neither of us said it. I'd made him better at being the one who sees first, and I knew exactly what that costs, because I'd watched what it cost Bohdan.

# Day 79 — Notes: Teaching Children to Listen

**Bottom line:** Mothers teach their children to tell a plane from a drone.

Mothers here teach their children to hear the difference between a plane and a drone, between a bang and a thunderclap. It's a skill. It's the one thing I'd take back from the world if I could.

I taught a nineteen-year-old to do it this month. He's good. I'm not proud.
