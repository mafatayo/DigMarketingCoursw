# Day 42 — Dispatch: Dark

It happened at 6:14 p.m., I know because Vira was looking at the clock when the lamp went out.

Not just ours. Everything. The generator coughed, shuddered, died, and from far beyond the walls came a long falling hum as the whole region exhaled and stopped. Then silence, and a darkness so total that for a moment I couldn't tell if my eyes were open.

"Don't move," said Motor calmly. "Everyone stay where you are. Hands in front."

"I'm not moving," said Bohdan. "I'm standing very still and screaming."

"You're not screaming."

"Quietly. Inside."

A match flared. Halyna, lighting a candle stub, steady, and the room swam back, yellow and trembling. Seven faces, huge shadows. The Colonel wide-eyed against my knee.

The grid was gone, we found out an hour later from the radio. The big power stations had been hit again overnight, a series of strikes across half the country, and the lines to the whole region had dropped. I thought of Katya at once, and then of Sonya, and then of my mother in a hospital with a generator that had to hold. I thought of the corridor, no windows, no light.

I expected panic. I got something stranger. A hush, a settling, as if the dugout had been waiting for permission to slow down. Without lamps, without screens, without the glow of Bohdan's bench, we sat in a small circle round the stove and the candle and found ourselves talking.

Zenko told how his grandfather once walked to Kyiv to find his brother. Vira sang a children's song about a sleepy bear. Yaroslav, who'd barely spoken in three days, told us about the farm where he grew up and a cow called Malvina who "was cleverer than most people I know." We laughed so hard Motor had to hush us.

It was the best night in weeks, and here's what's under it. Every one of us had somebody on the other end of that dead line. In the pauses I could see it. Each man had a face turned slightly toward a place he couldn't reach, and a number he'd try to call once the bar came back.

I stepped outside. The dark was colossal. No light from any village, no glow on the horizon. Just a field of stars, thousands, close enough to touch. The Milky Way hung overhead like spilled milk. Behind me, through the door, a warm low murmur of voices.

This is what the world looked like for most of history, I thought. Dark, cold, people talking by a fire.

Then I thought of the strikes, and who had ordered them, and what for, and that someone, tonight, was sitting in a room with a light on and a plan, and had decided that this was how you win. I stood there with my head back and I hated them, very cleanly, for about a minute. It didn't last. It never does. But I wanted it written down.

# Day 42 — Notes: A Cold Night

When the grid fails, the hospitals switch to generators and the generators have fuel for as long as they have fuel. Everyone in the country knows the number of hours. People send each other the number.

At two a.m. in a city I love there are old people lying in the dark, in their coats, listening to a fridge that isn't running.
