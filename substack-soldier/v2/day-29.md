*A work of fiction inspired by the accounts of Ukrainian soldiers.*

# Day 29 — Dispatch: A Blue Heart

I got a letter today that wasn't for me.

It came in a bundle of cards from a school abroad, hundreds, children's, in many languages, glittering with glue. Vira sorted them on the table trying to share them evenly. Mine was a folded blue paper with a lopsided heart on the front.

Inside, in careful uneven English: *Dear soldier. I am Maria. I am eight. I live in a small town in another country. My teacher told us about your country. I am sorry about the war. I drew you a flower. I hope you are warm. I hope you have a dog. Love, Maria.*

Under the words: an orange flower with a face. A dog, a circle with legs and a long smiling mouth. And beside the dog, a stick figure in a hat, labeled, in small letters, YOU.

I read it three times. My English is movie English, I follow it, I don't speak it. It didn't matter. A letter from a stranger who owes you nothing is lighter than one from home, which carries love and worry and history. It says only: *someone out there thought of you.* A thin clear message. It went through me like a drink of water.

I showed it to the Colonel. I know that's absurd. I held it up and said, "She drew you." He sniffed it thoroughly, with the air of a man inspecting a contract, and licked the flower. I'm counting that as a reply.

That night I wrote back, in bad English, with Bohdan checking spelling. *Dear Maria. Thank you for your letter. I am warm. I have a dog. His name is Colonel. He is very important. Your flower is on my wall. Please be kind to everyone. Please play outside. The sun is good. Love, Lys.* I drew a fox beside the dog.

Then I stopped, with the pen over the page, because I realized I'd written *I am warm* and it was a lie. I was sitting in a hole with the stove out because Vira's schedule said no smoke. I was wearing four layers. I could see my breath. But I'd written *warm* because that's what she'd hoped, and it seemed like a small thing to give her.

I thought about it for a while. There's a lie you tell your wife, to carry something for her. There's a lie you tell a child, so she can keep a corner of the world soft. They're not the same lie. I decided to leave it.

Maria will never know. She'll go to school, forget, grow up, become a woman in a city I'll never see, and somewhere in a pocket of a man in a hole, a blue heart will still be folded.

# Day 29 — Notes: Letters

If you write: keep it short. Be kind, not pitying. Ask a question. Add a drawing, even a bad one. Don't ask about the war. Ask about the dog.

Every card goes on the wall. We have a wall now. It's the only decoration in the dugout, and it's the warmest part of it.
