*A work of fiction inspired by the accounts of Ukrainian soldiers.*

# Day 76 — Dispatch: What the Thaw Gives Back

The mud came back and it had brought friends.

If the first mud was a lesson, this was a doctorate. The thaw loosened the world all at once. Frozen ruts turned to soup. Paths became canals. The trench floor, firm all winter, went to a thick brown porridge that took your boot and kept it. Yaroslav lost two in one morning and stood in the track on one leg like a statue of Defeat.

"Welcome to spring," said Vira from the steps with a mug.

"Help."

"No."

"*Please.*"

"I'm saving my strength for the laughing."

It took three of us to get him out. By the end we all wore a thin brown coat to the knee and none of us could speak for laughter. Motor arrived to see what the noise was, said "Disgraceful," and sat down on a crate and shook with silent mirth. That's the first time I'd seen him laugh since the winter.

It's remarkable what mud does to dignity. It levels everyone. I've seen generals on inspection with splendid polished boots buried to the ankle, standing in a field like men who've seen their own funeral.

But I'll tell you the other thing, because I've promised.

The thaw also gives back what the winter hid.

Last Thursday, on a carrying detail, Zenko and I walked past a place where the snow had gone, at the edge of a field, and I saw a boot. Just a boot. Then, further on, a shape under the matted grass that I took, at first, for a bundle of clothes. We stopped. Zenko took off his glasses and put them on again and said, "Lys. Don't go closer."

I didn't. I stood where I was. I looked at the horizon instead.

"We'll mark it," said Zenko. "And we'll tell someone. There are people whose job it is." He was shaking, I noticed, though his voice was steady. "He's been there since the autumn, I think."

"Whose?"

"I couldn't tell you." He folded his hands. "I'd rather not know."

We marked it with a stake and a strip of red cloth. We walked back in silence. That night Halyna told us that there's a team for this, volunteers mostly, who come in spring when the ground opens and go out with stretchers and notebooks and try to give people their names. "It's what happens after," she said. "It's the part nobody puts in the news."

She'd gone very quiet. When I asked, she said that every spring she thinks of the ones from the first year, the ones they never found, who are still out there somewhere, in somebody's field, waiting to be thawed.

"Come in," I said. "Tea."

"Yes," she said, finally. "Tea."

# Day 76 — Notes: Mud Season

Gaiters from old trousers. Plastic bags over socks, inside the boot. Planks. Walk like a heron. Clean your boots at every door; you'll fail.

Laugh. It's the only thing that doesn't stick.
