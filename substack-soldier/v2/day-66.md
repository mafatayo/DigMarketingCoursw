# Day 66 — Dispatch: The Third Stair

**Bottom line:** I got home; the third stair still creaks, and my daughter keeps a packed go-bag by the door.

I'd rehearsed a dignified entrance on the train. Shoulders back, steady voice. Instead I climbed four flights with my bag banging my knees and stopped on the landing to catch my breath, and put my foot on the third step from the top, and it creaked.

I've no idea why that was what did it. The creak, the cold painted rail, the smell of floor polish and someone's boiled cabbage, which I'd have said I hated. I stood in the stairwell and cried with one hand flat on the wall.

A door opened above. "Papa?"

She was at the top in her socks. Green jumper, two uneven plaits, one coming down. She stood there with her hands at her sides like she'd been shot, and then she ran, and jumped from the bottom step the way she always does, to make it creak on purpose, and hit me in the chest with her whole weight. I dropped the bag.

"You're scratchy," she said into my neck.

"I know."

"You smell weird."

"I know."

"Don't go."

I didn't answer that. Over her head Katya was in the doorway in the gray sweater with her hand flat against the frame as if the flat might fall down, and she didn't run. She waited. When I looked up she took a step, and another, and put her forehead against my jaw, round both of us.

"You're home."

"Seven days."

"You're *home,*" she said fiercely. "Shut up."

I don't remember the first hour. A kitchen. A bath she ran hotter than I'd have dared want, and I sat in it forty minutes and heard through the wall two people moving about a flat, a kettle, a cupboard, Sonya explaining something to the cat. The loudest silence of my life.

Afterward I looked at the kitchen window. The crack was still there, a long diagonal, taped over with neat strips of tape that had started to yellow. Beside it on the glass someone had drawn a small sun with a face, in marker. Sonya, obviously. She'd decided the crack needed company.

And the corridor. The mattress folded against the wall. A flashlight on a hook at child height. A small backpack by the door, packed, with a rabbit's ear sticking out of the top.

"She keeps it ready," Katya said behind me. "It's a game. She says it's a go-bag. Like Papa's."

**Tactical read.** Go-bags and corridor routines are home-front hardening, a social adaptation to sustained strikes.

**OPSEC.** Anything that could identify a place, a time or a person's unit has been changed.

# Day 66 — Notes: Go-Bags

**Bottom line:** Children's go-bags exist; think about that.

Children's go-bags. A little backpack by the door with a toy, a bottle of water, a card with their name and a phone number written in marker, in case.

Think of the person who thought of that. Think of what it takes.
