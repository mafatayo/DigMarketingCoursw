*A work of fiction inspired by the accounts of Ukrainian soldiers.*

# Day 66 — Dispatch: The Third Stair

The third stair still creaks.

That was the first thing. I'd planned to be dignified. I'd rehearsed on the train a calm strong entrance, shoulders back, steady voice. Instead I climbed four flights with my bag banging my knees, stopped on the landing to breathe, and put my foot on the third step from the top. It creaked. The exact tired complaint I'd carried in my head for months.

Something broke open. I stood in the stairwell and wept, one hand on the cold painted rail, the smell all round me, boiled cabbage and floor polish and somebody's coffee.

A door opened above. "Papa?"

She was at the top of the stairs in her socks. A small figure in a green jumper, hair in two uneven plaits, one slipping. She stood there as if she'd been shot, hands at her sides, eyes huge. Then she ran. She jumped from the bottom step, the way she always does, to make it creak on purpose, and hit me in the chest with her whole weight. I dropped the bag and then I was holding her and she was holding me and neither of us could breathe.

"You're scratchy," she said into my neck.

"I know."

"You smell weird."

"I know."

"Don't go."

I couldn't answer. Over her shoulder Katya stood in the doorway in her gray sweater, one hand flat against the frame as if the flat might fall down. She didn't run. She waited. When I finally looked up she took one step and another, and then she put her arms round the two of us and her forehead against my jaw.

"You're home."

"Seven days."

"You're *home,*" she said fiercely. "Shut up."

I don't remember the first hour. A kitchen. A bath she ran for me, hotter than I'd allowed myself to want. I sat in it forty minutes in the quiet, hearing through the wall two people moving about a flat, a kettle, a cupboard, Sonya telling the cat something. It was the loudest silence of my life.

I looked at the kitchen window afterward. The crack was still there, a long diagonal line taped over with neat strips of tape, the tape yellowing. Beside it, on the glass, someone had drawn with a marker a small sun with a face. Sonya, obviously. She'd decided the crack needed company.

I looked at the corridor too. The mattress was folded against the wall. A flashlight on a hook at child height. A small backpack by the door, packed, with a rabbit's ear sticking out of the top.

"She keeps it ready," Katya said, behind me. "It's a game. She says it's a go-bag, like Papa's."

I couldn't sleep that night. The mattress was too soft, the room too quiet. No stove. No snoring. No dog. At three I got up and sat on the kitchen floor with my back to the fridge, and a small hand slid into mine. Sonya, in her nightdress, silent. She sat down beside me and put her head on my arm and we stayed there until morning.

# Day 66 — Notes: Quiet

Doors: I kept closing them, then not wanting to. Hot water, forever, on demand; I wept. Light, so much light. A shop shelf stacked with food, and I stood there ten minutes. People who look fine, which is alarming. Time in a straight line.

This is why. This room. This soft ordinary enormous peace. And a go-bag by the door.
