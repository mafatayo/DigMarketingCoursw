# Day 6 — Dispatch: The Scarf

The boy who brought the mail was wearing the ugliest scarf I've ever seen, orange and brown, with a fringe, knitted by someone who clearly had strong views on color and none on length. It went round his neck about four times and he kept getting it caught in the truck door. I'm starting with the scarf because that's what I looked at for the first minute. I was trying not to look at the bag.

Mail comes when a truck comes, and a truck comes when somebody decides the track is survivable, so the whole dugout lives in a low hum of maybe. This morning Vira stopped in the middle of a story about her cousin's wedding. Head tilted, like a dog. Then I heard it too, an engine grinding down the track with its lights off. Everyone pretended not to listen. Nobody pretended well.

He read the names off a list in a bored voice. Zenko. Vira. Bohdan, two for Bohdan. Halyna. Motor. And then a gap while he turned the page and I stopped hearing anything at all, and then, "Lys."

A padded envelope, soft at the corners from handling. Katya's handwriting, which slants hard to the right when she's in a hurry. I held it for a bit before opening it, long enough that Zenko said "it won't bite, my friend."

Inside: socks, gray, wool, much too thick, which I put on at once. A chocolate bar already snapped in half. A drawing from Sonya, a house, a tree, a big stick man with enormous hands, and a sun with a face. The man had a mustache. I don't have a mustache. Katya says Sonya has decided all soldiers have them because all the ones on the news do. There was a small crooked heart on the man's chest, in red, and then the red ran out and the sun is yellow.

The letter was two pages and I read it behind the sandbags with the flashlight under my chin. The neighbor's cat had had kittens. Eggs cost more. She'd fixed the tap herself, "so you don't feel guilty about it." And then, in the middle of page two, in the same even writing, as if it were the next item after eggs: *Tuesday night there was a bad one on the next street. The kitchen window cracked, not broken. Sonya slept through. I taped it. Don't worry.*

I read that line several times. I read the rest of the letter too and couldn't tell you what it said.

Halyna was in the dugout when I came in, going through her bag, and I asked if anyone had ever had bad news by letter. "No," she said. "They phone." She said it kindly, without looking up. People who have something terrible to say can't stand to wait for paper. Letters are for the things that can wait. I turned that over for the rest of the afternoon, and I've started to think Katya knew exactly what she was doing, putting it between the eggs and the tap.

At the bottom, smaller: *I'm okay. Don't be a hero. Be home.*

I wore the socks to bed. They were far too hot. I wore them anyway.

# Day 6 — Notes: Mail Is for What Can Wait

A nurse told me that families phone with the news that can't wait, and write with the rest. So when a letter comes in a hurry, with the handwriting slanted, it's worth reading the middle.

Half the country is writing letters like that now. A tap fixed. A window taped. *I'm okay.* The hard sentence goes between the eggs and the weather.
