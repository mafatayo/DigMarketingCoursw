*A work of fiction inspired by the accounts of Ukrainian soldiers.*

# Day 6 — Dispatch: Mail

Mail comes when a truck comes, and a truck comes when somebody decides the road is survivable, so the whole dugout lives in a low hum of maybe.

Today maybe turned into yes. I knew because Vira stopped in the middle of a sentence. She'd been telling Bohdan some long story about a cousin's wedding and she just stopped, head cocked, like a dog. Then I heard it too, the grind of a vehicle coming slowly down the track with its lights off. Everyone pretended not to listen. Nobody pretended well.

The driver was about twenty, wearing a scarf that had clearly been knitted by a relative with strong views on color. He dumped two boxes and a sack on the crate and read names off a list in a bored voice.

"Zenko. Vira. Bohdan, two for Bohdan. Halyna. Motor."

A pause while he turned the page.

"Lys."

I didn't hear the rest.

A padded envelope, soft at the corners from being handled. Katya's handwriting, slanting hard to the right the way it does when she's in a hurry. I held it for a bit before opening it, long enough that Zenko said, "It won't bite, my friend."

Inside: socks. Gray, wool, too thick, I put them on at once. A chocolate bar, already snapped in half. A drawing by Sonya, a house, a tree, a big stick man with enormous hands, a yellow sun with a face. The man had a mustache. I don't have a mustache. Katya told me later that Sonya has decided all soldiers have mustaches because every soldier on the news does.

Then the letter, two pages, tiny writing. I read it outside, behind the sandbags, with the flashlight under my chin.

It wasn't about anything, mostly. The neighbor's cat had kittens. Eggs cost more. She'd fixed the dripping tap herself "so you don't feel guilty about it." And then, in the middle of page two, in the same even handwriting, as if it were a continuation of the eggs: *Tuesday night there was a bad one on the next street. The window in the kitchen cracked, not broken. Sonya slept through. I taped it. Don't worry.*

I read the line a few times. Then I read the rest and couldn't have told you what it said.

At the bottom, smaller: *I'm okay. Don't be a hero. Be home.*

When I came back down Vira had a letter open on her knee and her face turned to the wall. Bohdan held his two envelopes in both hands like they were warm. Zenko sat very straight, lips moving slightly as he read. Motor had a photograph, which he put away fast when he saw me looking.

I asked, later, whether anyone had had bad news. Halyna said, "In a letter? No." She said it kindly. She meant that the ones who write you the bad news send it quickly, on a phone, because they can't stand to wait for paper. It's a thing I hadn't known. Mail is for the stuff that can wait.

Katya taped the window and told me in the same breath as the eggs. I thought about that all through my watch. I think she put it in the middle on purpose, so I'd have to read past it.

# Day 6 — Notes: Sonya

If this reaches you, kid, I'm sorry about the handwriting. It's pencil, I'm on a crate.

The socks are on. Mama said you chose the color. They're very good. The man in your drawing has the best hands I've ever seen on a person, I'll take them.

Do what Mama says during the alerts. Go to the good wall. I know you like the other one.

Eat the vegetables. Tell Marmalade I said hello.

Papa
