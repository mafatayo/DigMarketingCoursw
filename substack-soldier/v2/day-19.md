# Day 19 — Dispatch: Rotation

**Bottom line:** A rumor of rotation raised our hopes, then died, because the units beside us had taken losses.

It started, as it always does, with a man who knew a man.

Bohdan heard it from a driver, who'd heard it from a cook, who'd heard it from "someone in the staff." Our section was to be rotated out in a week. Five days at a rear village. Beds. Showers. A roof that didn't drip into pots.

By dinner everyone had heard it. By evening everyone believed it. By midnight everyone had begun, quietly, to plan. I watched it happen and it was fascinating and a little sad. Men who hadn't smiled in a week were describing, in detail, what they'd do on the first day. Bohdan would sleep fourteen hours. Halyna would take a bath so long it would go cold twice. Zenko wanted a library. Vira wanted a bakery and a man with a guitar. Yaroslav wanted to see the river he grew up beside. Motor said nothing, but I caught him folding and refolding a clean shirt.

Near midnight he called us together. "Listen. I know nothing. Nobody's told me anything. If it's true, good. If it isn't, don't let me see you sulk."

It was hard to say, and he said it anyway, having seen the faces.

This is the cruelest thing here, I think. Not the shelling. The hope. A loose tooth you can't stop touching. A rumor of rest does something strange to the body, suddenly you notice every ache. The cold gets colder. You've been living with it all along, but now you know there's another world within reach, and the gap hurts.

Day two, nothing. Day three, nothing. On day four, a truck came, bringing ammunition and water and a young officer who looked at our hopeful faces and said, "No rotation. Maybe next month."

There was another sentence he didn't finish. The rotation had been canceled because the units on either side of us had taken losses and the line had to hold, and we were, in the language of the maps, "essential." I got that from Vira later, who'd got it from the net. She read it out like a bill.

Somebody put the kettle on.

That night I lay awake and thought about what we hadn't lost, because it had never been ours. We'd grieved it anyway, the way you grieve for a house you walked through once and almost bought.

I also thought, for the first time, about the units on either side of us. People I'd never meet, who'd taken the losses that bought us this particular quiet. It's not a thought you can do much with. You hold it a minute and put it down.

In the morning Motor handed out chores as usual. "Buckets. Boots. Firewood." Bohdan, red-eyed, looked up and half-smiled. We went to work.

Rest, I decided, is whatever you can steal from the day.

**Tactical read.** Relief cycles slip when adjacent units take losses. Commanders trade rest for line integrity, and morale pays for it.

**OPSEC.** Equipment counts, stock levels and supply routes are withheld.

# Day 19 — Notes: No Rotation

**Bottom line:** Mothers in basements have no rotation.

A soldier can be rotated out. A mother in a basement can't. She has no shift change, no leave order, no truck at six a.m. She does her watch every night, with a child's head in her lap, and nobody writes her name on a list.

I don't say this to make light of ours. I say it because the wrong people always forget that the war has a night shift.
