# Day 44 — Dispatch: Salt

**Bottom line:** Exhaustion made me put salt in my tea; we're on double watch because the neighbors are under pressure.

I put salt in my tea this morning and didn't notice until Vira stared.

"What?"

"You're drinking it."

"It's tea."

"It's salt. I watched you." She said it gently. She took the mug, sniffed it, and set it down. "Lys. When did you last sleep?"

I tried to count. Yesterday? The day before? A watch, a long carry, another watch, then Yaroslav had a nightmare and I sat up with him, then morning. Four hours in two days, maybe.

"I'm fine," I said, and forgot what I'd been about to do.

Exhaustion doesn't feel like tiredness. Tiredness is honest, it pulls your eyelids down and begs you to lie flat. This is a gray fog behind the eyes, a slow thickening of the world, where simple words slide off and decisions grow fur. You laugh at things that aren't funny. You nearly cry at the sound of a spoon. Bohdan, yesterday, tried to charge a phone with a pencil. Zenko lost his glasses and found them on his head and said, "That's where I keep them." Motor, who has never in my hearing made a mistake with a tool, put a wrench in the bread bin and stood there looking at it for a long time. We all looked at it. Nobody said anything.

The reason is simple. The neighbors on our left have been attacked three nights running. Not a big thing, the kind you can't call a battle, a probing, rounds coming in out of the dark and then going quiet again. And we're the ones who stay up for it. Every man in the section has been on double watch. The rotation has ceased to exist as an idea.

And it's dangerous. That's the truth under the comedy. A tired man misjudges a distance. A tired man misses a sound. A tired man takes the wrong path in the dark. Halyna sees this more clearly than any of us and has started enforcing sleep like a military operation. "You. Bed. You, thirty minutes. You, after tea. Do not argue with the nurse."

She sent me to my bunk at ten. I protested. She stood over me with her arms folded. "Lys, in your current state you're a threat to yourself and others. Go to sleep."

I lay down. The Colonel climbed up beside me, hot and heavy, and put his chin on my chest. Someone laid a blanket on me. I never found out who.

I slept eleven hours. I woke with a mug of tea beside my pillow, sweet, hot, correct, and nearly cried again. Slept-in people are very emotional.

I found out afterward that during those eleven hours the others had gone to the line without me, and that Yaroslav had covered my watch. Nineteen. Not a word of it was mentioned.

**Tactical read.** Sleep deprivation degrades judgment more than most equipment shortages. Fatigue errors cause losses that never appear in reports.

**OPSEC.** Positions, orientation of the line and distances are altered.

# Day 44 — Notes: Nobody Slept

**Bottom line:** The whole country is exhausted.

The tiredness isn't only in uniform. Every shelter has its zombies, people in coats on benches who haven't slept more than three hours in a month and keep going to work.

It's a national condition. I'm proud of it and I would pay everything to end it.
