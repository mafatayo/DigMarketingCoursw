# Day 58 — Dispatch: Lidiya

**Bottom line:** Lidiya, the borshch driver, learned Bohdan was gone and took off her headscarf.

The woman in the little hatchback came back, the one with the borshch, and this time I got her name.

Lidiya. Sixty-four. A retired seamstress. She turned up on a bright sharp Saturday in the same battered car with the cracked windshield, the same purple coat, a new thermos of something enormous and hot. I was ready this time. As she climbed out I stepped forward and said, "Madam, my section would like to know your name."

She looked at me with suspicion. "Why?"

"So we can thank you properly."

"You can thank me by eating."

"We'll do both."

She sighed as if conceding a point in a long trial. "Lidiya Petrivna. Don't write it down."

"I'm going to write it down."

"Then write it small."

So I wrote it in the back of the notebook, in the margin of the Names, under a heading of my own. *The Kind.* A different list.

Motor met her at the bottom of the steps bareheaded. "Mother."

"Don't mother me. You've lost weight again." She stopped. She looked at him a second longer. Then she looked at the dugout, the shelf, the faces. She counted us. I watched her count, and I watched her get to six and keep looking for a seventh.

"Where's the boy," she said. "The thin one who said sorry to the plates."

Halyna, who'd come up behind me, said, "Last week."

Lidiya put down the thermos. She didn't say anything for a while. Then she took her headscarf off, a thing I'd never seen her do, folded it in her hands, and bowed her gray head. She stood like that for a minute. Then she put the scarf back on and picked up the thermos.

"Then we'll eat for him too," she said.

We ate. Borshch again, steaming. Yaroslav, who'd never had it, took the first spoonful and went completely still. "My grandmother's," he said.

"Everybody says that."

"It's true."

"It's the dill. Everyone's grandmother used dill."

I asked, as casually as I could, why she did it. How many trips? How much of her own money? She waved me off. "It's nothing."

"It isn't nothing."

"My husband died in the first year. My sons are abroad, safe, thank God. I was alone in a house with a very large pot." She smiled. "I don't know what else to do with a very large pot."

"So you drive six hours."

"Seven, today. The bridge." She patted my arm. "I sit at home and think about you. I cook. I think, this will go in somebody's mouth. That's what a mother does. It's the same."

It was a flat, unashamed, practical love with no reward in it, and I think it's the strongest force I've met out here. It can't be shot at, it doesn't need a medal, it only needs a pot.

When she left she gave each of us a hard look and a hard pinch on the cheek, even Motor, who bore it like a statue. At the car she turned, pointed at the dugout, and said in the tone of a general: "Eat. Wash. Wear a hat."

"Yes, ma'am," said six tired people.

# Day 58 — Notes: The Other Army

**Bottom line:** If this is won, the civilian army won it.

Grandmothers, taxi drivers, bakers, students, seamstresses. They haven't been issued weapons. They've been issued a conscience, and they're using it better than most.

If this ends, history should say they won it.
