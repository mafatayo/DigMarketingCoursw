# Day 28 — Dispatch: An Envelope

**Bottom line:** Zenko's daughter is expecting; she wrote him a letter because the phone is for bad news now.

The next day it was Zenko's turn, and I watched how a man receives good news after a month of none.

He was at the table in his two scarves, small round glasses on his nose, a pencil behind his ear. The mail had come at noon. Vira handed him a thin gray envelope with a child's careful handwriting on it. He frowned at it and set it aside.

"Aren't you opening it?" said Bohdan.

"I'm saving it."

"For what?"

"For when I'm ready."

We left him alone. He went back to his book but I noticed he'd turned the same page for twenty minutes. His hand drifted to the envelope. Then away. Then back.

It was evening before he opened it. He took it up the steps into the cold, where the light was nearly gone, and through the open door I could see him in profile on the sandbags, tearing the paper carefully along the top the way you handle something you intend to keep.

He read it standing. Then he sat. Then he put his hand over his mouth, and his glasses fogged, and for a moment I thought something terrible had happened and started to rise. Vira caught my sleeve. "Wait."

He came down the steps with the letter held to his chest. His face was wet and he was grinning, a huge lopsided embarrassed grin I'd never seen on him. "My daughter," he said. "She's expecting."

The dugout exploded. Bohdan whooped. Vira threw a sock in the air. Halyna hugged Zenko so hard his glasses went sideways. Motor said from the corner, "Zenko. You're going to be a grandfather."

"I'm forty-one," said Zenko.

"Grandfather," said Motor, with immense satisfaction.

"It's—" said Zenko, and couldn't finish, and sat down. "She wrote it in a letter. She said she didn't want to tell me on the phone, because the phone is for bad news now. She wanted me to have a good one in an envelope."

I kept coming back to that afterward. The phone is for bad news. It's true. Everyone here has learned to flinch at a buzz. And a woman who knew that sat down with her pregnancy and a pen so her father could have one clean moment.

We celebrated with tea and a spoonful each of Babusya's last plum jam. Zenko gave a toast, longer than the one for Bohdan. "To the next generation. May they be bored. May they be thoroughly, magnificently bored."

I looked at him then. He was holding the envelope like a bird, with both hands, and I saw the other thing in his face, under the happiness. He'd done the sum. Seven months, maybe eight. A child coming into a city that still had sirens. And it wasn't fear exactly. It was the look of a man who's just been handed a reason, and understands what reasons cost.

**Tactical read.** Family news is a morale buffer. Communication discipline lets it reach soldiers without revealing positions.

**OPSEC.** Names of living people are altered; locations and unit details are removed.

# Day 28 — Notes: The Phone Is for Bad News

**Bottom line:** Phones mean bad news now; write a letter instead.

Everyone in this country flinches when a phone buzzes. A woman I know sets her ringtone to a bird so it won't sound like what it is.

Write someone a letter this week, a real one, with paper. Give them something to open that doesn't flinch.
