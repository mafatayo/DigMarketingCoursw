# Day 35 — Dispatch: Evacuation

**Bottom line:** We evacuated a wounded man by night, and he died in the van while Halyna kept talking to him.

I'll tell this one carefully, and leave out everything that could be used. What I can give you is how it felt.

It was past midnight, cold enough that the metal stung, the sky thick with stars, the dugout dark except for one red lamp. I was half asleep when the radio clicked and Vira's whole body changed. She sat up like a pulled string. Her voice was low and calm. "Copy. Yes. How many? Understood. Ten minutes."

Motor was on his feet before she'd finished, boots on, hand on belt. "Halyna."

"Ready." She was. She had been, for an hour, bag at her side. She slung it, took her gloves, and looked at each of us once, like checking an old photograph. "Lys. Bohdan. With me."

We went out into the dark and the sharp smell of frozen earth and smoke. A vehicle idled behind a low rise, engine muttering, lights off, its driver a thin silhouette. I climbed in beside Bohdan, who was holding the stretcher frame in white hands.

I won't say where. I won't say who. On that road, in that dark, in the back of that van, I understood something I hadn't thought of before.

Somebody drives toward it.

Everyone else's instinct is away, and these people go toward. The driver, a young woman with a ponytail, Oksana, was cheerful in the way people are when they're keeping something at arm's length. She took the bumps without braking, humming, one hand on the wheel. When I tried to speak she said, "Don't talk. Listen. Count my light taps. When I say go, we go."

When we got there Halyna was out before the van stopped. I saw her run, bent over, bag swinging, into the dark. I saw the shape on the ground, someone's brother, someone's father, curled round himself. She knelt, and I heard her voice, the other voice, low and steady and unbearably gentle. "Hello, friend. I'm here. Look at me. Tell me your name."

We carried him. I didn't know how heavy a person is until you carry one who can't help. The stretcher bit my hands. My legs shook. Then he was in the van, wrapped in foil like a gift, and Halyna had his hand and was talking, talking, never stopping, as she'd said.

We tore back through the night with the lights off, no sound but the engine and her voice. "You're doing well. We're nearly there. Squeeze my hand. Good. Good."

He squeezed. Then, somewhere between one bump and the next, he stopped.

I didn't understand at first. Halyna didn't change her voice. She kept talking for another minute, the same words, *we're nearly there,* with two fingers at his neck and her eyes on the roof of the van. Then she stopped. In the sudden quiet I heard Oksana, in front, breathing very carefully.

"Keep going," said Halyna. "They still need to take him."

At the handover the doors opened and other hands took him into a lit room, and she stood in the cold with her gloves red, looking at nothing.

"Come," she said at last. "Tea."

I never learned his name. I asked her, much later. She said she hadn't had time to ask, and that she'd hold it against herself until she died.

# Day 35 — Notes: Volunteers Die Too

**Bottom line:** Volunteers die too, and nobody keeps that list.

The drivers aren't soldiers. They have no helmets worth the name. Some of them have died on those roads and nobody gave a speech.

When you read about casualties and the number is "military," remember that there's a second list nobody keeps in the same place.

*Dispatches from the Line is a work of fiction.*
