# Day 34 — Dispatch: Halyna's Hands

**Bottom line:** Halyna taught us the tourniquet, and I realized she packs her bag like someone leaving on a trip she knows she'll take.

Halyna's hands are the most important thing in the dugout and nobody ever says so.

Square, strong, a little rough from washing. A nurse's hands, short nails, a faint pale mark on the left thumb where a scalpel once slipped. Always warm. She keeps them in her armpits when she's not using them, a habit from the ward, so they're not cold when they touch a patient. "It's courtesy," she says.

Today she taught the thing everyone dreads. We sat on crates in a half-circle with the Colonel asleep in the corner and she held up a flat black strap with a plastic bar.

"This will save a life. Yours. Mine. His," nodding at Bohdan. "I want you to put it on in the dark with your eyes closed, with your teeth. Understand?"

We practiced. It's harder than it sounds. The strap's stiff. Your fingers shake. She made us do it again and again on our own arms, our own legs, each other's. "Higher. Tighter. More. It will hurt. It should hurt. If it doesn't hurt it isn't working."

When it was my turn I fumbled, and she didn't say a word. She put her warm hands over mine, steadied the strap, and guided it as you'd guide a child's hand to a light switch. "There. Pull. Turn the bar. Good. Now the time. Write the time. Always the time."

She wrote it on my arm in marker. 14:07. It's still there, faded to gray. I haven't washed it off.

At the end she looked at the six of us, frowning, a bit pale, and said something I've kept. "You won't use this. I hope you won't. But one day you'll be in a field with someone you love, and you'll be the only one with two hands. I'm giving you the hands."

Nobody laughed.

Over tea, I asked how she managed. Twenty years of wards, and now this. She stirred for a while.

"You do the next thing," she said. "Always. Stop the bleeding. Check the breath. Talk to them. Never stop talking. Half the time they need the voice more than the hands." Crooked smile. "And you cry later. In the shower. When there's a shower."

I laughed, and then didn't.

Later, going out, I saw her kneel by the door with her bag, checking it. The tourniquets, the bandages, the foil sheets. She counted them under her breath. She'd counted them three times that day. Every night, before she sleeps, she packs the bag as if for a trip she knows she's taking.

# Day 34 — Notes: Basement Ward

**Bottom line:** The medical workers in the basements deserve sleep.

The doctors worked a whole night in a ward with no windows and told me about it afterward in the same voice they'd use for a shopping list. A man with a hand. A child with a lung. A woman who kept apologizing for the blood.

Everyone I know who works in those wards is somebody's brother. I'd like them given medals. I'd like them given sleep.
