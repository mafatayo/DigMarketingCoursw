# Day 17 — Dispatch: Twenty-Three

**Bottom line:** We made Bohdan a biscuit cake for his birthday, and I understood that he is the man most likely to be targeted.

Bohdan turned twenty-three today and tried very hard to keep it secret, and failed, because Vira reads everybody's documents. She calls it administrative awareness. He calls it being stalked.

Three days ago she announced over the stove, to the entire dugout: "Bohdan's birthday is Thursday. We're doing something."

He went the color of a beet. "Please don't."

"Too late."

You can't order a cake here. You can't order a pizza. What you can do is conspire, and this group conspires beautifully. For three days there were whispers over the tea. Zenko was writing a speech. Halyna was in charge of "the cake situation." Motor was seen pretending not to be involved while sanding a small piece of wood for reasons nobody mentioned.

This morning Bohdan went out early to fly a short check, nervous as always, apologizing to the machine. He's never out there long. That's the rule: keep it short, because the longer it's up, the more likely somebody on the other side finds him. He doesn't talk about it. I've noticed that after every flight he sits very still for a minute before he comes in.

When he came down the steps, the lamp was lit, the crate had a cloth on it, a real cloth, embroidered, somebody's grandmother's, and in the middle there was a cake. Crushed biscuits, condensed milk, cocoa, pressed into a mess tin and chilled outside in the cold. One stubby white candle, the kind you keep for power cuts. Vira had cut little paper flags from the back of a ration box.

It looked terrible. It was the most beautiful thing I've seen all month.

We sang, badly. Zenko was the only one in tune and refused to lead. The Colonel barked at the high notes. Bohdan stood in the doorway with both hands over his face and then, to everyone's horror, began to cry, quietly, with his shoulders going.

"Don't," said Vira, but her voice went.

"I'm just—" he said. "My mother always makes this one."

Motor held out the thing he'd been sanding. A tiny swallow, rough, flat, worn smooth so it'd fit in a palm. "For your pocket," he said gruffly. "With the other things you carry."

Bohdan took it with both hands. He couldn't speak. He nodded.

Zenko's speech was short. "Twenty-three. At twenty-three I did nothing useful. You do something useful, daily. You are also, I'm told, polite to machines. That is the whole of a good man. Happy birthday."

The cake was gone in about four minutes. The Colonel got a corner. Bohdan kept the candle, and later I saw him put it carefully in the pocket with the swallow.

I should say what I was thinking, because it wasn't about cake. I was thinking about the sheer number of days a person has to get through to reach twenty-three, and how many of those he'd spent in a room with a screen, being quiet, and how he was now the most targeted man in the dugout, whatever the rank says. I was thinking: *keep your head down, kid.*

I didn't say it. I ate the cake.

**Tactical read.** Drone operators generate observation, which makes them priority targets for the other side. That raises their risk well above that of the rest of the section.

**OPSEC.** Equipment counts, stock levels and supply routes are withheld.

# Day 17 — Notes: Candles

**Bottom line:** Birthday cakes in every shelter are made from biscuits.

A mother in a basement shelter made her daughter a birthday cake out of biscuits and condensed milk and put a candle on it, one of the emergency ones. Eleven children and four old people sang. The little girl blew it out and everyone laughed because the lights had just gone anyway.

It's the same cake we make. It tastes the same in every shelter.
