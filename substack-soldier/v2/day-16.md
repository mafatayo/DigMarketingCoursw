# Day 16 — Dispatch: The Fox

**Bottom line:** My callsign came from a nervous radio check that made the whole net laugh, before a very different message that night.

I promised the story of my callsign and I'm a man of my word, even when I'd rather not be.

It was my third day. Vira was teaching me the radio the way she teaches everyone, with the patience of someone defusing something. "Press, speak, release. Say who you are, say who you're calling. Short. Never say anything you wouldn't say in front of your mother."

The net is a bad place to be an amateur. Half the time it's hissing. The other side leans on our frequencies most evenings, a wash of noise and sometimes a voice, a man reading numbers in a flat tone, or music, once, an accordion, which Vira says is worse. So you key the mic knowing you're a speck in a loud room.

My assigned callsign, the one on the paper, was a boring code. The first time I pressed the key my hands were wet and my voice came out like I'd been caught shoplifting. I was supposed to say my code and *radio check.* What I said, in a whispery, breathless voice, was:

"This is... uh. This is the fox. The fox, checking in. Over."

Why the fox? The evening before, at dusk, a real fox had trotted across the track in front of me, red and quick, and looked at me over its shoulder like someone who's forgotten his keys. I'd been thinking about it ever since. It just came out.

A long pause on the net. Static. Then a voice from another position, deadpan, an older man I later learned was a legend in the battalion:

"Fox, this is Control. Copy. Do you have a burrow, or are you sleeping rough?"

I didn't know what to say. Vira had both hands over her mouth and her whole body was shaking.

Then the net woke up. One voice after another. "Fox, are you hunting chickens tonight?" "Fox, request permission to hear you bark." "Control, I have a report of a fox in sector two, he's wearing a very nice scarf." Somebody sang four lines about a fox and a rooster. For about ninety seconds, a whole network of tired, frightened, bored soldiers spread across a dozen positions was laughing, in code, on an open channel. I've never been so embarrassed and so grateful in the same breath.

Vira took the mic. "Control, Magpie. That was a training exercise. The fox is stable."

"Copy, Magpie. The fox is now official."

So I was Lys. Not by order. By acclamation.

She told me later it was the funniest thing she'd heard in a year. I asked why she hadn't cut me off. "Because it was the first time anyone laughed on that net since the winter," she said.

I could end it there. But there's the other half of the night, and I told you I'd be honest. Around two, the net went very quiet, and then a voice I didn't know came on and read out, slowly, a list. A number, a time, a word I won't write. Then a second one. Nobody joked. When he finished there was only static and then, after a while, one click as someone acknowledged. That was all.

I looked at Vira. She was sitting very still with her hand flat on the set.

"That's what it's for," she said. "The other thing's just so we can stand it."

# Day 16 — Notes: The Net at Night

**Bottom line:** The net carries mothers' questions, and Vira answers every one.

The net carries more than us. Some nights a woman from a village calls the first number she can find to ask whether the position by the river is still there, because her son's in it, and she hasn't heard.

Vira answers every one. She has a rule: she never says "I can't tell you." She says, "I'll find out," and then she finds out, even when the answer isn't one she wants to give.

*Dispatches from the Line is a work of fiction.*
