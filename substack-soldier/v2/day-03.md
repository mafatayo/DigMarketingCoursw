*A work of fiction inspired by the accounts of Ukrainian soldiers.*

# Day 3 — Dispatch: Who's Down Here

Six people, one hole. I'll go through them quickly, because that's how they were introduced to me, between jobs, over somebody's shoulder.

**Motor.** The sergeant. Fifty-three. Twenty-six years fixing buses in a depot. If he looks at you for more than two seconds a bucket appears in your hand. He told me on day one, "I don't need you brave, I need you on time," and I've built my whole personality around that since.

**Vira.** Radio. Thirty-one, from Poltava, bank clerk until the invasion, which she says is why she's so good at listening to people lie. Her laugh sounds like a door slamming. The radio is the only thing in the dugout she treats with reverence, and when the other side jams the channel, which is most evenings, she stares at the set like it has personally betrayed her and then switches frequency by hand, muttering, with the patience of someone defusing a bomb.

**Bohdan.** Twenty-two. Third-year computer science, until it wasn't. He flies the small drones, our eyes, and he's lost four this week. I mean lost. Shot, jammed, dropped in a field. Each one is someone's donation, a month of somebody's wages in a plastic frame, and he writes every loss in a notebook with the date and apologizes to it. Out loud. "Sorry. Sorry. Good girl." I've watched it happen.

**Halyna.** Medic. Forty-four, a nurse from Lviv for twenty years, hands like a pianist's, voice like a prison warden's when anyone skips washing. Her kit sits by the door and she checks it every night. Not because something's missing. Because she can't not.

**Zenko.** Forty-one. History teacher. Mobilized three months ago, still calls everyone "gentlemen," including the women, who've asked him to stop. He wears two scarves. He's the only one of us who actually reads the instructions on things.

And a bunk with a razor on the shelf and nobody in it.

Here is what I didn't expect, and I'll put it plainly: nobody here is who you'd pick. If you'd asked me to design the unit, I'd have designed a different one. Younger, harder, less funny. We have a teacher in two scarves and a nurse who cries at dog videos and a kid who says sorry to quadcopters.

But watch them for a day. Motor sets a bowl next to Bohdan's elbow without comment, because Bohdan forgets to eat. Vira reads Zenko's letters aloud when his eyes are too tired. Halyna checks everybody's feet every evening like a kindergarten teacher checking mittens, and none of this has a name. It's just Tuesday.

Last night Zenko leaned across the crate and said, "They'll give you a name. Not the callsign. The other kind. The kind that means they've decided to keep you."

"What's mine?"

"You haven't got one yet." He almost smiled. "It means you're new. It also means you can still leave."

I thought about that for a while. I'm not sure whether he meant leave the section, or the thing underneath it. I didn't ask.

# Day 3 — Notes: Callsigns

You don't choose. If you choose, you get "Wolf" or "Viper," and by week three your own side is calling you Wolfie.

A man in the next dugout is called Pot, because he dropped one on his own foot the first morning. He's a very good soldier. He is still Pot. Motor is Motor, which is obvious. The tall one in the supply truck is Lamppost. Anyone from Odesa is Sea, whether or not that's their name.

Mine happened on the radio, in my first week. I'll tell you in a few weeks. I'll regret it.

One rule I've picked up: never use a real name on the net. Not when you're angry, not when someone's hurt. A real name is a door to someone's mother. You don't open it where there's smoke.
