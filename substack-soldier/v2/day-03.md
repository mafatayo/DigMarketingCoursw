# Day 3 — Dispatch: Soup

I met them over a pot of soup, which turned out to be an argument, so that's how I'll do it.

"There's too much salt," Halyna said, tasting it.

"There is exactly the correct amount of salt," said Vira, without turning from the radio. She was wearing the headset like a collar and eating with her left hand. "Poltava salt. You can't understand."

"Poltava isn't a salt region."

"You're from *Lviv.*"

That's the first two. Vira, thirty-one, was a bank clerk until the invasion, and says it's why she's so good at hearing people lie. The radio is the only object in the dugout she treats with reverence; in the evenings, when the other side leans on our channels, she shifts frequencies by hand and mutters at the set like someone defusing something. Halyna's a nurse. Forty-four. Twenty years on a ward in Lviv, hands like a pianist's, a voice like a prison warden's if anyone skips washing. Her kit sits by the door and she checks it every night, whether or not anything has moved, because she can't not.

The soup was beets and a tin of something. It was fine. I said so and was told by both of them, separately, that I hadn't been asked.

Bohdan came in then with a drone under his arm and mud to the knee. Twenty-two, third-year computer science, until it wasn't. He flies the small ones, our eyes, and he'd lost one that morning. I mean lost: jammed, dropped in a field. He writes each one in a green notebook with the date, and he sat down at the crate and wrote it, and then said, quietly, "Sorry," to nobody, to the notebook, or to the machine. It was his fourth that week. I've done the arithmetic on what a drone costs relative to what the people who donate them earn and I try not to think about it when he does the apologizing.

Zenko arrived last, wearing two scarves. Forty-one. A history teacher, mobilized three months, who still addresses the whole dugout as "gentlemen," including the women, who've asked him to stop. He read the instructions on the soup tin aloud. He is the only one of us who reads instructions.

"And the sergeant," said Halyna, tasting again, "doesn't eat soup, he eats what's left."

"Motor," said Motor, from the corner. He had a bolt in his hand. "Fifty-three. Buses."

"Twenty-six years of buses," said Vira.

"Twenty-six."

That's five and me, and a bunk with a razor on the shelf and nobody in it.

I should say that nobody here is who you'd pick. I'd have designed a different unit, harder, younger, less funny. This one has a teacher in two scarves, a nurse who cries at dog videos, and a boy who apologizes to machines. But I noticed that Motor had set a bowl of soup next to Bohdan's elbow without comment, because Bohdan forgets to eat, and that Vira had started reading Zenko's mail aloud to him because his eyes were tired, and that nobody remarked on either.

Zenko leaned across the crate and said, quietly, "They'll give you a name. Not the callsign. The other kind. The kind that means they've decided to keep you."

"What's mine?"

"You haven't got one yet." He almost smiled. "It means you're new. It also means you could still leave."

I never worked out whether he meant the section or something underneath it. Vira said the soup was getting cold and Halyna said it had been cold for ten minutes and the argument started up again from the beginning.

# Day 3 — Notes: Before

Every uniform in this war is a borrowed one. The nurse was on a ward in Lviv. The sergeant fixed buses. The boy was in third year at university. The soldier you imagine, the one who was always going to be a soldier, is rare here. We are mostly people waiting to go back to what we were.

Ask the nurse what she'd rather be doing. She says: anything quiet.

*Dispatches from the Line is a work of fiction.*
