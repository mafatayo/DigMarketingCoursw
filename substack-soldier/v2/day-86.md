# Day 86 — Dispatch: The Count

Zenko is alive. I'm putting that first because for six hours I didn't know it.

They took him out at four in the morning in the van, with Oksana driving, Halyna in the back with her hand on his chest. I stood in the doorway and watched the lights go. Then I did a thing I hadn't done in eighty-five days. I walked behind the sandbags and was sick.

At ten, a message came through the net from the stabilization point, passed hand to hand. *Zenko. Stable. Leg saved. Surgery done. Asking for his glasses.* Vira read it out in her radio voice and stopped at the last line and put her head on the set and laughed until it turned into something else.

Halyna came back at noon. She washed her hands for a long time. Then she sat down at the crate and said, to the table, "I need to say who."

It was a short list. I'll give you its length. Two from our neighbors' section, whom I never met. A boy from the supply group, nineteen, whose mother had sent a letter with a small drawing of a cat. And Pot, the man from the next dugout who'd dropped one on his own foot on his first morning. I'd known him a little. He'd been a very good soldier. He was still Pot.

She read the names out one by one and Vira, at the table, wrote them in the notebook, in the Names, and we sat there in a silence I can only compare to the last note of a song.

And then, an hour later, in the middle of all of it, a message arrived on Vira's phone. She read it twice. She stood up. She was crying and laughing at once and she said, to nobody, to all of us, "Zenko's daughter. She had the baby."

Nobody understood at first. Then Halyna said, "What?"

"Three days ago. Early. A girl. Both fine." Vira put a hand over her mouth. "Somebody sent it to the net. A cousin. They didn't know—they hadn't been told he was hurt."

I sat down. I sat down on the floor with my back against the bunk and for a long minute I couldn't do anything at all.

Here's how it feels to be given a birth and a death in the same hour by the same piece of paper. It doesn't feel like balance. It doesn't feel like the world being fair. It feels like being handed two stones of the same weight, one in each hand, and not being allowed to put either down.

That evening I read the note to the section, in full, out loud. A girl. Three kilos two hundred. Dark hair, "like her grandfather's, before he lost it." We toasted her with tea. Yaroslav drank his and said, "Zenko's going to be insufferable."

"He's going to give her a lecture," said Halyna.

"On the potato," said Motor.

It was the nearest thing to a laugh any of us had. We gave it a minute.

# Day 86 — Notes: Two Lists

Halyna keeps one list of soldiers and one of civilians. I asked why. She said, "Because if I put them together someone will think the second one is shorter."

It isn't. It never has been. I'd like that known.
