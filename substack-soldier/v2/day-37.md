*A work of fiction inspired by the accounts of Ukrainian soldiers.*

# Day 37 — Dispatch: Oleh and the Cabbage

A thing I wouldn't have believed on Day 1: one of the bravest people I know drives a truck full of cabbage.

Oleh turned up this week, a stout ruddy man with a thick neck and a belly straining his jacket, who greeted me with a handshake that could have cracked a walnut. A supply driver. He brings us water, fuel, food, batteries, boots, medicine, mail, and six crates of canned peaches I suspect were stolen from a wedding.

He explained how it works over tea, with the slow pleased voice of a man who's done it before.

"Think about a loaf of bread. Someone bakes it. Someone drives it to a depot. Someone loads a truck. Someone drives that truck down a road that isn't a road, in the dark, with the lights off. Someone carries it down a trench. Someone slices it. Someone hands it to you. That's seven people for a loaf."

"That's a lot of people," said Bohdan.

"That's a little. Wait until you count the fuel."

Then he got quiet and told us the other part. For nine days the main road to us had been "under observation," which means any vehicle on it was a target. He'd been taking the field tracks instead, which was slower and worse, and last Tuesday a driver he knew, a friend, had taken the same field track an hour before him. Oleh found the truck in the morning. He said, flatly, that he'd moved it off the road, and that the cabbage was all right.

I asked if he was afraid.

He laughed, a big round laugh. "All the time. Do you think I like it? I'd rather be in my garden with my tomatoes. But if I don't bring the bread, you don't eat. If you don't eat, you don't fight. It's very simple."

"That's brave."

He looked genuinely embarrassed, the way a large man does. "No. It's just work."

"It's both."

He thought about it. "You know what's brave? A cook who makes a hot meal for sixty with two pots. I'm only a driver."

And then the part that stayed with me. Before he left, he told Motor that the next delivery of ammunition would be smaller. "Everyone's getting smaller," he said. "Not just you." He didn't say anything else. Motor nodded.

In the dugout afterward, nobody discussed it. But that night I saw Motor sitting at the crate with the ammunition count, a page in his square handwriting, doing sums. He did them three times. He crossed out a number and wrote a smaller one and crossed that out too.

Oleh left at dawn. I stood on the steps and watched his battered truck, loaded to the roof with other people's hope, rumble out of sight, a plume of dust hanging gold in the early light.

# Day 37 — Notes: Lines

Water. Food. Gas for the stove. Fuel for the generator. Batteries. Mail. Medicine. Spare parts. Boots. Dry socks. New faces. Information. Morale. Someone to say thank you.

A war is also a very large, very fragile delivery system. Every mile of that road is somebody's courage.

Thank the person who does the logistics. Out loud. Today.
