*A work of fiction inspired by the accounts of Ukrainian soldiers.*

# Day 46 — Dispatch: The Bag

Yaroslav lost his bag.

Not in any exciting way. We were moving supplies between positions, a short muddy errand, and somewhere between the truck and the dugout his backpack slipped off the tailgate. By the time he noticed, a quarter of an hour later, the track behind us was empty. A long brown ribbon of ruts and nothing else.

He came in white-faced. "I lost it. I lost everything."

"Everything?" said Motor.

"Spare socks. My razor. My—" He swallowed. "My mother's letters."

Nobody said anything for a second. Then Halyna put down her mug, stood, and said, "All right. Let's make a list."

She gets practical when she's moved. Pencil, scrap of paper, Yaroslav on a crate. "What do you need? Not what you miss. What you need."

He listed. Socks, underwear, a hat, a spare shirt, a toothbrush, soap, a notebook. The list grew longer and sadder, and then, one by one, people in the dugout started to move.

Vira gave him a pair of thick gray socks. "They're clean. Mostly." Bohdan went to his corner and came back with a razor. "It's not very good. It's what I have." Zenko took off one of his two scarves and draped it round Yaroslav's neck with ceremony. "A gentleman never loses both." Motor gave a hat, a tin of tobacco he didn't smoke, and a short rough lecture on tying down your gear. I gave a notebook and a pencil.

In half an hour the pile at his feet covered almost everything on the list. A toothbrush from someone. A comb with three teeth missing. A shirt that had once been mine and was too long for him. He sat in the middle of it with his hands on his knees, embarrassed and astonished and something close to worship.

"I can't take all this."

"You can," said Halyna. "That's how it works."

"But I'll pay you back."

"With what?"

"With next month's—"

"Yaroslav," said Motor. "It's socks."

He wept then, a bit, wiping his face on the new scarf, and then laughed, because Vira told him the scarf smelled of history, which offended Zenko.

The letters, though, weren't replaceable. Motor took a lantern and went back out along the track alone, into the dusk. I watched him go. It was the wrong hour for it. The road was quiet but not safe, and I thought about saying so, and thought about who I'd be saying it to. Two hours later he came back, mud to his knees, silent, and set a crumpled backpack on Yaroslav's bunk. He didn't say where he'd found it. He didn't say what it had cost him, either, though I saw him that night with his sleeve rolled up, rubbing a bruise along his forearm that I don't think was there before.

Then he hummed his four bars until he fell asleep.

# Day 46 — Notes: Replaceable

Socks, gloves, hats, razors, mugs, tools. Even a rifle. Even a radio.

Not replaceable: letters, photographs, a child's drawing, a pencil from your father. Anything with a voice in it.

It's easy to be generous with what can be replaced. Motor walked back into the dark for a bag of letters, and I'm still thinking about that.
