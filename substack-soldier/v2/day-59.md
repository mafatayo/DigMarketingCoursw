# Day 59 — Dispatch: Dmytro

**Bottom line:** I told Bohdan's best friend in a hospital that Bohdan is dead.

I went to the hospital to tell Dmytro. The corridor comes first, because it's the one thing I'll never forget.

Disinfectant, boiled cabbage, wet wool, and underneath it a faint sweetness I won't name. The smell of people being put back together. A long clean corridor painted a pale uncertain green, a row of plastic chairs against the wall, and at the end an old woman mopping the floor in long slow sweeps, like a priest.

Motor had arranged a day's pass to the rear. A truck took us part of the way. I'd come with Zenko and Yaroslav and Halyna, carrying oranges, a deck of cards, and a letter from Vira.

Dmytro was Bohdan's friend. Twenty-four, same year at university, same room, same kettle, same stupid running joke about a three-legged chair. He'd been wounded some weeks earlier. He was in the fourth bed on the left by a window that looked onto bare linden trees.

I'd expected a pale silent man bound in white. What I got was Dmytro sitting up against a heap of pillows, one leg in a frame, a book open on his chest, and an expression of vast irritation.

"You're late."

"Traffic," said Zenko.

"I look brilliant. I have a nurse who brings me jelly." He looked round at the four of us. Then he looked again. I watched his eyes go over each face and come back, and I watched him count, and I watched him get to a number he didn't like.

"Where's Bohdan?"

Nobody said anything.

I'd rehearsed it on the truck. A careful sentence. I said, "Dmytro," and nothing came out.

"No," he said. "No, he's on a patrol, he's—he's busy. That's why he didn't come."

Halyna sat on the bed and took his hand and said it. She said it very simply, in the voice she uses, with his name in it and the word and the day. She didn't use *passed* or *lost.* She told him the way she'd want to be told.

For a long moment he didn't move. Then he said, in a very small voice, "He apologized to the drones. Did you know that? In the room. At the university. He used to apologize to the printer."

"I know," I said.

"He was so—" He stopped. His face did something I'll remember, not crying, a kind of slow collapse, like a tent when the pole's pulled. Halyna held him. The rest of us stood, uselessly, looking at the lindens.

We stayed three hours. We didn't play cards. At some point Dmytro asked to hear it again, the whole thing, how and when and who was there, and I told him, the parts I could. I told him the cake. I told him the swallow. I told him that Bohdan hadn't been alone.

At the door, he caught my sleeve. "You're the sound man. He talked about you."

"What did he say?"

"That you listen like it matters."

I didn't know what to say.

"It does," said Dmytro, and turned his face to the wall.

**Tactical read.** Rehabilitation capacity limits how quickly the wounded return. The bottleneck is often beds and staff, not surgery.

**OPSEC.** Medical details and casualty identities are limited to what a family would already know.

# Day 59 — Notes: The Ward

**Bottom line:** In the ward, soldiers and civilians share a corridor.

The ward has soldiers and civilians in the same corridor. A boy with a leg in a frame next to an old woman with a hip. A girl visiting her father. A man visiting his mother.

I'd like to say they've nothing in common. They have everything in common. They're tired of being brave.
