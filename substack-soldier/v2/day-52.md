*A work of fiction inspired by the accounts of Ukrainian soldiers.*

# Day 52 — Dispatch: Press

They came on Thursday, a man and a woman in clean vests that said PRESS, a camera as big as a small dog, and a young local driver who refused to leave the car.

The commander had authorized it. We'd been told the day before with the weary politeness reserved for bad weather. "They'll want to see the dugout. Be natural."

"Natural?" said Vira. "I'm a radio operator and I smell of onions."

"That's natural."

I'll be honest, I was angry before they arrived. It was four days since Bohdan. I'd watched hundreds of reports on my sofa and I knew how this went. A shot of the dugout. A close-up of the stove. A soldier with a sad face saying something dignified about courage. It seemed obscene.

These two weren't what I'd imagined. The woman was small and quick with a notebook and steady dark eyes. In careful Ukrainian she said, "Please don't change anything for us. We're guests." The man set up the camera in a corner and then mostly stood back.

They talked to everyone. They asked Vira about the radio. They asked Halyna about the tourniquet. They asked Zenko about history and listened to the whole answer, which took eleven minutes. They asked Yaroslav if he was afraid and he said, "Yes, every day, but I get up," and the camera moved gently closer.

Then, at the end, they turned to me. "What would you like people to know?"

I'd prepared an answer. Something grand. I opened my mouth, looked at the lens, and felt the whole thing evaporate.

"That we're ordinary," I said. "That's all. We had a boy here who was twenty-three and apologized to machines. He died on Tuesday. I'm not going to say how. He was a person. We're all people. We're tired. We're doing something very hard and we'd like to be home."

The woman nodded. She wrote nothing. She said, quietly, "That's what I came for."

After they left, the dugout was subdued, as if a bright light had been shone on a delicate room and then turned off. Motor swept the floor, which he never does. Halyna made tea. Nobody mentioned the camera.

I've thought about it since. We were witnessed. A small crew from another country would carry us home in a box of film and strangers would watch for ten minutes and think: those are people. Or it would just be a segment before the weather. I'll never know.

But I'd said his name on camera, and I don't think I'd have said it otherwise. I'd said it into something that would go somewhere. I stood a bit straighter for a day.

# Day 52 — Notes: Reading Reports

Look for the ordinary detail: a mug, a dog, a sock. That's where the truth sits. Be wary of the perfect quote; real people mumble. Check the date. Check the source. Notice who's missing.

Don't confuse one story for the whole. Ours is one dugout of thousands. And be kind to yourself. You don't have to watch everything.
