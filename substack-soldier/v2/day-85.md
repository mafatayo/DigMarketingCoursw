# Day 85 — Dispatch: The Night

I'm going to tell this in pieces, because that's how I have it.

It started at nine in the evening with the larks silent. Vira said, at the radio, in her flat voice, "Everyone. Now." Then the ground began to move.

I won't say how many or from where. It was long: a continuous roll rather than a spaced rhythm, a roar that took the walls and the roof and the breath out of my chest, dust falling in sheets from the beam and the lamp swinging and a pot hitting the floor and none of us able to hear it. Motor was shouting at us to get down. We were already down. We were as down as a person can be.

I remember the Colonel. He was pressed into my ribs and shaking, his claws scraping the floor, and I had my arms round him and my face in his neck and I could feel his heart going like a bird's.

I remember Halyna, across the dugout, sitting upright with her back to the wall and her bag in her lap, calm, her lips moving. Counting something.

I remember Zenko saying, quite clearly, in the middle of it, in a conversational voice, "In the year seventeen..." and not finishing.

It went on for what I later learned was forty minutes. It felt like a year. Then it stopped, and the silence was enormous. In the silence Vira said, "Contact," and I heard what she'd heard, a sound that I'll know all my life and won't write.

After that I don't have it in order.

I was in the trench. I was handing something to someone, and someone was handing something to me. Yaroslav was beside me. He was white and fierce and he was doing exactly what he'd been taught, he was listening. "Left," he kept saying. "From the left. Under the noise." He was right every time. I thought: *I did this. I taught him this.* And then I stopped thinking, because there wasn't room.

There were bird sounds overhead, the wrong birds. The little ones. Motor ordered us under cover and I put my back against the clay and looked up and saw them hanging in the flare-light like a dark swarm, patient, and I thought of Bohdan, and I think I said his name. I don't know. Nobody was listening.

At some point I heard Halyna's voice, the steady one, saying, *look at me,* and then I heard another voice, higher, and Motor saying "Zenko!" and I understood that I was running.

He was in the passage, on his side, with his glasses off. He'd been carrying a boy from the next position, a boy I'd never seen, and he'd put the boy down and covered him and then something had come through the roof of the passage. The boy was alive. Zenko, I saw in the red light, had both his scarves wrapped round his thigh. The tourniquet strap was above them, and the time was written on his forearm in marker, in someone's steady hand, and it was in his own handwriting. He'd done it himself.

"Gentleman," he said, when he saw me. His voice was quite clear. "I appear to have been a poor student of cover."

"Don't talk."

"You always say that." His eyes were unfocused. "Is the boy all right?"

"He's all right."

"Good. Then I'm all right." He started to smile and stopped. "Lys. Tell my daughter I'm sorry about the—" He lost the word. "The thing I said. About her husband. Tell her it was the history, not me."

"You'll tell her."

"Yes," said Zenko, politely, and closed his eyes.

Halyna had him. She'd had him for ten seconds already. She was saying his name and she wasn't saying anything else, and I understood that she was working.

# Day 85 — Notes: Shelter Count

In the cities that same night, in the cellars and the metro, there were people who listened to it and counted. Children asleep on coats. Old women with their hands over the ears of a dog. A man reading the same page of a book.

Nobody wrote about them. They are the war too.

*Dispatches from the Line is a work of fiction.*
