# Day 8 — Dispatch: Mud

**Bottom line:** A shell landed thirty meters away and Motor knocked me flat; nobody was hurt.

Nobody warned me about the mud. They told me about the cold and the shooting and the fear, and not one person said, Danylo, you'll think about mud more than you think about your mother.

It's orange-brown and it holds on. It took my boot on day four and wouldn't give it back, and I stood on one leg in a field while Bohdan hauled it out like a cork. Vira laughed so hard she had to sit down, in the mud, which she complained about for an hour. You learn a different walk. Short steps, weight low, never trust the front foot. Twenty-year-olds here walk like my grandfather on ice.

This morning we were digging. The trench needed to be deeper, and it always needs to be deeper, and Zenko was telling us about Napoleon's retreat, how it was mostly mud and cold and bad logistics and much less romantic than people prefer. I was standing in a hole up to my ankles listening to a lecture, thinking it was the least heroic thing I'd ever done.

I heard it leave. A thump far off, in the sternum.

"Ours," said Motor, not looking up.

Then I heard the other one. I'd been told not to listen for it and I heard it: a tearing, high up and widening. I stood there with the spade in my hands. I've gone over this. I didn't think *get down.* I didn't think anything. My only thought was the administrative one that there was something odd about the sound, and then Motor hit me.

He's fifty-three and roughly the size of a wardrobe. He took me off my feet and put me against the trench wall with all his weight on top of me, and the world went white and flat and loud and then just loud, and dirt came down on my back like someone emptying a bucket. Something small and hard hit my helmet. Then ringing, which went on.

Thirty meters, maybe. We paced it later.

When he got off me I couldn't hear him. I could see his mouth. He was going over my arms and legs like a car, quickly, without ceremony, and he said something and I said "What?" and he said it again, slower, and I read it off his face.

"You didn't get down."

"No."

"Next time you get down."

Zenko was sitting in the bottom of the trench with his glasses in his lap, looking at them. One lens had a crack straight across. Bohdan had thrown himself flat and was apologizing to the ground. Nobody was hurt. I need that stated clearly, because it'd be absurd to leave it out. Nobody was hurt.

That evening Halyna looked at my feet and handed me a dry pair of socks from the shelf she keeps for the purpose. I wanted to say thank you and couldn't build the sentence, so I took the socks.

I couldn't hear in my left ear until the next morning. Zenko wore the glasses cracked for two weeks, till a parcel came. He said it gave him a more balanced view.

**Tactical read.** Indirect fire against dug-in positions rewards depth and discipline. A trench only saves lives if people are in it when the rounds arrive.

**OPSEC.** Positions, orientation of the line and distances are altered.

# Day 8 — Notes: A Field You Can't Plow

**Bottom line:** A field you can't plow is a field the war has already won.

The mud is on farmland. In the spring the farmers can't reach their own fields, some for the shells, some for what's in the ground. A field you can't plow is a field the war has already won, one crop at a time.

A man told me he's been carrying seed in his coat for a year. He doesn't know where to put it.
