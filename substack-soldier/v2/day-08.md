*A work of fiction inspired by the accounts of Ukrainian soldiers.*

# Day 8 — Dispatch: Mud

I need to talk about mud because nobody warned me. Everybody warned me about the cold, the shooting, the fear. Nobody said, "Danylo, you will think about mud more than you think about your own mother."

It's orange-brown and it holds on. It takes your boot and doesn't give it back unless you ask nicely. I lost one on day four and stood on one leg in a field while Bohdan pulled it out like a cork. Vira laughed so hard she had to sit down. In the mud. She complained about that for an hour.

You learn a new walk. Short steps, weight low, never trust the front foot. Twenty-year-olds here walk like my grandfather on ice.

This morning we were digging. The trench needed to be deeper, and it's always something that needs to be deeper, and the ground was heavy and wet, and Zenko was reading us a bit about Napoleon's retreat, and I was thinking this was the least heroic thing I'd ever done. Standing in a hole, up to my ankles, listening to a lecture.

I heard it leave, far off. A thump I felt in my sternum.

"Ours," said Motor, not looking up.

Then I heard the other one. I'd been told not to hear it, and I heard it. A tearing noise, high and getting wider, and I stood there with the spade in my hands doing absolutely nothing. I've gone over it since. I didn't think "get down." I didn't think anything. I had the purely administrative thought that there was something odd about the sound, and then Motor hit me.

He's fifty-three and about the size of a wardrobe. He took me off my feet and put me against the trench wall with his whole weight on top of me, and the world went white and flat and loud, and then it was just loud, and dirt came down on my back like someone emptying a bucket. Something small and hard hit my helmet. A lot of earth. Then the ringing, which went on, and on.

It was maybe thirty meters. I know because we paced it afterwards.

When Motor got off me I couldn't hear him. I saw his mouth moving. He was checking my arms and legs the way you'd check a car, quickly, without ceremony, and he said something and I said "What?" and he said it again, slower, and I read it off his face.

"You didn't get down."

"No."

"Next time you get down."

Zenko was sitting in the bottom of the trench with his glasses in his lap, looking at them, and one of the lenses had a crack. Bohdan had thrown himself flat and was now apologizing to the floor. Nobody was hurt. I need to say that because it's the sort of thing that would be absurd to leave out. Nobody was hurt.

That evening Halyna took one look at my feet and said nothing and handed me a dry pair of socks from the shelf she keeps for the purpose. I thought about thanking her, and found I couldn't make the sentence.

I couldn't hear in my left ear until the next morning.

# Day 8 — Notes: Feet

Wool, not cotton. Two pairs, one drying. Dry feet at night even if you have to sit awake with your boots off. Powder. Cut your nails straight. Let someone else look at your heels because you can't see them.

I used to think an army marched on its stomach. I'd now say socks.

And when the next one comes, get down first and think after.
