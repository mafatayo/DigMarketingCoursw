# Day 41 — Dispatch: Daniel

The foreigner came with a delivery of medical supplies, and for ten minutes nobody could tell if he was a doctor, a journalist, or lost.

Tall, thin, bearded, in a jacket several sizes too clean, with a backpack that looked bought the day before and a face of sincere wide-eyed cheerfulness. He spoke quick English and a very small amount of Ukrainian, learned from an app. "Hello," he said to Motor, with a deep bow. "I am happy to see you. I am a volunteer. I have... *bandages.*"

Motor considered him gravely. "Welcome," he said in Ukrainian. "You are very tall."

The foreigner smiled enormously and nodded.

"I don't think he understood that," said Vira.

"He understood the tone."

He was Daniel, a nurse from a small city in Canada, who'd taken three months off work and paid his own way. He had a phrasebook. He consulted it with great seriousness and said things like "I am warm in the stove" and "Please, I desire the bread."

"This man is a treasure," whispered Bohdan.

Zenko, who speaks decent English, volunteered to translate and stepped into the role with the dignity of a diplomat at a peace conference. For an hour Daniel told us about his mother, his dog, his cold winters. We told him about borshch. Zenko translated, edited, and sometimes just invented, because a man of his training can't resist a good transition. When Daniel asked the word for grandmother, Vira said "babusya," and he said it in a flat Canadian accent, "bah-boo-sya," and we all fell about.

But late in the afternoon, the laughter stopped. Daniel had gone quiet. He was looking at the wall of cards, the children's drawings, Maria's blue heart. He put a finger on a sunflower.

"I came," he said slowly, in careful English, and Zenko leaned in, "because I didn't know what else to do. I watched on a screen. It wasn't enough."

Zenko translated. Nobody spoke.

"It's a small thing," said Daniel. "A few bandages."

Halyna reached over and took his hand. In Ukrainian, which he didn't understand, she said, "It's not a small thing." Somehow he understood. He squeezed back.

He stayed the night. At about three, the ground jumped, somewhere not far, and I heard the thin sharp inhale of a man waking up in a country he doesn't know. In the dark he whispered, "Is that normal?" And Motor, from his bunk, in English, in the only English I've ever heard him use: "Yes. Go to sleep."

In the morning Daniel looked ten years older. He drank his tea with both hands. When he left, he gave Halyna a card with an address and a phone number in Canada. "If you ever need anything. Anything." She put it in her pocket with the Babusya note. It's the fifth thing in that pocket. I've counted.

# Day 41 — Notes: What the World Can Do

A man from Canada came with bandages and said it was a small thing. It isn't. It is exactly the size of what's needed, repeated ten thousand times by people who decided not to look away.

If you can't come: write. Give. Share what is true, and ignore what isn't. That's the size of it.
