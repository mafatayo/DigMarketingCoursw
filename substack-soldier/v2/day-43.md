*A work of fiction inspired by the accounts of Ukrainian soldiers.*

# Day 43 — Dispatch: Currency

After the blackout, electricity became money.

Nobody wrote a rule. But in the days after, as power stayed thin, an informal economy grew up, rich and subtle and entirely unspoken, like trade routes of some ancient desert people.

It started with the power banks. Everyone has one. Mine's a gray slab the size of a paperback, a gift from my brother, good for three phone charges. It sat in my pocket humble and forgotten. Suddenly it was gold.

"Lys," Bohdan said carefully, on the second morning. "Can I ask you something... theoretical?"

"No."

"If a man had a quarter of a phone left, and a friend had a whole power bank—"

"Take it."

That's how it works. Nobody asks for favors. They approach sideways, hat in hand. And nobody says no. The bank passes round the dugout like a warm cup. You charge for ten minutes and hand it on. Vira keeps a ledger: *Lys, 10 min. Halyna, 5. Yaroslav, 8.* She won't say why. I think she just likes order.

Everyone became inventive. Bohdan found that a battery from a dead truck, with the right wires and a great deal of swearing, would charge a radio. Motor found a hand-crank charger left by a volunteer, and the crank became a communal duty, every man turning it a few minutes, a slow whirr like a gramophone.

I used my last ten minutes on the one call I could afford. Katya picked up on the second ring and said, before hello, "We have light. It came back last night. We're fine. Sonya made a tent." I could hear a small voice behind her saying something about a flashlight.

"Did you use the corridor?"

"Every night. It's fine. I got used to it." Then, because she's Katya: "I'm not saying that to comfort you. I'm saying it because it's true."

I held the phone against my ear for the last minute, not speaking, listening to a kitchen two hundred kilometers away. I'd have given every minute I had left.

On day three a miracle. A truck pulled up driven by a woman in a pink knitted hat, loaded with twelve car batteries and a small generator. She'd driven from a town where a school had run a bake sale. "For you," she said, handing Motor a receipt in pink ink. "A hundred and four children baked for this."

Motor looked at the generator, at her, at the sky. He took his cap off. "Madam. You've made this whole position very happy."

"Including the dog?"

"Especially the dog."

We charged everything. Every phone, every radio. Zenko charged a tiny mp3 player nobody knew he owned and played Bach, very quietly, in the corner, for an hour.

# Day 43 — Notes: Exchange Rates

One phone charge: roughly one call home. A shared battery, ten minutes: a favor, always repaid. A working torch: a small fortune. A radio battery: worth more than gold. A bake-sale generator: beyond price.

Scarcity makes people generous. Nobody keeps score. Except Vira.
