*A work of fiction inspired by the accounts of Ukrainian soldiers.*

# Day 23 — Dispatch: The Generator

The generator died on Saturday night in the middle of Vira's favorite radio program, which she listens to on a tiny speaker with the volume at one. She made a noise I can only describe as a teakettle losing faith.

It's ancient, Soviet, painted a green that's been peeling since before Bohdan was born. It runs the lamp, the radio charger, and the small heater that makes the dugout bearable. Without it, we sat in the dark and the cold with one candle.

Motor looked at it for a long time. "It's dead."

"Can you fix it?" said Bohdan.

"I'm a bus mechanic. Everything's a bus, if you're brave enough."

That's about as encouraging as he gets.

He had it open on a sheet on the floor in five minutes, and the rest of us gathered round like surgeons. Halyna held the flashlight. Zenko, who knows nothing about engines, offered historical context. "The internal combustion engine was developed in the nineteenth century—"

"Zenko."

"Yes?"

"Hold the bolt."

It took two hours. Motor talked to the machine in a low voice, the way you talk to a frightened horse. He cleaned a filter with a toothbrush, so now I knew where the toothbrush had gone. He found a cracked wire and wrapped it with tape. He scraped a spark plug. He asked for my pocket knife and then for my watch strap, and cut the strap into a little washer, and I should have been furious, and instead I watched him thread it into place and felt weirdly proud.

I was useless, mostly. But near the end he held out a hand and said "Listen." I leaned in. He pulled the cord. Nothing. Pulled again. A cough, a sputter. Then a rough steady chugging, and the lamp flickered on, yellow and ridiculous and perfect.

The dugout cheered. Vira put her face in her hands. Halyna kissed Motor on the cheek, which he took like a man receiving a medal he hadn't asked for.

"It'll hold a week," he said. "Maybe two. Don't touch it."

"We won't."

"Bohdan will."

"I will not," said Bohdan, already touching it.

It wasn't until later that I put it together. The generator is the only thing that runs the charger for the radio. If Vira's set dies, we have no net, and no net means no artillery correction, no evacuation calls, no warning. It isn't a lamp. It's a limb. And Motor, at two in the morning, had put his whole self into a single washer cut from my watch strap, and nobody had said a word about what it was actually for.

I lay awake and listened to it run. Chug, chug, chug. A very small, very stubborn engine, keeping a hole in the ground alive.

# Day 23 — Notes: Trades

Motor: buses, so everything. Halyna: nursing, and sewing. Zenko: patience and explaining things to frightened people. Bohdan: small machines. Vira: numbers, lists, sharp tongues. Me: I can tell what an engine is doing from far away, and where a noise is coming from. I'm learning that's a kind of weapon.

There's no useless trade. Everyone's old job found a corner of this where it was needed. That's the only kind thing I can say about it.
