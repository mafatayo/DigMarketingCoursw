*A work of fiction inspired by the accounts of Ukrainian soldiers.*

# Day 2 — Dispatch: Boots On

"Boots on," said the sergeant. "Laces loose."

That was the whole briefing. I'd had six weeks of training and an hour in a truck and a man I'd never met leading me the last stretch with a red light the size of a thumbnail, whispering "step here, no, *here*," and then I was at the bottom of some earth steps with a blanket hung over a door. He lifted the blanket and warmth and yellow light fell out. A lamp. A room. For about three seconds I was ridiculously happy.

"Drop the blanket, you idiot," somebody said.

The smell I hadn't been warned about. Wet earth, woodsmoke from a stove the size of a hatbox, feet, old tea, and under all of that something sweet and sour it took me a week to place, and when I did I wished I hadn't. It's what air smells like when too many people have breathed it.

Six of us in a space my grandmother would have used for preserves. Bunks stacked like shelves. A crate for a table, a board with nine mugs, none matching. One had a cartoon cat on it, and that was the first thing I noticed; I couldn't tell you what was on the map pinned to the wall.

The sergeant was fifty-odd, thick through the shoulders, with a gray mustache that seemed to hold opinions. He was cleaning something tiny with a toothbrush. "Top bunk's yours," he said, without looking up. "If you have to run you won't have time to find your boots."

The bunk below mine had a folded blanket and a razor on the shelf, a decent one, set at an angle. I asked in the morning whose they were and the nurse said "Pasha's" in the voice you use to shut a door. Evacuated, she said, three weeks ago. Not killed. She was careful to add that. I noticed her being careful, and that nobody had moved the razor, and that "evacuated" is a word you can use about a man when you don't know yet.

I lay on the top bunk in my boots. Around an hour in there was a sound far off like someone dropping a wardrobe, then a long rushing noise overhead, going away. A second. A third from behind us, closer, with a hard flat crack at the end.

"Ours," said the sergeant in the dark. "Going out."

A while later a different sound, a dry tearing high up, and the ground gave a small jump under my back and a little dust sifted down off the beam onto my face.

"Theirs."

"How do you tell?" I whispered.

"Ours leave. You hear them leave. Theirs arrive." A pause. "If you hear it arrive it's already someone else's. If you don't hear it, you won't. So stop listening so hard and sleep."

I didn't, much. At four the kettle started and a mug was pushed into my hand with no explanation. I looked at it. Cartoon cat. Across the crate sat a woman of about thirty with a radio headset round her neck and one eyebrow already up.

"Don't get used to it," said Vira.

There was a chip in the handle. I put my thumb in it.

# Day 2 — Notes: Counting

Leave-to-land, if you catch both. Everyone has a formula and none of them agree. Outgoing is a thump you feel in the sternum and then a long rushing sound moving away. Incoming close you don't get a sound worth the name. Incoming far is a tearing, then a gap, then the arrival.

Motor's summary: if it's loud, it's probably not for you.

I wrote that in the notebook, and under it, *probably.*
