*A work of fiction inspired by the accounts of Ukrainian soldiers.*

# Day 2 — Dispatch: Which Way It's Going

Training was six weeks. Nobody in those six weeks mentioned the smell, so let me do that first.

Wet earth. Wood smoke, from a stove the size of a hatbox. Feet. Old tea. Underneath it all, something sweet and sour that took me a week to place, and when I did I wished I hadn't: it's the smell of a place where people live without enough air.

We came in after dark. Everyone comes in after dark. A man I'd never met walked me the last stretch with a red light the size of a thumbnail, whispering "step here, no, here, no, *here*," and at the bottom of the steps there was a blanket hung over the door, and when he lifted it, warmth and yellow light fell out. For about three seconds I was ridiculously happy. A room. A lamp.

"Drop the blanket, you idiot," said someone, and the three seconds were over.

Six of us in a space my grandmother would've used for preserves. Bunks stacked like shelves. A crate for a table. Nine mugs on a board, none matching. One of them had a cartoon cat on it. That was the first thing I noticed. Not the map on the wall. The cat.

The sergeant was in his fifties, thick through the shoulders, gray mustache with opinions. Motor. He was cleaning something tiny with a toothbrush and he didn't look up. "Top bunk's yours. Boots on. Laces loose."

"On?"

"If you have to run, you won't have time to find them."

That was the whole briefing.

The bunk below mine had a folded blanket and a razor on the shelf, a decent one. Nobody touched either. I asked, the next morning, whose they were, and Halyna said "Pasha's" in the voice you use to close a door, and that was all I got. Evacuated three weeks ago, she said. Not killed. I noticed she was careful to say it, and I noticed that nobody had moved his razor, and I noticed that "evacuated" is also a word you can say about a man when you don't yet know.

I lay on the top bunk in my boots and listened. About an hour in, there was a sound far off like someone dropping a wardrobe, then a long rushing noise overhead, going away from us. A second one. Then a third, from behind us, closer, with a flat hard crack at the end of it.

"Ours," said Motor, in the dark, to nobody. "Going out."

Then, a while later, a different sound. A dry tearing, high up, and then the ground jumped under my back and a little dust sifted down from the beam onto my face.

"Theirs."

"How do you tell?" I whispered.

"Ours leave. You hear them leave. Theirs arrive. If you hear it arrive, it's already somebody else's." A pause. "If you don't hear it, you won't hear it. So stop listening so hard and sleep."

I don't think I slept. But around four the kettle started, and a mug was pushed into my hand without a word, and it was the one with the cat. I looked across the table. A woman, thirty or so, with a radio headset round her neck and one eyebrow raised.

"Don't get used to it," said Vira.

I held it in both hands like it was something holy.

# Day 2 — Notes: Counting

You learn to count the way you learn a language, badly and then suddenly.

Leave-to-land, if you hear both: that's distance, roughly, and everyone here has their own formula and nobody agrees. Outgoing: a thump you feel in the sternum, then a long rushing sound moving away. Incoming close: no warning worth the name. Incoming far: a tearing sound, then the delay.

Motor says all of it can be summed up as: *if it's loud, it's probably not for you.* I wrote that in the notebook. Under it I wrote: *probably.*
