*A work of fiction inspired by the accounts of Ukrainian soldiers.*

# Day 1 — Dispatch: Ten to Five

The alert app goes off most nights, so when my phone started buzzing against the nightstand at ten to five I did what everyone in Kharkiv does. I lay still and counted.

Not the buzzing. The gap after it. If the thuds start inside forty seconds, it's close, and you grab the child and go to the corridor, two walls between you and the street. If nothing comes, you're being warned about somewhere else, and you're allowed to hate your phone and try to sleep.

I got to sixty. Nothing. But the phone kept going, and when I finally looked it wasn't the app. It was a number I didn't know. At that hour it's either very good or very bad, and I'd been waiting for it for three months, ever since the letter, so I already knew which.

The voice was young and polite and a little bored. He gave me an address, a time, a list. Warm clothes, documents, a cup, a spoon. A spoon. I remember thinking, well, at least they're going to feed us. That's where my head was.

Katya was standing in the kitchen doorway by the time I hung up. She hadn't been woken by the phone. She doesn't sleep properly anymore, nobody does, you just lie there in shifts. She looked at me and I looked at her and neither of us said the word.

I put the kettle on. I can't tell you why. My hands wanted a job. She got a second cup down without being asked and we drank standing up, like people waiting for a bus, listening to the fridge.

Sonya was asleep in the corridor. That's where she sleeps now, on the camping mattress we drag out around midnight, because the corridor has no windows. She's six and she has opinions about which wall is the good wall. She sleeps with one foot out of the blanket, always has, and I stood over her for longer than I should have, and I did not wake her. I told myself later that it was kindness. It might have been that I didn't trust my face.

Katya fixed my collar at the door. It didn't need fixing. "Call when you can," she said. "Eat something." Twelve years, and the big sentence turned out to be about eating, and it was exactly right.

It was just getting light. On our street, one of the buildings has had plywood where the windows should be since the spring, and someone has painted a sunflower on the plywood, a big lopsided one. I'd walked past it a few hundred times. That morning I stopped and looked at it properly, like you do with a thing you realize you might not see again, and felt stupid, and kept walking.

The bakery on the corner was open. It's always open. There was a queue of four, in the dark, in coats, and the owner had written *Fresh. Probably.* on the chalkboard by the door, which she's done since the day the glass went. I didn't buy anything. I stood at the back of the queue for a minute only to breathe it in, and then I went to the bus.

I'd like to say I was thinking about the country, or the front, or what was coming. I wasn't. I was wondering whether I'd packed the right charger.

# Day 1 — Notes: The Bag

Packed, and glad: four pairs of wool socks. Pencils, because pens freeze. A printed photo of Katya and Sonya, because phones die. A red headlamp. Foot powder.

Packed, and sorry: a hardcover book (heavy, read in one night), three cotton T-shirts, and my good razor. There is no mirror. There's barely a sink.

The sergeant looked through my bag on the first night and held up the razor. "You're an optimist," he said. He wasn't being kind.

Nobody told me the weight of things. Everything I carry I'll carry on my back across ground that is worse than a road, sometimes at night. Every gram is an argument. I lost three of them already.
