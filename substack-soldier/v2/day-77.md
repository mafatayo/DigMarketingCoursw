# Day 77 — Dispatch: Larks

I've been watching a pair of larks, and I'm in love with them.

They arrived in the second week of the thaw, a small brown couple, unremarkable at a distance, and set up house in the tussocky grass just beyond the wire. Every morning at an hour I'd thought was mine alone the male rises straight up into the sky. A little column of song. He climbs and climbs, fluttering, pouring out a glittering unbroken stream of notes until he's a speck, until he's gone, and the music hangs there coming from nowhere.

I stand on the sandbags with my tea and listen with everything. In my old life I'd have wanted to record it. I'd have set a shotgun mic in the grass and taped it for hours. Now I just listen with my ears and my skin and the whole of my slow tired body.

I've learned the names: the larks, the wagtails, the little gray warblers in the bushes. A pair of storks has settled on a broken chimney in the village, an enormous untidy nest balanced on crumbling brick. Zenko says they've been coming back to that house for thirty years. "Constancy. The bombs come and go and the storks return."

"Storks are very reliable," says Motor.

"They are the most reliable creatures in Europe."

There's something about birds I'd never thought of. They don't care, and I mean that with love; they don't read the news. A lark sings because it's spring and its mate is near and the sun is up. Nothing in its small brain is about us. It's the most restful thing in the world.

Last Tuesday I found a nest in a crushed helmet. A rusted helmet, discarded, half sunk in the grass. A small round cup of dry stems and feathers tucked inside, four tiny speckled eggs. I knelt in the wet grass looking at them.

I told no one at first. Then I told Halyna. We walk wide round it. We carry it like a secret.

This morning one egg had hatched. A wet blind shivering scrap with a huge beak and a mouth like a small red door. I cried.

I wanted to say something else here, about helmets, about whose it was. I don't know. Somebody's. It's the kind of thought that won't leave. But there's a bird in it now. That's what I decided. A thing that was somebody's is now somebody else's home, and I think that's the closest I'll ever come to understanding what this ground wants.

# Day 77 — Notes: The Fields With Signs

There are fields in this country with red signs at the edge. Don't walk here. Children learn to read them before they learn to read.

A lark nests on the other side of the sign. It doesn't know. I find that I'm grateful for it.
