# Day 30 — Dispatch: One Month

**Bottom line:** One month in, I'm frightened, clear-headed, and not the man who stood at that bus stop.

Nobody made a fuss, which I appreciated. Motor didn't announce it. Vira didn't bake anything. But at breakfast Halyna added an extra spoon of sugar to my tea, slid it across the table without a word, and I understood the whole thing.

I went up on the sandbags this morning with the notebook. The cold was clean and bright, every blade of grass wearing its own frost coat, the field dusted like salt. The sun came up pale yellow behind a line of bare trees. The Colonel stood next to me breathing steam.

I tried to think what to say about thirty days. What came was a list.

I know the names of birds I used to call "birds."
I can sleep sitting up.
I can eat anything hot and lie about the flavor.
I can tell Motor's mood by how he hangs a towel.
I've said goodbye to people I barely knew: the supply drivers, a man from another unit with a very good laugh, a woman who brought cabbage and wouldn't take thanks.
I've learned the weight of a stretcher.
I've learned the sound of a shell I didn't hear, and it was thirty meters away, and I'm fine.
I've learned that there's an empty bunk below mine and a boy sleeping in it.

I miss Katya so much it sits in my teeth. I miss Sonya's small voice on the phone, *Papa, Papa, guess what,* followed by an enormous silence in which she's clearly forgotten.

There's something else, harder to name. A kind of clarity. For thirty days I haven't worried about my email, or my rent, or whether I said the clever thing. The problems have shrunk to their true size. Is there water? Is there a dry sock? Is my friend all right? It's terrible, and it's clean.

I should say, because I haven't, that I'm frightened. Most of the time it's a lower, steadier thing, a hum under everything. I keep waiting for it to stop and Motor says it doesn't, you just get used to the volume.

Zenko came up and stood beside me, wrapped in both scarves. "A month," he said.

"A month."

"You've done well, gentlemen."

"Don't call me gentlemen."

"A habit." He looked at the field for a while. "Do you know what Plutarch says about courage?"

"No."

"Neither do I. I made it up. But it sounded good."

I laughed, a real one, a big one. It rolled out across the frost. We went down for tea.

# Day 30 — Notes: What We Want

**Bottom line:** We want attention, not pity.

People ask what we want. Not pity. Pity expires in a week.

We want attention, which is slower and harder to give. We want you to keep looking. We want the people who decide things in rooms to remember that the phrase "the situation in Ukraine" means a woman on a landing carrying two bottles of water.

*Dispatches from the Line is a work of fiction.*
