*A work of fiction inspired by the accounts of Ukrainian soldiers.*

# Day 12 — Dispatch: Borshch

The borshch came in a tin the size of a bucket, strapped to the back seat of a hatchback driven by a woman who looked about sixty and who refused to give her name.

Short, round, in a purple coat that did not belong anywhere near a front line, with a headscarf covered in roses. Her car was caked in mud to the windows and had a crack across the windshield you could've lost a coin in. She climbed out and started unloading like a woman arriving at a family picnic. Thermoses. Foil packets. Two loaves wrapped in a towel. A jar of something pickled. The tin.

"Where's the sergeant?"

Motor came up the steps wiping his hands. He took one look at her and his whole face changed. I've never seen it do that. He took his cap off.

"Mother."

"Don't *mother* me. You look like a scarecrow. Eat."

She'd driven six hours, we found out, because a neighbor's son was in this brigade and the neighbor had said, "They never get proper food." So she cooked. A whole day of it. Then she drove, in a car with a cracked windshield, down a road she didn't know, past things I'd rather she hadn't seen.

We lined up with our mugs. Nobody talked. There was only the clink of the ladle and the sound of people breathing in steam. The first spoonful was beets and cabbage with sour cream going soft, and dill, so much dill, and it tasted precisely like my grandmother's kitchen on a Sunday when I was nine.

Beside me Bohdan had his eyes closed. His spoon was halfway up and he'd just stopped. Vira was looking at the wall, but her shoulders were shaking. Zenko ate slowly, formally, nodding to himself, and said very quietly, "Madam, this is the finest borshch in Ukraine."

"I know," she said.

When it was gone she packed up and did a round of the dugout, giving each of us a quick hard look like she was checking the count. At me she stopped. "You. You're thin." And she pinched my cheek. At thirty-four. In front of my section.

Halyna walked her up the steps. At the top the woman said something to her, quietly, and Halyna said something back, and the woman put a hand flat on Halyna's cheek for a second. I didn't hear it. Then she got into her little car and drove away, the whole way back, six hours, in the daylight, to do whatever else she had to do. I never learned her name.

Motor stood at the top of the steps watching the dust until it was gone.

"That," he said, to nobody, "is why we're going to win."

He said it flat. Like a man reporting the weather. I've been thinking about it since. I'd like to believe him.

# Day 12 — Notes: 3 a.m.

Nobody's hungry at three in the morning. They're lonely. Food is company you can chew.

Warm beats good. A hot plain cup of anything beats a gourmet cold sandwich.

What we talk about on watch is always what we'll cook when we're home. Bohdan: his mother's dumplings. Vira: a particular cake in a particular bakery in Poltava, which I've now heard described forty times. Zenko: an enormous plain omelet and a silent morning.

Me: eggs, for Katya, standing at my own stove in my own socks. That's the whole list.
