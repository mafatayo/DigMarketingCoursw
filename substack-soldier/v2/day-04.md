*A work of fiction inspired by the accounts of Ukrainian soldiers.*

# Day 4 — Dispatch: The Kettle

The first thing a unit decides about you is how you take your tea. I thought it was about my rifle handling, my silence, whether I'd flinch. It was about whether I'd put the bag back in the jar or leave it in the mug. Zenko watched me do the wrong thing and made a small mark in his notebook. I've never asked what it said.

Here is how tea works in a place with no kitchen. There's a kettle, dented, older than Bohdan. There's a gas burner, and the gas is rationed like a small religion. A tin of black tea. A jar of sugar with a lid that doesn't fit. And one rule: whoever empties the kettle fills it.

That's it. It sounds like nothing. I watched a man who'd been awake close to two days walk to the water canister before he lay down, because he had poured the last cup. The kettle stood full on the burner for whoever came next. Nobody thanked him.

You can't just light the burner whenever you like, though. That's the part nobody tells you. Smoke is a signal, and the other side has drones that sit up there for hours looking for exactly that, a thin gray thread out of a hole in the ground. So the stove is lit on a schedule, in the gaps between flights, and Vira keeps the schedule on a scrap of cardboard by the door, in pencil, crossed out and rewritten daily. Tea at 6:10. Tea at 11:40. Tea, if possible, at 4. There's a column headed "if they're bored."

Yesterday we missed the 11:40 because there was something overhead, a buzzing, thin and patient, and everybody just stopped talking and sat very still around a cold kettle, like a Victorian séance. Nobody said "drone." You didn't need to. We sat for forty minutes and Bohdan, white-faced, said, "That's not ours," and nobody answered.

When it passed, Halyna lit the burner in about four seconds flat. Nobody commented on that either. But the first mug that got filled went to Bohdan.

It's the one meal where nobody's in charge. Motor gives orders about everything and doesn't about tea. Shoulders drop when the kettle starts. Vira stops being sharp. Zenko tells a story about the Cossacks that's about forty percent true and a hundred percent worth hearing.

The day before, I'd been outside digging for six hours in a wet cold that gets in behind your ears, and when I came down the steps nobody said hello. Halyna just put a mug in my hands and closed my fingers around it, one by one, like I was a child. I nearly cried. I want to be accurate about this: I didn't, but I considered it for a good minute, which in this company is practically the same.

# Day 4 — Notes: Rankings

Buckwheat with tinned meat: eight out of ten. Boils in twelve minutes, never lets you down, nobody has ever complained about it, which is how you know.

Instant noodles: six. Dry, as a snack, Bohdan eats them. I find that upsetting.

Borshch, when a volunteer brings it in a thermos: not on the scale.

Cold porridge from a bag: two. We don't discuss it.

Anything a grandmother sent: infinity.

The sergeant says an army marches on its stomach. I've now watched this one function mostly on tea and spite, but I didn't say that to him.
