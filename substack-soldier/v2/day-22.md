*A work of fiction inspired by the accounts of Ukrainian soldiers.*

# Day 22 — Dispatch: Tanks

At the edge of the village there's a boy of about nine who sits on a wall every afternoon with a sketchbook and watches us pass.

He doesn't wave. He's too cool for that. But he looks, with the full serious attention of a child who's decided something matters. Bohdan noticed him first. "He's drawing us," he whispered, as if we might scare him off.

Today the truck broke down at the edge of the village, so we stood about while Motor lay under it with a wrench and a stream of quiet profanity. I wandered over to the wall. "What are you drawing?"

He turned the page toward me without a word.

A tank. A big, careful, detailed tank with a long barrel and a hundred rivets and a flag on the turret slightly too large. Beneath it, in neat capitals: OURS. On the next page, a second one, rougher, scribbled over in black: THEIRS.

"Are you a soldier?"

"Yes."

"Do you have a tank?"

"No. I do sound. And drones. Small ones."

He looked at me with enormous scorn. "Drones don't have a gun."

"No."

"Tanks are better."

"For drawing, absolutely."

That seemed to settle something. He held out his pencil. "Draw me something."

I'm no artist. I drew a fox on a clean page, scruffy, with a big tail and a tiny scarf. "Why a fox?"

"It's my name. At work."

"Foxes are sneaky."

"Yes."

"That's good," he decided. "You can sit here."

So I sat on the wall for twenty minutes and watched a nine-year-old draw tank after tank, each a bit more elaborate. He knew more than I'd like him to. He could name the types by silhouette. He knew which direction a sound came from. He told me, helpfully, that when the sirens go the safest place is the bathroom because it has no windows. He said it the way a child tells you about the zoo.

"My dad's in the army," he said, not looking up.

"Which part?"

"I don't know. He calls on Sundays." He gave the turret a final line. "Sometimes he doesn't."

I didn't say anything. I took the pencil and did another fox, smaller, to go in the corner.

When Motor shouted, he handed me back my pencil. "Come again. I'll do your portrait."

"With a mustache?"

"With a fox."

Walking back, Bohdan, loading tools, asked what the boy had been drawing. "Tanks," I said. "A lot of them."

"Of course he was." He took a long breath. "Do you think he'll ever draw something else?"

I said I thought so. I'm trying to believe it.

# Day 22 — Notes: Children

I don't have the right to speak for the children who live it. Only what I see from the edge: they know more than we think. The sounds, the words, the codes. They draw what's around them. They play at sirens and shelters.

They're also still children. They fight about a ball. They want sweets. They're kinder than anyone I know.
