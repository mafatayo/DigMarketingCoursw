*A work of fiction inspired by the accounts of Ukrainian soldiers.*

# Day 65 — Dispatch: What Home Looks Like Now

The leave order came as a surprise, on a Monday, in the middle of a lecture from Zenko on the Treaty of Hadiach.

A young officer ducked in, shook snow off his cap, and read from a sheet in the careful flat tone of a man who knows he's about to make someone's year. "Corporal Lys. Seven days. Departure tomorrow, six a.m., from the junction. Truck, then a train."

Nobody moved. I didn't, either. I was fairly sure I'd misheard.

"Lys," said Motor, quietly. "That's you."

"I know it's me."

"Then say something."

I opened my mouth. What came out was, "Who'll feed the Colonel?"

The whole dugout broke into laughter. Halyna put down her mug to wipe her eyes. Vira said, "He's been given leave to go home and he's worried about a dog." "That dog," said Zenko solemnly, "is a better soldier than any of us."

I packed that night in a daze, which didn't take long. A clean shirt. The notebook. Three of the children's cards for Sonya. A small, rough carving Motor had made me, a fox, no bigger than a thumb, handed over without ceremony. "For the girl," he said. "Tell her it's from the sergeant."

I couldn't sleep. I lay there in the dark with my heart going like a drum, imagining it: the door, the stairs, the third step that creaks. What would the flat smell like? Would Katya's hair be different? Would Sonya be shy? Would I be the same man who left?

That last question stayed with me through the night. I wasn't sure. I realized I hadn't looked in a proper mirror in nearly three months. I'd seen myself only in the dark window of a truck, and in the cloudy shaving glass Zenko props on a nail.

At the junction, the truck was late. I stood in the snow with my bag, among a handful of other soldiers also going on leave, all of us silent, all of us looking half terrified. A very young man next to me was bouncing on the balls of his feet. "My mother doesn't know," he said, not looking at me. "I'm going to knock on the door and say, 'Surprise.'"

"She'll faint."

"I hope so," he said. "I really do."

When the truck came, I climbed up without looking back. Then, at the last moment, I did. In the gray dawn, small and square against the white, a figure stood on the far rise. A tall silhouette with a very big mustache, a small dog at his feet.

He didn't wave. He just stood. I watched until the truck turned the corner.

# Day 65 — Notes: Returning Changes You

I'm trying to prepare, and I don't think it's possible. But here's my list.

**Things I'm afraid of:**
- That they won't recognize me.
- That I won't recognize them.
- That I'll flinch at a car door.
- That I'll be too quiet.
- That I'll say the wrong thing.
- That I won't want to go back, or that I will.

**Things I'm hoping for:**
- A hot bath, a long one.
- The creak of the third stair.
- Katya's hand in mine.
- Sonya on my shoulders.
- My mother, in the hospital, awake and difficult.
- To sleep in a bed without wondering.

**What the others told me:** "Don't plan. Just be there."

I'll write when I can.
