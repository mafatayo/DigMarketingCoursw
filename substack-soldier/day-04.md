# Day 4 — Dispatch: Tea Is a Strategy

The first thing a unit decides about you is how you take your tea.

I'm not joking. In my first week, I was watched. I thought it was about my rifle handling, my posture, my silence. It was about whether I'd put three sugars in a mug and whether I'd leave the bag in. Zenko watched me do both and made a small mark in his notebook, which he keeps in a plastic bag so it won't get wet. I still don't know what the mark said.

Here's how tea works in a place without a kitchen. There's a kettle, dented, older than Bohdan. There's a gas burner, and the gas is rationed like a small religion. There's a shelf with a tin of loose black tea, a few boxes of bags from someone's grandmother in Kyiv, and sugar in a jar with a lid that doesn't fit. And there's a rule, unspoken and absolute: whoever empties the kettle fills it.

That's all it is. Whoever empties it fills it.

You'd think a rule that small couldn't hold anything up. But I've watched a very tired man, a man who had been awake nearly two days, walk the last steps to the water canister before he lay down, because he had poured the last cup. The kettle stood full on the burner for whoever came next. Nobody thanked him. Nobody had to.

That's the thing about tea here. It's the only meal where nobody's in charge. Motor gives orders about everything, but not tea. Tea is a commons. Every few hours someone puts the kettle on and the whole dugout changes. Shoulders drop. Vira stops being sharp. Bohdan finally takes his earphones out. Zenko tells a story about the Cossacks that's about forty percent true and a hundred percent worth hearing.

Yesterday it was bitterly cold, the wet kind that gets in behind your ears. I'd been outside for six hours helping dig and my hands had stopped being mine. I came down the steps and nobody said hello. Halyna just handed me a mug and wrapped my fingers around it, one by one, with her own hands, like I was a child. The heat went in so slowly I thought I might cry. I didn't. But I thought about it for a good minute, which in this company is basically the same.

You can build an army with rifles and tanks and drones. You'll still need tea. People who say otherwise haven't been cold.

# Day 4 — Notes: A Field Kitchen, Ranked

The kitchen is a gas burner and an opinion. Still, here are our meals, scored out of ten, by a man who now has views.

**Buckwheat with tinned meat — 8/10.** The backbone. Boils in twelve minutes, sticks to your ribs, survives being reheated three times. Nobody ever complained about it, which is how you know.

**Instant noodles — 6/10.** Fast. Good at 3 a.m. Makes your fingers smell like chemicals for a day. Bohdan eats them dry as a snack, which I find upsetting.

**Borshch, when it arrives — 11/10.** Volunteers bring it in thermoses sometimes. The first spoon is a spiritual event. I've watched Motor close his eyes.

**Canned sprats — 5/10.** Cheap, salty. A good smell for exactly nobody.

**Bread — 10/10, but rare.** Fresh bread makes everyone stop talking for a second. I don't know why. It must go back to something old in us.

**Cold porridge from a bag — 2/10.** I won't discuss it.

**Anything somebody's grandmother sent — infinity/10.** It doesn't matter what it is.

**Rules:**
1. Nobody cooks when the sky is loud.
2. Wash the pot. Halyna will know.
3. If someone offers you their share, say no once. Then take it.

What's the food you'd want after a long cold day? Don't say fancy. I'll know you're lying.
