# Day 27 — Dispatch: Bad News from Home

The call came at four in the afternoon, which is a strange hour for bad news. I'd expected it at night, in a dark. Instead it was a bright flat gray afternoon, with Bohdan arguing about socks in the corner and the kettle just starting to whistle.

My phone buzzed. A message from my brother, Andriy. Just four words: *Call me. It's Mama.*

I went up the steps with my hands shaking. Signal is bad in the dugout, you have to stand by the post at the far end, where one bar flickers. I stood there in the wind and dialed. It rang six times. Then my brother's voice, low and careful.

"She fell," he said. "Last night. In the kitchen. She broke her hip."

My mother is seventy-three. She lives alone, in the flat I grew up in, on the fourth floor, with no lift. She makes dumplings on Sundays. She grows basil in a yogurt pot on the windowsill. She's the kind of person who will not be helped.

"Is she okay?"

"She's in the hospital. They operated this morning. It went well. She's awake. She's... she's being herself. She told the surgeon his hands were cold."

I laughed. It came out as a sound I didn't know I could make. "That sounds like her."

"Danylo," my brother said. "She asked if you'd eaten."

I leaned my head against the post. The wind dragged across the field. A hundred meters away someone was chopping wood, steady, thock, thock.

I want to tell you what's hard about this. I'm four hundred kilometers from my mother's bed. There's no leave on offer. There's no way to hold her hand. I stood in a field, holding a phone, with my whole body pulling toward a place I couldn't go. It's a kind of helplessness I hadn't known existed. Not the sharp kind. The slow flat kind that settles in the ribs.

"I can't come," I said.

"I know. Nobody's asking."

"Tell her I'm eating. Tell her I'm eating a lot."

"I'll tell her."

"Tell her the dog likes me."

"What dog?"

"Just tell her."

When I came back down, the dugout went quiet the way it does. Nobody asked. Halyna took one look at me, put a mug of tea in my hands, and sat next to me, shoulder to shoulder. Not a word. Motor, at the table, started humming his four bars, a bit louder than usual. Vira turned the radio down. Bohdan quietly moved his boots.

That was all. It was enough. It was somehow everything.

# Day 27 — Notes: Grief at a Distance

There's a particular kind of loss that happens when you can't be there. I'm not the first. I'm learning from the others.

**What's hard:**
- No hands to hold.
- No funerals attended. No hospital visits.
- Guilt. Always guilt. "I should be there."
- The world at home keeps going, and you hear about it in pieces.
- You have to keep working.

**What helps:**
1. **Say it out loud.** To one person. The right one.
2. **Don't stay alone.** Even if you don't talk, sit near someone.
3. **Do one small thing for them.** Write a letter. Send money. Pray, if you pray. Something.
4. **Let yourself feel it for an hour.** Then do the next job.
5. **Trust the people there.** My brother has her. I have to believe that.
6. **Keep the voice.** Record a message. They'll replay it.

**What not to do:** Don't say "it's fine" when it isn't. Don't make the others carry your face.

If you're far from someone you love, tell them today. Don't wait for a better call.
