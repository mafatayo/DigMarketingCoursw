*A work of fiction inspired by the accounts of Ukrainian soldiers.*

# Day 58 — Dispatch: The Volunteer Driver

The woman in the little hatchback came back, the one with the borshch, and this time I got her name.

It's Lidiya. She's sixty-four. A retired seamstress from a town whose name I'll keep to myself. She turned up on a bright sharp Saturday, the same battered car with the cracked windshield, the same purple coat, a new thermos of something enormous and hot. This time, I was ready. As she climbed out I stepped forward and said, formally, "Madam, my section would like to know your name."

She looked at me with suspicion. "Why?"

"So we can thank you properly."

"You can thank me by eating."

"We'll do both."

She sighed, as if conceding a point in a long trial. "Lidiya," she said. "Lidiya Petrivna. Don't write it down."

"I'm going to write it down."

"Then write it small."

So I did. I wrote it in the back of the notebook, in the margin of the Ledger of Names, under a heading of my own. A different list. *The Kind.*

I walked her to the dugout steps and Motor met her at the bottom, bareheaded. "Mother," he said.

"Don't mother me. You've gained a little weight. Good."

We ate. The borshch again, steaming. This time Yaroslav, who'd never had it, took the first spoonful and went completely still, eyes shut, and then said softly, "My grandmother's."

"Everyone says that," said Lidiya.

"It's true."

"It's the dill." She gave a small proud sniff. "Everyone's grandmother used dill."

I asked her, as casually as I could, why she did it. How many trips? How much of her own money? She waved me off. "It's nothing."

"It isn't nothing."

"My husband died in the first year. My sons are abroad, safe, thank God. I was alone in a house with a very large pot." She smiled. "I don't know what else to do with a very large pot."

"So you drive six hours."

"Seven, today. The bridge." She patted my arm. "I sit at home, I think about you. I cook. I think, this will go to someone's mouth. That's what a mother does. It's the same."

There was a thing in her voice. A flat, unashamed, practical love, with no reward. I think it's the strongest force I've met at the front. It can't be shot at. It doesn't need a medal. It only needs a pot.

When she left, she gave each of us a hard look and a hard pinch on the cheek. Even Motor. He bore it like a statue. At the car she turned, wagged a finger at the dugout, and said, in the tone of a general: "Eat. Wash. Wear a hat."

"Yes, ma'am," said six grown soldiers.

# Day 58 — Notes: The Civilian Army

I think about them often. The ones in no uniform.

**Who they are:**
- Retired seamstresses with large pots.
- Taxi drivers who became ambulance drivers.
- Bakers who bake at 3 a.m. for the hospital.
- Teachers who teach in basements.
- Programmers who build tools for free.
- Students who fold camouflage nets.
- Grandmothers who knit. So many grandmothers.

**What they do:**
1. They fill the gaps nobody planned for.
2. They keep the whole thing human.
3. They refuse to wait for permission.

**What I'd say to them:** You are as essential as any soldier. Not metaphorically. Literally.

**What I'd say to the rest of us:** Find your pot. Fill it. Drive it somewhere.
