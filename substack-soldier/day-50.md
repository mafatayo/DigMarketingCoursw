*A work of fiction inspired by the accounts of Ukrainian soldiers.*

# Day 50 — Dispatch: The Cat in the Command Post

I have to tell you about the cat, because after the last two days, I think we all need it.

There's a command post about a kilometer down the line, a larger, better-built dugout where the lieutenants and the radio relays live. We go there occasionally for supplies or briefings. I'd always found it stiff, clean, full of tired men writing things on maps.

Until I saw the cat.

He sits on the map table. Right in the middle of it. A very large, very fat, very orange cat, with a single torn ear and the expression of a man who has read your résumé and found it wanting. His name, I was told, is *Shtab,* "headquarters," because he refuses to leave. He appeared one winter morning, walked in, stepped onto the central map, and went to sleep. Nobody dared move him.

Now he's the center of the operation. Officers plan around him. Maps are folded carefully to preserve his space. When a briefing is held, the cat sits at the head, tail flicking, as the commander points at things. If he stretches out on a crucial sector, everyone discusses it with perfect seriousness.

"He's very good for morale," a young lieutenant told me, dead-faced.

"Does he have a rank?"

"Colonel-General," said the lieutenant. "But the dog at your post is a colonel, I believe, so there's a certain tension."

Vira, who was with me, put her face in her hands.

I stayed a few minutes longer than I needed, because the cat noticed me. He raised his head, fixed me with a cold amber eye, got up, stretched, and walked deliberately across a hand-drawn map of an entire region, a stately paw on each of several key positions, and sat in my lap. I hadn't even sat down. I just appeared to have a lap.

"He doesn't do that," said the lieutenant. "He doesn't do that for anyone."

I'll confess that I felt chosen. Ridiculously. Sitting in a command post, in a hole, in a cold war, with forty pounds of orange cat spreading over my knees and purring like a small tractor. I stroked him. He allowed it, with great condescension.

On the walk back, Vira said, "You were smiling like a fool."

"He chose me."

"He chooses everyone. It's a very good racket."

"I don't care," I said. "I'm going to be insufferable about it for a week."

# Day 50 — Notes: Mascots and Morale

A few facts about animals and the high command.

- **They're never officially recognized.** They're simply everywhere.
- **They're always ranked.** Colonels, majors, one very smug corporal.
- **They get fed first.** By everyone. Quietly, in violation of every regulation.
- **They have rituals.** Our Colonel greets each new arrival with a single, formal sniff.
- **They have opinions.** Shtab hates drones, loud engines, and Mondays.
- **They're medicine.** Not metaphor. Medicine. Soldiers with an animal near sleep better and cry less and talk more.

**What they don't do:** They don't ask how you're feeling. They just sit.

If you've ever been comforted by an animal, you already know this.
