*A work of fiction inspired by the accounts of Ukrainian soldiers.*

# Day 21 — Dispatch: Babusya's Pickles

A box arrived today addressed to "Any Soldier, Please." That was all. A hand-lettered label, blue ink, underlined twice.

Vira opened it with the careful reverence she brings to any cargo that might be explosive or emotional. On top lay a folded note. Underneath, wrapped in newspaper, were four jars: pickled cucumbers, pickled tomatoes, something dark red that turned out to be beets, and a little jar of plum jam with the lid sealed in wax.

The note was written on lined paper, in the shaky, round handwriting of someone who learned penmanship from a strict teacher seventy years ago. I'm going to copy it here, with permission from the section, because I think more people should read it.

*"Dear soldier. I'm Halyna Ivanivna. I am 79. I can't fight, my knees are bad. But I can pickle. These are from my garden. I put dill and garlic and one horseradish leaf, like my mother did. Eat them with something hot. Don't be hungry. I pray for you at 6 every evening. Come home. Don't forget to wear a hat. — Babusya."*

Nobody said anything for a bit. Halyna, our medic, whose name was the same, picked up the note and read it twice. Then she folded it and slipped it, without a word, into her breast pocket.

We opened the cucumbers that night. I will tell you that I have eaten pickles in restaurants in three countries and I've never had one like that. Crisp, cold, sharp with garlic, a bright clean crack when you bit in. Bohdan went quiet. "That's what my grandmother's tasted like," he said. "Exactly. How is that possible?"

"Because it's the same recipe," said Zenko, "in the same sense that every grandmother is the same grandmother. She's a Platonic form."

"Don't philosophize the pickles," said Vira.

I was on watch that evening at six. I remember it clearly, because I had the note in my pocket and I looked at my watch, and thought: right now, somewhere, a woman I'll never meet is kneeling by a window, and she's praying for a stranger with her whole heart. For me, as it turns out. For all of us.

I'm not religious, exactly. But I stood there in the cold and I didn't want to move, in case it was interrupting.

I've written a letter back. I don't have an address, only the town on the box. I put it in the post anyway, with a photo of Colonel, the dog, looking dignified. If there's justice in the world, the letter will find her.

# Day 21 — Notes: What Strangers Send

Some of the things that have arrived in the last three weeks, for any soldier, from anyone.

- Knitted socks, scarves, hats, balaclavas.
- Children's drawings, signed with tiny hearts.
- Chocolate. A lot of chocolate. Vira has opinions.
- Handwritten letters from people I will never meet.
- Hand warmers, packs of 20.
- Tea, in tins, in bags, in a thermos, in an old coffee jar.
- A small plush toy, a rabbit, worn soft. "For luck." It now lives in Bohdan's pocket.
- Pickles. Jam. Smoked fish. Honey. A brick of homemade bread, still slightly warm when it got here, somehow.
- Seeds, in an envelope. "For when you're home."

**What they mean:** I used to think a package was about the contents. It isn't. It's a message that says: *someone thought of you for an hour.* That's the cargo.

If you want to send something, write a note. Even four words. We read them all.

What small thing has someone given you that you've never forgotten?
