*A work of fiction inspired by the accounts of Ukrainian soldiers.*

# Day 11 — Dispatch: Shower Day

Eleven days. I'd stopped noticing the smell around day six, which Halyna says is the dangerous stage.

Word came at breakfast: a mobile shower unit had been set up at a rear position, a truck with a tank and a boiler and a tarp. We'd have ninety minutes in groups of three. Not everyone could go, someone had to hold the position, but the sergeant read a list, and mine was the first group.

I won't pretend I handled it with dignity. When Motor said my name, I stood up so fast I hit my head on the beam. Bohdan clapped slowly. Vira said, "He's going to smell like roses and shame."

The ride took forty minutes in the back of a battered pickup, three of us squeezed between jerrycans, going over every pothole in the country. I didn't care. I was holding a small plastic bag with a towel, a new bar of soap Katya had sent, and a clean shirt I'd been saving for a day I didn't know the name of.

The shower was the most wonderful stupid thing I've ever seen. A blue tarp on poles. Wooden pallets underfoot. A man in a hat who ran the boiler and shouted the time. Hot water came out of a hose rigged to a bucket with holes punched in it, and when it hit my shoulders, I made a sound. I'm not going to describe the sound. Let's say the man in the hat had heard it before and didn't look up.

The water ran gray at first, then brown, then slowly, slowly, clear. I stood there with my eyes shut and let it run over my head. I thought of nothing. For about four minutes, I was a person with no past or future, just warmth and a bar of soap that smelled like a lemon.

"Ten minutes," the man shouted. "Let the next one in."

I came out wrapped in a towel, steam coming off me in the cold, and put on my clean shirt. It smelled like Katya's closet. That nearly undid me. I stood by the truck with my hair dripping and a stupid look on my face, and one of the other guys, a big quiet man from another unit, held out a cigarette. I don't smoke. I took it anyway. We stood there not saying anything, two clean men in the cold.

On the ride back, nobody spoke either. Someone was asleep against my arm. The truck bounced. The light was going gold on the fields, and I watched it, and I thought: I'm alive, and I'm clean, and tonight I'll sleep.

Back at the dugout Vira sniffed the air as I came in. "Lemon," she said. "He smells like lemon. Fine, Lys. You can sit at the table."

# Day 11 — Notes: The Economics of Hot Water

I did the math afterward, because I'm that kind of person.

- **One shower:** about 40 liters of hot water.
- **Heating it:** roughly an hour of boiler time and a few liters of fuel.
- **Cost to bring a truck:** a driver, a day, a risk.
- **People it serves:** around thirty in a day, if all goes well.

So one ten-minute shower is, in a very real sense, somebody's whole morning. I tell you this because I wasted twenty minutes in the bathroom before I left home. I stood under water I never thought about.

**What I learned:**

1. Hot water is a service, not a given. Somebody drives it. Somebody lights the boiler. Somebody stands in the cold to shout "ten minutes."
2. Being clean changes your mood in a way that has nothing to do with vanity. It's dignity, in a bar of soap.
3. Wet wipes are a good idea, but they're not a shower.
4. If someone offers to wash your clothes, say yes. Don't be proud.

If you want to help a soldier, I'll tell you a secret: small things matter. Soap. Dry socks. A towel. I know it's not as dramatic as a big donation to a big thing. It's just what we think about at night.

What small luxury would you miss first, if it vanished tomorrow?
