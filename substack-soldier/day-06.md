*A work of fiction inspired by the accounts of Ukrainian soldiers.*

# Day 6 — Dispatch: Mail Day

Mail doesn't come on a schedule. It comes when a truck comes, and a truck comes when somebody decides it's safe, so the whole dugout lives in a low hum of maybe.

Today, maybe turned into yes.

I knew because Vira stopped mid-sentence. She was telling Bohdan some long thing about her cousin's wedding, and she just stopped, head cocked, the way a dog goes still. Then I heard it too: the grind of a vehicle down the track, slow, careful. Everybody pretended not to listen. Nobody pretended well.

The man who brought it was young, wearing a scarf that had clearly been knitted by a relative with strong opinions about color. He had two boxes and a sack. He put them on the crate and read names off a list in a tired voice, like a priest reading the dead, except the opposite.

"Zenko."
"Vira."
"Bohdan. Two for Bohdan."
"Halyna."
"Motor."
"Lys."

I didn't hear the rest. I'd heard my name, and for a second the dugout was made of glass.

A padded envelope, soft at the corners from travel. Katya's handwriting, slanting hard to the right like she always writes when she's in a hurry. I held it for a bit before I opened it, long enough that Zenko said, "Open it, my friend, it won't bite."

Inside: a pair of socks (wool, gray, too thick, I wore them immediately). A chocolate bar, already broken in half. A letter, two pages, in her tiny writing. And a drawing from our daughter. Sonya is six. It showed a house, a tree, a big stick-figure man with enormous hands, and above him, a very yellow sun with a face.

The man had a mustache. I don't have a mustache. Katya told me later, on the phone, that Sonya had decided all soldiers have mustaches, because every soldier on the news does. She'd put a small crooked heart on his chest, in red, and then run out of red and finished the sun in yellow.

I didn't read Katya's letter right then. I read it later, outside, behind the sandbags, with a flashlight tucked under my chin. It wasn't about anything. The neighbor's cat had had kittens. The price of eggs. She'd fixed the tap that drips. "I fixed it myself," she wrote, "so you don't have to feel guilty about it." Then, at the bottom, in smaller writing: "I'm okay. Don't be a hero. Be home."

I read that line maybe thirty times.

When I came back down Vira had a letter open on her knee and her face turned toward the wall. Nobody said anything. Bohdan was holding his envelope in both hands like it was warm. Zenko sat very straight, reading, his lips moving slightly.

Motor was humming his four bars. He was holding a photograph. He looked up and caught me watching, and for once he didn't hand me a bucket.

"Good mail day," he said.

"Yeah," I said.

# Day 6 — Notes: Letter to My Daughter, Unsent

Sonya,

You won't read this for years. Maybe never. I'm writing it in pencil in a notebook so it won't smear, and if it gets lost, that's okay too.

I want to tell you about the socks you didn't knit. They came in the mail. Mama sent them. They are gray. They are too warm. I put them on and cried a little, which a soldier isn't supposed to say, but you're my daughter and I'll tell you anything.

You drew me with a mustache. I decided to take it as a compliment.

There's a man here named Motor who hums the same four bars over and over. I want to find out the song. I'll sing it to you when I'm home, off-key.

Eat your vegetables. Be kind to the kittens. Don't let your mama carry the heavy bags. I know you're small. Do it anyway, in spirit.

If I come back late, it isn't because I forgot you.

If I don't come back at all, I need you to know one thing, and I'll write it plain: I wasn't thinking about the war when I left. I was thinking about your foot sticking out of the blanket.

Papa
