*A work of fiction inspired by the accounts of Ukrainian soldiers.*

# Day 78 — Dispatch: The Young Lieutenant

We got a new lieutenant, and he looks about fifteen.

Maksym is twenty-four, he assures us, with a boy's smooth face, big dark eyes, and a haircut so regulation it hurts. He arrived on a Tuesday with a spotless map case and a rucksack that had clearly been packed by a mother. He stood in the dugout doorway, saluted, and said in a voice that cracked on the second word, "Sergeant. I'm Lieutenant Maksym Koval. I'm... pleased to be here."

Motor looked at him for a long, long time. "Sit," he said.

I'd expected resentment. When a green young officer arrives, the old hands tend to circle like cats. But I noticed something else. A quiet, unspoken protectiveness. Halyna, instantly, put a mug in his hands. Vira told him where to put his boots. Zenko, with great gravity, began explaining the layout of the dugout as if conducting a tour of a cathedral.

Maksym drank his tea. He had very clean hands, with a tiny nick on one thumb from shaving. He looked round the dugout the way a child looks at a world he's been told about.

"I want to say something," he said at last, and everyone stopped. "I've read the manuals. I did the courses. I know how to give orders. But I've never actually been here. I'd like you to know that I know that."

A silence. Then Motor, grudgingly, said, "That's the first sensible thing an officer's said to me this year."

"I'll need you to teach me," said Maksym. "If you're willing."

"We're not teachers."

"Zenko is."

"Zenko is *definitely* a teacher," said Zenko, delighted.

I saw the shape of it, then. The old problem of authority, which I'd never examined. He had rank without experience. We had experience without rank. The two things, together, could make either a disaster or a family.

For the next few days, I watched it work. Maksym asked questions, plenty, small ones. "What does that sound mean?" "How do you light that stove?" "Why does the tea go first?" He took notes. He didn't pretend. When Motor corrected him, he said "Understood, thank you," and did it again, right.

On the fifth day he did something I'd never seen an officer do. He carried water. All the way from the canisters, two buckets, in the rain, without being asked, staggering slightly under the weight. Motor watched from the steps.

"Lieutenant," he said, when Maksym finally came in, soaked, red-faced, panting. "You've got the buckets wrong. You carry them one at a time, then your back lasts longer."

"Thank you, Sergeant."

"Next time."

Maksym grinned, enormously. I think it was the proudest moment of his life.

# Day 78 — Notes: Authority and Doubt

What I think a young officer needs.

1. **Honesty about what he doesn't know.**
2. **Teachers, unofficial ones.**
3. **A chance to carry the buckets.**
4. **The courage to be corrected.**
5. **The wisdom to ask for help.**

What the rest of us need to offer:
- **Patience.** We were new once.
- **Honesty.** Tell him when he's wrong.
- **Loyalty.** He's trying.

**What I've seen:** The best authority is the kind that grows out of work. You earn it, bucket by bucket.

Who gave you authority before you'd earned it, and what did you do with it?
