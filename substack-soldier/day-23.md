*A work of fiction inspired by the accounts of Ukrainian soldiers.*

# Day 23 — Dispatch: Fixing a Generator

Our little generator died on Saturday night, in the middle of Vira's favorite radio program, which she listens to on a tiny speaker with the volume at one. She made a noise I can only describe as the sound of a teakettle losing its faith.

The generator is old, Soviet, painted a green that has been peeling since before Bohdan was born. It runs the lamp, the radio charger, and the little heater that makes the dugout bearable. Without it, we were sitting in the dark, in the cold, with a single candle.

Motor looked at it for a long time. "It's dead," he said.

"Can you fix it?" Bohdan asked.

"I'm a bus mechanic," said Motor. "Everything is a bus, if you're brave enough."

That's about as encouraging as he gets.

He had the generator open in five minutes, on a sheet on the floor, and we all gathered round like surgeons. Halyna held the flashlight. Zenko, who knows nothing about engines, offered historical context. "The internal combustion engine was developed in the nineteenth century—"

"Zenko," said Motor.

"Yes?"

"Hold the bolt."

It took two hours. Motor muttered to the machine in a low voice, the way you'd talk to a frightened horse. He cleaned a filter with a toothbrush, and I now understand where the toothbrush from day one had been. He found a cracked wire. He wrapped it in tape. He checked a spark plug, scraped it, put it back. He asked me for my pocket knife and then, solemnly, for my watch strap, which he cut into a small washer. I'd have been furious in any other circumstance. I watched him thread it into place and felt oddly proud.

I was useless, mostly. But there was a moment, at the very end, when he held out a hand and said, "Listen." I leaned in. He pulled the cord. Nothing. Pulled again. A cough. A sputter. Then a rough steady chugging, and the lamp flickered on, yellow and ridiculous and perfect.

The whole dugout cheered. Vira put her face in her hands. Halyna kissed Motor on the cheek, which he took like a man receiving a medal he didn't want.

"It'll hold a week," he said. "Maybe two. Don't touch it."

"We won't."

"Bohdan will."

"I will not," said Bohdan, who was already touching it.

I've decided that this is the skill I'll respect forever now. Not the speeches. A man with a toothbrush and tape, on his knees, telling a machine that it can do this.

# Day 23 — Notes: Civilian Skills That Matter Here

I used to wonder if my job would be useless at the front. A sound engineer, in a war. But I've noticed that everyone's old trade has turned out to matter.

- **Motor, bus mechanic:** Keeps everything running. Gets us from A to B. Fixes the generator with a toothbrush.
- **Halyna, nurse:** Obvious. Also, the best stitcher of torn jackets.
- **Zenko, history teacher:** Patient explainer. Can calm a scared recruit in four sentences.
- **Bohdan, student:** Knows the little machines. Swaps code like breathing.
- **Vira, a bank clerk, if you can believe it:** Perfect with numbers, forms, lists, and sharp tongues.
- **Me, sound:** I can tell what an engine is doing from far away. I can tell where a noise came from. I'm learning that's a kind of weapon.

**Skills that surprised me:**
- Cooking. Everyone's grateful for the person who can make buckwheat taste like something.
- Sewing. A lot of buttons come off.
- Patience, which isn't a skill but acts like one.
- Knowing how to listen, which I'd say is the most rare.

**What I think now:** There's no useless job. Every trade I've met has found a corner of this where it was needed.

What's a skill of yours you'd never imagine being useful in a crisis?
