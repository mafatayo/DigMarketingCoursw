*A work of fiction inspired by the accounts of Ukrainian soldiers.*

# Day 16 — Dispatch: The Radio Operator's Joke

I promised you the story of my callsign, and I'm a man of my word, even when I'd rather not be.

It was my third day. Vira was teaching me the radio, the way she teaches everyone, with the patience of a woman defusing something. "Press, speak, release. Say who you are, say who you're calling. Keep it short. Never say anything you wouldn't say in front of your mother."

My assigned callsign, the one on the paper, was a boring code. I'll spare you. The first time I keyed the mic, my hands were damp and my voice came out like I'd been caught shoplifting. I was supposed to say my code and "radio check." Instead I said, in a whispery, breathless voice:

"This is... uh. This is the fox. The fox, checking in. Over."

Why the fox? I'd like to claim a reason. The truth is that the evening before, I'd been outside at dusk, and a real fox had trotted across the track in front of me, red and quick, and looked at me over its shoulder. I'd been thinking about it ever since. It slipped out. The fox. Over.

There was a long pause on the net. Static. Then a voice from another post, deadpan, an older man who I later learned was a legend in the battalion, said: "Fox, this is Control. Copy. Do you have a burrow, or are you sleeping rough?"

I didn't know what to say. Vira had both hands over her mouth. Her whole body was shaking.

Then the net woke up. One voice after another. "Fox, are you hunting chickens tonight?" "Fox, request permission to hear you bark." "Control, I have a report of a fox in sector two, it has a very nice scarf." A man somewhere sang a little song about a fox and a rooster. For ninety seconds, an entire network of tired, frightened, bored soldiers spread across a dozen positions was laughing, in code, over an open channel.

I've never been so embarrassed and so grateful in the same breath.

Vira finally took the mic from me. "Control, this is Magpie. That was a training exercise. The fox is... stable."

"Copy, Magpie. The fox is now official."

And just like that, I was Lys. Not by order. By acclamation.

Vira says it was the funniest thing she'd heard in a year, and that she's saving the recording in her head forever. I asked why she didn't cut me off. She said, "Because it was the first time anyone laughed on that net since the winter."

That's what a joke does. It isn't frivolous. It's maintenance.

# Day 16 — Notes: Humor as Equipment

I'd put humor on the kit list, between the first-aid pouch and the spare batteries.

**Why it works:**
1. It's the only thing that lowers the pulse and raises morale at once.
2. It tells the group: we're still ourselves.
3. It puts a small fence around the thing that scares you. You can't joke about something and be wholly ruled by it.

**Rules of soldier humor:**
- **Dark is fine. Cruel is not.** You can joke about your own cooking. Not about someone's lost brother.
- **Timing is a skill.** Vira has it. Bohdan has not found it yet.
- **Repeat the old ones.** A joke that's been told forty times is a ritual, not a joke.
- **Nicknames are affection.** If you have one, someone likes you.
- **When someone is silent for three days, don't make a joke. Sit beside them.**

I used to think humor was a luxury. I know now it's closer to a tourniquet.
