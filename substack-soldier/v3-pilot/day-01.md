# Ten to Five: How a Call-Up Lands on a Household That Already Sleeps in a Corridor

*Notes from a section forming up, on what mobilization looks like in a city under nightly alerts.*

**Bottom line:** The call came at 4:50 a.m., and I left for the front with half a cup of my wife's tea still on the kitchen table.

**OPSEC & Context:** *Fiction.* The narrator is a mobilized sound engineer assigned to a drone-support section. Dates, places and callsigns are altered for the story.

## The Reality on the Ground

The alert app goes off most nights, so when my phone started buzzing against the nightstand at ten to five I did what everyone in Kharkiv does. I lay still and counted the gap after the buzzing. Under forty seconds before the first thud and it's close, you grab the child and go to the corridor, two walls between you and the street. Over forty and you're being warned about somewhere else, and you're allowed to hate your phone and go back to sleep.

I got to sixty. Nothing came. The buzzing didn't stop, though, and when I finally picked it up it wasn't the app. A number I didn't know. At that hour it's either very good or very bad, and I'd been waiting for it since the letter in the spring, so I'll say I wasn't surprised.

The voice was young and polite and a little bored, like someone confirming a dental appointment. He gave me an address, a time, a list. Warm clothes, documents, a cup, a spoon. A spoon. I remember thinking, well, they're going to feed us. That's honestly where my head was.

Katya was standing in the kitchen doorway when I hung up. She hadn't been woken by it; nobody sleeps properly anymore, you lie there in shifts. We looked at each other. I put the kettle on. My hands wanted something to do and the kettle was nearest.

She took down two cups. We drank standing up, like people waiting for a bus, listening to the fridge, which has a rattle in it I've been meaning to fix for two years.

Sonya was in the corridor, on the camping mattress, because that's where she sleeps now. She's six and has views about which wall is the good wall. One foot out of the blanket, as always. I stood over her longer than I'd planned, and I didn't wake her, which I've told myself was kindness. The other possibility is that I didn't trust my face.

"Call when you can," Katya said at the door, and fixed my collar, which didn't need fixing. "Eat something."

Twelve years. You'd think there'd be a bigger sentence.

It was getting light. On our street one building has had plywood where the windows were since spring, and someone painted a sunflower on it, a huge lopsided one with too many petals. I've walked past it a few hundred times. That morning I stopped and looked at it for a good ten seconds, the way you do with something you suspect you won't see again, and then felt ridiculous and kept going.

The bakery on the corner was open. It's always open. Four people in the queue, in coats, in the dark, and the owner had chalked *Fresh. Probably.* on the board by the door, as she has since the glass went. I didn't buy anything. I just stood at the back for a minute with my bag and breathed it in.

The bus was late and then it was full. I stood. A woman with a very small dog inside her coat looked at me, at the bag, at my face, and moved her shopping so I could put my hand on the rail. Neither of us said anything.

It was only at the second stop that I remembered the tea. I'd left about half of it in the cup on the kitchen table. Katya hates waste. She'd have drunk it cold, standing at the counter, and I thought about that for the rest of the ride, more than I thought about anything else.

## Tactical Observations

- Mobilization turns civilians into soldiers in weeks, and the gap between summons and readiness is the most perishable asset an army has.
- Alert apps have become a household instrument: the interval between warning and impact now sets what families do, long before any soldier does anything.
- Call-ups arrive by phone at odd hours, which is why units insist on a short, plain list of what to bring.

## The Human Element

Nobody says the big sentence. The last words in a doorway are about food and collars, and that is how a household shows what it can't say.
