*A work of fiction inspired by the accounts of Ukrainian soldiers.*

# Day 83 — Dispatch: Dreams of a Kitchen

Tonight we did something we'd been avoiding. We described, in detail, our perfect ordinary day.

It was Vira's idea. A damp quiet evening, the stove glowing, the rain on the roof like a thousand small fingers. She was darning a sock, by the lamp, with her tongue between her teeth. "Let's do a game," she said. "Tell me a day. Not a great day. A Tuesday. The best, plainest Tuesday you can imagine, when it's over."

"That's a dangerous game," said Halyna.

"Why?"

"Because we'll want it."

"We want it already," said Vira. "Go."

Zenko went first. "I wake at eight. No alarm. The window's open. There's a smell of coffee, and the wireless is playing something dull. I read for an hour, in a chair that fits. I walk to the market. I buy an enormous melon. I come home and I eat it, entirely, alone, with a small spoon."

"That's it?" said Bohdan.

"That is *everything,*" said Zenko.

Yaroslav: "I wake up and the cows are making noise. I do the milking. It's cold, and my hands are warm from the cow. I eat bread and cream. My mother's yelling about something, in a nice way."

Vira: "I wake up late. My mother's cooking. I go to the bakery with my cousin, we eat cake on a bench, we talk nonsense for three hours, and a man with a guitar plays something stupid."

Halyna: "I sleep until the sun hits my face. I water the garden. I pick a tomato and eat it standing up, with salt. A neighbor drops by with plums. I'm on the porch until dusk with a book and nobody needs me."

"Nobody needs you?" said Vira.

"For one day," said Halyna. "Just one."

Bohdan: "I fix something. A radio, maybe. It's a nice old radio. Nobody's in a hurry. It works. Everyone's pleased."

Maksym, shyly: "I go to my mother's. I eat her soup. I don't say anything about the war. She doesn't ask."

Then everyone turned to Motor, who'd been silent, whittling. He looked up. For a moment, I thought he might refuse. Then he said, in his low gravel voice, "I get home at six. The bus is late, as usual. She's at the stop, with the thermos. She's got a scarf on. She's looking down the road, and I come round the corner, and she sees me."

He stopped. Nobody breathed.

"That's all," he said. "That's the day. Everything else, I can improvise."

I didn't say mine. Not aloud. But I thought it, in the silence that followed. A kitchen. A kettle. Two hands, one small, one large. A stair that creaks. A third, softer hand, laid over the top.

The rain went on, and the lamp, and the darning needle, and the Colonel sighed in his sleep. For a long time, nobody wanted to be the first to speak.

# Day 83 — Notes: Plain Ambitions

What we want, in a word.

- **Zenko:** a melon.
- **Yaroslav:** a cow.
- **Vira:** cake.
- **Halyna:** a tomato and a quiet porch.
- **Bohdan:** a radio.
- **Maksym:** soup.
- **Motor:** a bus stop.
- **Me:** a kitchen.

**What strikes me:** None of it's large. None of it's impossible. All of it's ordinary.

**What it teaches:** We've been fighting, in the end, for Tuesdays.

Tell me yours. I'll read it by the stove.
