*A work of fiction inspired by the accounts of Ukrainian soldiers.*

# Day 49 — Dispatch: The Chaplain

He came the morning after, as if somebody had sent for him, though no one had.

A small man, perhaps sixty, with a round gray beard and a heavy brown coat that had once been black. A cross hung at his neck on a thin cord, wrapped in a bit of cloth against the cold. He walked down the dugout steps carefully, one hand on the wall, and paused at the bottom to let his eyes adjust. "Peace to this house," he said.

Nobody quite knew how to answer. Motor, finally, said, "Father."

"Not Father," the man said, smiling. "I'm Mykhailo. Today I'm just a visitor. I heard there was a hard day here."

He sat on a crate. He declined tea, then accepted it. He didn't say a word about God for the first twenty minutes. Instead he talked about the road he'd come by, a ruined bridge, a fox he'd seen, a ridiculous mule called Vasyl who'd carried him half a mile. He had a quiet, unhurried voice, a bit like Zenko's but slower, as if each sentence were being laid down like a stone.

At last, when the room had loosened, he said, "May I ask? Is there anyone who'd like to say a name?"

A long silence. The stove ticked. Then Vira, almost inaudibly: "Yes."

She said it. A name. I won't repeat it. She said it and then she said nothing else, and the chaplain nodded slowly, and said, "Thank you." Then one by one, around the table, we said it again. Halyna. Bohdan. Zenko. Yaroslav, whose voice cracked. Motor, in a rough whisper. And I, last, with my mouth dry.

I'm not a religious man. I was baptized and then forgotten by the church, and I've mostly forgotten it back. But I'll tell you what that small ritual did. For the first time since the day before, the name had air to breathe in. It was no longer a stone in the chest. It was a word in a room, a presence, said and heard.

The chaplain prayed, briefly, in the old language, his hand lifted. I didn't follow all the words. I closed my eyes and let the sounds wash over me, low and ancient, like water over stones. Beside me Bohdan was crying quietly. Motor had his cap against his chest.

When he left, he put a hand on each of our shoulders, one by one, and said something short. Nobody has ever told me what he said to me. It was a few words. They went somewhere I haven't visited since.

"Come back," said Motor at the door.

"I will," said Mykhailo. "Mind the mule."

# Day 49 — Notes: Faith in Dugouts

An honest and partial survey.

**What I've seen:**
- Men who prayed before they'd ever believed.
- Men who stopped praying and began again.
- A girl who tucked a tiny icon in her helmet, and kept it there.
- A cheerful atheist who said grace over buckwheat "just in case."
- A Muslim volunteer who prayed beside a Christian chaplain, both quietly, side by side.
- People who'd say, "I don't know what I believe, but I believe in this tea."

**What it does:**
1. It gives grief somewhere to go.
2. It gives words to the unsayable.
3. It makes a small circle, a shared moment.
4. It reminds us we're not the first.

**What I'd say to anyone, believer or not:** Say the name. Out loud. In a room. It works.
