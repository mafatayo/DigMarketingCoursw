*A work of fiction inspired by the accounts of Ukrainian soldiers.*

# Day 35 — Dispatch: Evacuation Night

I'm going to tell this one carefully, and I'll leave out anything someone could use. What I can give you is the feeling.

It was after midnight. The cold was savage, the sky thick with stars, and the dugout was dark except for a single red lamp. I was half asleep when the radio clicked, and Vira's whole body changed. She sat up like a string pulled tight. Her voice was calm and low. "Copy. Yes. How many? Understood. Ten minutes."

Motor was on his feet already, boots on, hand on his belt. "Halyna," he said.

"I'm ready." She was. She had been, I realized, for the last hour, kit by her side. She slung her bag over her shoulder, took her gloves, and looked once at each of us in turn, like checking an old photograph. "Lys. Bohdan. With me."

We went out into the dark, into the cold, into the sharp smell of frozen earth and smoke. A vehicle idled behind a low rise, engine muttering, lights off, its driver a thin silhouette. I climbed in. Bohdan beside me, holding the stretcher frame in white hands.

I won't say where we went. I won't say who. I'll say only that on that road, in that dark, in the back of that van, I understood something I'd never thought of before.

Somebody drives toward it.

Everyone else's instinct is to go away, and these people go toward. The driver, a young woman with a ponytail and an enormous cheerful face, took the bumps without braking, humming to herself, one hand on the wheel. When I tried to speak, she said, "Don't talk, listen. Count the seconds between my light taps. Then we go."

When we reached the place, Halyna was out of the van before it stopped. I saw her run, bent over, with her bag, into the dark. I saw the shape of the man on the ground, someone's brother, someone's father, curled around himself. I saw her kneel, and I heard her voice, the new voice, the one I'd never heard her use, low and steady and unbearably gentle. "Hello, friend. I'm here. Look at me. Tell me your name."

We carried him. I can't tell you how heavy a person is until you carry one who can't help. The stretcher bit into my hands. My legs shook. The cold was a wall. Then he was in the van, wrapped in foil like a gift, and Halyna was holding his hand and talking, talking, never stopping, the way she'd said.

The driver swung us round and we tore back through the night, lights off, no sound but the engine and Halyna's voice. "You're doing well. We're nearly there. Squeeze my hand. Good."

He squeezed. That was all I needed to know.

I won't tell you the ending, because I don't know it. At the handover, the doors opened, other hands took him, and he was gone into a lighted room. Halyna stood in the cold with her bag, her gloves red, looking at nothing.

"Come," she said at last. "Tea."

# Day 35 — Notes: The People Who Drive Toward Danger

This one's short, because I think it needs to be.

They don't carry rifles, many of them. They carry:
- stretchers
- bandages
- thermoses
- a steady voice
- a vehicle, often an old one, often bought with donations, often covered in mud and prayers

**Who they are:** Nurses. Mechanics. Students. Retired teachers. Taxi drivers. Priests. Girls with ponytails.

**What they do:** They go where the road is worst, at the worst hour, and bring back whoever they can.

**What I want to say:** If there is any group of people in this war deserving of a statue, it's them. Not the commanders. Not the famous. The ones in the vans.

I don't have a question for you today. I only ask that you remember them.
