*A work of fiction inspired by the accounts of Ukrainian soldiers.*

# Day 20 — Dispatch: The Village Nearby

We got a half-day to help in the village, a few kilometers back, and I'd like to tell you what I saw there without making it into something grander than it is.

It was a village the way any village is. A single road, a church with a dented dome, a shop with its shutters half down, fields behind. About a third of the houses had people in them. The rest had windows boarded up with plywood, or missing altogether, and the gardens had grown wild. A sunflower, dead and gray, stood bent by one door like a man leaning on a wall.

Our job was simple: carry wood, fix a fence, help an old man move his things from one room to another because the ceiling in the first one had come down. I carried an armchair with Bohdan. It was heavier than it looked, stuffed with horsehair, and smelled of lavender and cats.

The old man's name was Pavlo. Eighty-one, tall, bent, with enormous hands like bundles of roots. He directed us in a voice used to commanding oxen. "Not there, boy. There. Careful, that was my mother's."

"Sorry, grandfather."

"Don't be sorry, be accurate."

I liked him immediately.

When we were done, he sat us down at a table in his kitchen. The kitchen had no window glass, just a plastic sheet that breathed in and out with the wind. He put out a bowl of walnuts and a bottle of something clear and a plate of dried apples, and he said, "Now. Tell me you've eaten."

We hadn't, not really. We ate the apples.

I asked him, awkwardly, why he stayed. Everyone else on the street had left, or half left. He looked at me for a long time. Then he said, "Where would I go? My wife is in that cemetery. My father is in that field. I know every tree on this road. If I leave, who tells the dog where I've gone?" And indeed there was a dog, old, gray-muzzled, lying under the table, who thumped his tail at the word.

He wasn't being brave. That was the point. He was simply being a man who lived there.

"You think it's courage," he said, as if reading my face. "It's laziness. Packing is hard."

Bohdan laughed so hard he had to put down a walnut.

Walking back, none of us said much. But Bohdan, ahead of me, was carrying a jar of pickled tomatoes that Pavlo had pressed on him, and he held it carefully in both arms like it was a baby.

# Day 20 — Notes: Civilians Who Stayed

I want to say something about the people who stay. It's not a thing I understood before.

**Why they stay (as far as I can tell):**
- **Age.** Moving at eighty is another kind of war.
- **Roots.** Graves, orchards, a house built by hand.
- **Animals.** Cows don't evacuate. Somebody has to milk.
- **Stubbornness.** A very Ukrainian art form.
- **No money, nowhere, no one.** The less romantic reasons are more common.
- **Defiance.** A kind of quiet one. "You'll have to carry me."

**What they need:**
- Food, gas, medicine.
- Someone to repair a roof, or a fence.
- Someone to listen.
- Not pity. They don't want pity.

**What they give us:**
- Walnuts. Apples. Pickles. Warm bread.
- Directions, warnings, gossip.
- A reminder that this is somebody's home.

If you ever want to help, don't send a big gesture. Find a small group that drives food and medicine to the villages. Those drivers are some of the bravest people I've met, and they're mostly retired teachers.

Do you know someone who stayed somewhere hard?
