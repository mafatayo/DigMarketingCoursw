*A work of fiction inspired by the accounts of Ukrainian soldiers.*

# Day 14 — Dispatch: Two Weeks In

Two weeks. I'd like to say I feel like a veteran, but I feel like a man who's learned where the bathroom is.

Let me tell you what's different. On day one, I counted every sound. By day fourteen I've stopped counting and started sorting, which is a more tiring way to listen. I know the kettle from the stove from the generator. I know which bunk creaks. I know that Bohdan sleeps with one arm hanging off the bed and that Zenko talks in his sleep, softly, in complete grammatical sentences. Last night he said, "No, no, the Treaty of Pereiaslav is not a mere formality." Vira, awake on watch, wrote it down. She plans to read it to him at breakfast.

I know the paths. I know which sandbags are the comfortable ones. I know when Motor is about to say something and when he's about to say nothing, which is both the same expression.

What I haven't gotten used to, and don't want to, is the strange gap between what's happening and what it feels like. You'd think a place like this would make everything sharp. Instead it makes everything blurry at the edges. Days run into each other like paint. Someone asks what day it is and half the table says "Tuesday?" and the other half says "No, Friday," and nobody's sure.

There's a boredom in this that I wasn't ready for. Hours of waiting, watching, being cold. And then, a few minutes of something else. I used to think of war as exciting, I think, the way you do when you've only watched it. It's long stretches of dull, interrupted by fear, and the fear doesn't announce itself with music.

Tonight I wrote a long letter to Katya, much of which I tore out and burned in the stove, because it was too heavy. What I sent was short. "I'm fine. I'm learning. The dog likes me best." That last part was true.

Halyna stopped beside my bunk before bed, as she does, and checked my feet. "Two weeks," she said. "You're doing all right."

"Am I?"

"You've stopped saying 'sorry' every five minutes. That's a good sign."

"Sorry," I said, and she smiled, and the smile is the first thing I can remember that day that I'm sure was real.

# Day 14 — Notes: What I Got Wrong

I made predictions before I came. Here's how they held up.

**I thought it would be louder.** It's mostly quiet. The loud parts are short, and the quiet parts are the ones that hurt.

**I thought I'd be braver.** I'm not. I'm exactly as brave as I was, only now I'm better at hiding it.

**I thought the hard part would be the enemy.** The hard part is the cold, the mud, the waiting, the missing. The enemy is a distant weather system. The mud is personal.

**I thought I'd have nothing in common with these people.** I have everything in common with them. We all want dry socks and a call home.

**I thought I'd stop being a husband, a father, a man who fixed taps.** I haven't. I've just become those things from further away.

**I thought it would change me instantly.** It's slower. It's like a tide. You don't notice it going out until you look down and the beach is far away.

What did you predict, once, that turned out different?
