# Day 43 — Dispatch: Charging Stations

After the blackout, we learned that electricity is a currency.

Not officially. Nobody wrote a rule. But in the days afterward, as supplies of power stayed thin, a kind of informal economy sprang up, rich and subtle and entirely unspoken, like the trade routes of some ancient desert people.

It started with the power banks. Everyone has one. Mine's a slab of gray plastic the size of a paperback, a gift from my brother, which holds enough juice for three phone charges. It had sat in my pocket, humble and forgotten. Suddenly it was gold.

"Lys," Bohdan said carefully, on the second morning. "Can I ask you something... theoretical?"

"No."

"If a man had a quarter of a phone left, and a friend had a whole power bank..."

"Take it," I said. "Take it."

That's how it works. Nobody asks for favors. They approach sideways, hat in hand. And nobody says no. The power bank passes round the dugout like a warm cup. You charge for ten minutes, you hand it on. Vira, meticulous, keeps a ledger: *Lys, 10 min. Halyna, 5 min. Yaroslav, 8 min.* She won't say why. I think she just likes order.

We all became enormously inventive. Bohdan found that a certain car battery from a broken truck could, with the right wires and a great deal of swearing, charge a radio. Motor discovered a hand-cranked charger left by a volunteer, and the crank became a communal duty. Every man turned it for a few minutes, the sound a slow gentle whirr, like an old gramophone.

And then, on day three, a miracle. A truck pulled up, driven by a woman in a pink knitted hat, loaded with twelve car batteries and a small generator. She'd driven from a town where a school had held a bake sale. "For you," she said, hopping down, handing Motor a receipt, in pink ink. "A hundred and four children baked for this."

Motor looked at the generator, then at her, then at the sky. He took his cap off. "Madam," he said. "I would like you to know that you've made this whole position very happy."

"Including the dog?" she said.

"Especially the dog."

We charged everything. Every phone. Every radio. Every torch. Zenko charged a tiny mp3 player nobody knew he owned, and played Bach, very quietly, in the corner, for an hour.

# Day 43 — Notes: The Power Bank Economy

A rough guide to the currency of electricity at the front.

- **One phone charge:** Roughly one call home. Treasured.
- **Ten minutes on a shared battery:** A favor. Always paid back.
- **A working torch:** A small fortune.
- **A radio battery:** Worth more than gold, and I'm only mildly exaggerating.
- **A hand-crank charger:** A moral duty.
- **A kid's bake-sale generator:** Beyond price.

**What I notice:** Scarcity makes everyone generous. People lend. People share. Nobody's keeping score except Vira.

**What I'd ask for, if you want to help:** Small, rugged power banks. Hand-crank torches. Spare batteries. Solar chargers.

Who in your life would you lend your last bit of battery to?
